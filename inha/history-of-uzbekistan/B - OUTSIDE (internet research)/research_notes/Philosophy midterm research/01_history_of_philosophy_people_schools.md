## 1. What is philosophy? Main branches, the "basic question", and key terms

### Takeaway
"Philosophy" comes from Greek *philosophia*, "love of wisdom". Sources list its core branches as metaphysics (with ontology), epistemology, logic and ethics; aesthetics and axiology (value theory) are usually added. Post-Soviet curricula, including Uzbek ones, typically frame the "basic (fundamental) question of philosophy" in Engels' terms: how thinking relates to being. Answering "spirit first" makes one an idealist; answering "nature/matter first" makes one a materialist.

### Cited Findings
**Definition and origin of the word**
- Britannica: "philosophy, (from Greek, by way of Latin, *philosophia*, 'love of wisdom') the rational, abstract, and methodical consideration of reality as a whole or of fundamental dimensions of human existence and experience." — [Britannica: philosophy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/philosophy)
- Philosophy is "from Ancient Greek φιλοσοφία (philosophía), lit. 'love of wisdom'". It is "a systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning topics like existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value". — [Wikipedia: Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)
- Tradition says Pythagoras was "reputedly the first man to call himself a philosopher ('lover of wisdom')". Augustine praised him for calling himself *philosophos* rather than "sage". — [Wikipedia: Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras). Caution: the SEP stresses that later antiquity glorified Pythagoras "in a largely unhistorical fashion", so treat this as tradition, not established fact. — [SEP: Pythagoras](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Wikipedia names the major branches as "epistemology, ethics, logic, and metaphysics". — [Wikipedia: Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)

**Branches: Greek roots and what each studies**

| Branch | Root / coinage | Studies (typical MCQ question) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|

| **Logic** | *logos* = "reason, discourse, language" | Correct reasoning, the "laws of thought", valid arguments | [Wikipedia: Logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic) |
| **Ethics** (moral philosophy) | Greek *ēthos*; also called moral philosophy | What we ought to do. Its branches are normative ethics, applied ethics and metaethics | [Wikipedia: Ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics) |
| **Aesthetics** | Latin *aesthetica*, coined by **Alexander Baumgarten in 1735**, from Greek *aisthēsis* "sensation" | Beauty, art, taste | [Wikipedia: Aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) |

**The "basic (fundamental) question of philosophy" (Engels, 1886). This framing is standard in post-Soviet textbooks.**
- Engels: "The great basic question of all philosophy, especially of more recent philosophy, is that concerning the relation of thinking and being." Asked "which is primary, spirit or nature", philosophers "split… into two great camps". "Those who asserted the primacy of spirit to nature… comprised the camp of idealism. The others, who regarded nature as primary, belong to the various schools of materialism." — [Engels, *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy*, Part 2 (marxists.org)](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm)
- The question's second side: "Is our thinking capable of the cognition of the real world?" Engels calls this "the question of identity of thinking and being", and says most philosophers answer yes. (Agnostics such as Hume and Kant are the usual counter-examples in textbooks.) — [Engels, Part 2](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm)
- Kant summarised his concerns in three questions: "What can I know? What should I do? What may I hope?" — [Wikipedia: Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)

**Key "-isms" (frequent MCQ distractors)**

| Term | Definition | Classic representatives | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Materialism** | A form of monism holding that **matter** is the fundamental substance. Mind and consciousness arise from material processes | Democritus, Epicurus, Charvaka, Feuerbach, Marx | [Wikipedia: Materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism) |
| **Idealism** | **Consciousness/mind/idea** is fundamental. *Subjective* idealism restricts reality to experience; *objective* idealism posits a trans-empirical reality that is still not prior to mind (e.g., Plato's Forms, Hegel's Absolute Idea) | Plato, Berkeley (subjective), Hegel (objective/absolute) | [Wikipedia: Idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism); [Wikipedia: Materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism) |
| **Monism** | Attributes "oneness": one substance or principle | Thales (water), Parmenides (Being), Spinoza (God-or-Nature) | [SEP: Monism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/monism/) |
| **Dualism** | Two fundamental substances. Classic case: Descartes' mind (*res cogitans*) vs body (*res extensa*) | Descartes; Zoroastrian cosmic dualism (good vs evil) is a religious analogue | [Wikipedia: Mind–body dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind%E2%80%93body_dualism) |
| **Pluralism** | Many different substances or principles constitute reality (vs monism "all is one" and dualism "all is two") | Empedocles (4 roots), atomists, Leibniz (monads) | [Wikipedia: Pluralism (philosophy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralism_(philosophy)) |
| **Rationalism vs empiricism** | The dispute "concern[s] the extent to which we are dependent upon experience in our effort to gain knowledge". Rationalists stress reason and innate ideas; empiricists stress sense experience and the *tabula rasa* ("blank slate") | Rationalists: **Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz**. Empiricists: **Bacon, Locke, Berkeley, Hume** | [SEP: Rationalism vs. Empiricism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/); [Wikipedia: Rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism); [Wikipedia: Empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) |
| **Dialectics** | From Greek *dialektikē* / *dialegesthai* "to converse". A method that progresses by resolving contradictions; popularised by Plato's Socratic dialogues | Socrates/Plato (dialogue), Hegel (idealist dialectic), Marx/Engels (materialist dialectic) | [Wikipedia: Dialectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic) |
| **Hegelian "thesis–antithesis–synthesis"** | The SEP calls this "the traditional idea" or "textbook" reading, illustrated by Being → Nothing → Becoming. Hegel's own key term is *aufheben* ("sublate"): to cancel and preserve at once | Hegel | [SEP: Hegel's Dialectics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-dialectics/) |

Islamic and medieval terms (kalām, tasawwuf, tariqa, fiqh, ijtihad, patristics, scholasticism) are defined in sections 4 and 6 below.

### Inferences
- MCQ pattern: "Which branch studies being/existence?" → ontology/metaphysics. "...knowledge?" → epistemology (gnoseology). "...values?" → axiology. "...beauty?" → aesthetics. "...correct reasoning?" → logic.
- Expect at least one item on the Engels/Soviet "basic question": materialism = matter primary, idealism = consciousness primary. In Uzbek-language textbooks the second side (can the world be known?) separates "gnoseological optimism" from agnosticism. This vocabulary is an inference from Engels' two-sided formulation, not from an Uzbek source.

- I could not fetch the Britannica "philosophy" article (Cloudflare block), so the definitions above rely on Wikipedia and SEP.

- Kant's fourth question ("What is man?", from his *Logic* lectures) was not confirmed in the fetched sources.

## 2. Ancient Eastern philosophy: India, China, ancient Iran and Central Asia

### Takeaway
- **India:** the Vedas, then the Upanishads (*ātman*, *brahman*, karma, *mokṣa*). Schools are split into "orthodox" (*āstika*, accepting Vedic authority) and "heterodox" (*nāstika*: **Buddhism, Jainism, Charvaka**).
- **China:** Confucius (ethics of ritual and humaneness), Laozi (Dao, *wuwei*), Mozi (impartial or "inclusive" care, the earliest consequentialism) and the Legalists (*fa*: law/standards, plus rewards and punishments).
- **Iran/Central Asia:** Zoroaster and the Avesta. Ethical dualism of Asha/Truth (Ahura Mazda) vs Druj/Lie (Angra Mainyu), and the triad "good thoughts, good words, good deeds". Many scholars place Zoroaster's homeland in eastern Greater Iran or Central Asia (Bactria, Chorasmia/Khwarazm, Margiana), which is why Uzbek curricula emphasise it.

### Inferences
- MCQ hooks: "unorthodox (nāstika) schools" = Buddhism + Jainism + Charvaka. "Indian materialism" = Charvaka/Lokayata. "Four Noble Truths" = Buddhism. "*Ahimsa* and the 24 tirthankaras" = Jainism. "*Wuwei*" = Daoism. "*Ren/li/junzi*" = Confucius. "Universal/inclusive love (*jian ai*)" = Mohism. "Rule by law, rewards and punishments" = Legalism (Han Fei). "Good thoughts, good words, good deeds" = Zoroastrianism.
- Uzbek curricula usually present Zoroastrianism as a Central Asian heritage (Khwarazm/Bactria) and the Avesta as the oldest written monument of the region. The scholarly homeland theories above (Frye: Bactria/Chorasmia; Sarianidi: Bactria–Margiana) support "eastern Iran / Central Asia" as plausible, but **not certain**.

### Gaps
- **Date discrepancy (important):** older textbooks, and older Britannica editions, give Zoroaster c. 628 – c. 551 BCE (the "traditional" date). Britannica's current article says "2nd millennium BCE", and Iranica says the date is unknown. If an MCQ asks for "the traditional date", choose the 7th–6th c. BCE; if it asks about "modern scholarship", choose the 2nd millennium BCE. The old "c. 628–551" figure itself was not re-verified in a fetched source.
- The claim common in Uzbek textbooks that "the Avesta was created in Khwarazm" has no single authoritative source among those fetched. It is one of several scholarly hypotheses.

## 3. Ancient Western (Greek) philosophy: Presocratics to the Hellenistic schools

### Takeaway
- The Presocratics (6th–5th c. BCE) asked about the ***archē*** (origin or first principle) of all things: Thales said water, Anaximander the boundless (*apeiron*), Anaximenes air, Heraclitus fire, flux and the logos, the Pythagoreans number. The Eleatics (Parmenides, Zeno) denied change and plurality, and the atomists (Leucippus, Democritus) answered with atoms and void.
- The Sophists and Socrates turned philosophy toward human affairs. Plato (Forms, the Academy) and Aristotle (logic, the four causes, the Lyceum) built systems.
- The Hellenistic schools (Stoics, Epicureans, Skeptics) aimed at tranquillity: *apatheia* (Stoics), *ataraxia* (Epicureans, Skeptics).

### Inferences
- Common MCQ traps:
  - "Who said water?" → Thales; "air?" → Anaximenes; "*apeiron*?" → Anaximander (not Anaximenes).
  - Flux and the river → Heraclitus (Ephesus), not Parmenides. The motion paradoxes → Zeno **of Elea**, not Zeno **of Citium** (the Stoic founder).
  - The Academy → Plato; the Lyceum/Peripatetics → Aristotle; the Garden → Epicurus; the Stoa → Zeno of Citium.
- Plato's birth year varies across sources (SEP: "429?"; other references give 428/427). In MCQs, recognise Plato as **c. 427–347 BCE**.

## 4. Early medieval Central Asia and Islamic philosophy (7th–13th c.): Islam basics, the Arab conquest, kalām, Sufism, and the "first Renaissance" scholars

### Takeaway
- **Context:** Arab armies under **Qutayba ibn Muslim (governor of Khurasan 705–715)** conquered Transoxiana, and the **Battle of Talas (751)** ended Tang Chinese influence there. Under the Abbasids (the House of Wisdom of **al-Maʾmūn, r. 813–833**) and the **Samanids (819–999; capital Bukhara from 892)**, Central Asians led science and philosophy.
- **Theology:** kalām split into the rationalist **Muʿtazila** (God's unity and justice, the created Qurʾān), the **Ashʿarī** school, and the **Māturīdī** school of **al-Māturīdī of Samarkand (d. 944)**, which is predominant in Central Asia together with the **Hanafi** law school.
- **Sufism:** **tasawwuf** was organised into orders (**tariqa**). The great Central Asian orders are the **Yassawiya** (Ahmad Yasawi, d. 1166), the **Naqshbandiya** (Baha al-Din Naqshband, 1318–1389, Bukhara) and the **Kubrawiya** (Najm al-Din Kubra, killed by the Mongols in 1221, Khwarazm).
- **Most exam-relevant people:** al-Khwarizmi (algebra/algorithm), al-Farabi (the "Second Teacher"), Ibn Sina (the *Canon of Medicine* and the *Book of Healing/Cure*), al-Biruni (*India*, *Chronology*, *Masʿudic Canon*), al-Bukhari and al-Tirmidhi (hadith), al-Maturidi (kalām), Mahmud Kashgari (*Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk*), Yusuf Khass Hajib (*Kutadgu Bilig*).

### Cited Findings
#### 4.1 Islam: basic principles, branches, law schools (overview)
- **Five Pillars:** "*shahādah*, the Muslim profession of faith; *ṣalāt*, or prayer… five times each day; *zakāt*, the alms tax…; *ṣawm*, fasting during… Ramadan; and *hajj*, the major pilgrimage to Mecca". — [Britannica: Pillars of Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pillars-of-Islam)
- **Sunni vs Shia:** Shia Islam is "the second-largest branch of Islam". It holds that Muhammad designated his cousin and son-in-law **Ali ibn Abi Talib** as successor (caliph/imam); Sunni Islam is the largest branch. — [Wikipedia: Shia Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam); [Wikipedia: Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam)
- **Madhhab** ("way to act") = a school of Islamic jurisprudence. "The major Sunni madhhab are **Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali**." They emerged in the 9th–10th centuries and recognise each other's validity. — [Wikipedia: Madhhab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab)
- **Hanafi school:** from **Abū Ḥanīfah (c. 700–767)** via his disciples Abū Yūsuf and al-Shaybānī. "Noted for its extensive reliance on systematic reasoning (*raʾy*)". It "currently predominates in **Central Asia**", India, Pakistan, Turkey and the former Ottoman lands. — [Britannica: Ḥanafī school](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hanafiyah). It is the largest school, about 45% of Muslims; its sources are Qurʾān, Sunna, *ijmāʿ* (consensus) and *qiyās* (analogy). — [Wikipedia: Hanafi school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi_school)
- **Fiqh** = Islamic jurisprudence, literally "deep understanding". — [Wikipedia: Fiqh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiqh)
- **Ijtihād** = "the independent or original interpretation of problems not precisely covered by the Qurʾān, Hadith… and *ijmāʿ*", mainly via *raʾy* (personal judgment) and *qiyās* (analogy). Those qualified to do it are called ***mujtahids***. — [Britannica: ijtihād](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ijtihad)

#### 4.2 The Arab conquest of Central Asia and the setting of the "Golden Age"
- Under al-Walīd I (705–15), **Qutayba b. Muslim**, governor of Khurasan, recruited local non-Arabs and campaigned across the Oxus every year from 705. He took **Paykand (706)** and **Bukhara (709)**, where he imposed tribute and a garrison. — [Iranica: Arab ii. Arab conquest of Iran](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/arab-ii); [Wikipedia: Qutayba ibn Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutayba_ibn_Muslim) (governor of Khorasan 705–715; d. 715/6 in Ferghana)
- **Battle of Talas, 751:** Abbasid Caliphate (with Tibetan allies) vs **Tang China**; an Abbasid victory that consolidated Abbasid rule and ended Tang presence in Transoxiana. Chinese prisoners are "said to have introduced papermaking" to West Asia, but this is disputed. — [Wikipedia: Battle of Talas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Talas)
- **Samanid Empire (819–999):** a Sunni Iranian dynasty with capitals **Samarkand (819–892)** and **Bukhara (892–999)**; Persian at court, Arabic for theology. — [Wikipedia: Samanid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire). SEP gives the Samanid span as 819–1005, with Bukhara as capital. — [SEP: Ibn Sina](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/)
- **House of Wisdom (*Bayt/Dār al-Ḥikma*), Baghdad:** turned into a public academy and library under caliph **al-Maʾmūn (r. 813–833)**; destroyed in 1258. — [Wikipedia: House of Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Wisdom). Al-Khwarizmi worked there. — [Britannica: al-Khwārizmī](https://www.britannica.com/biography/al-Khwarizmi)
- **Khwarazm, early 11th c.:** the Maʾmunid Khwarazm-Shahs ruled from Gurganj (Urgench) from 995. Under Abu'l-Abbas Maʾmun, "several scholars, such as al-Biruni, resided in Khwarazm"; after his murder in 1017, Mahmud of Ghazna annexed Khwarazm. Uzbek sources call this court circle the "**Maʾmun Academy**" (Ibn Sina was there 1005–1012). — [Wikipedia: Maʾmun II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma%27mun_II); [Wikipedia: Khwarazm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazm); [BEA: Ibn Sina (Gurganj 1005–1012)](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ibn_Sina_BEA.htm)
- **Islamic Golden Age:** "dated from the 8th century CE to the 14th century", beginning under Harun al-Rashid (786–809) and the House of Wisdom. Some extend it to include the **Timurid Renaissance**. — [Wikipedia: Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age)

#### 4.4 Sufism (taṣawwuf) and the Central Asian orders (ṭarīqa)
- **Sufism / *taṣawwuf***: "mystical Islamic belief and practice in which Muslims seek… divine love and knowledge through direct personal experience of God". *Taṣawwuf* literally means "to dress in wool", from *ṣūf* "wool", after the woollen garment of early ascetics. Sufis are also called *fuqarāʾ* ("the poor"; Persian *darvīsh*, hence "fakir" and "dervish"). — [Britannica: Sufism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sufism)
- **Ṭarīqa** ("road, path, way"): at first an individual Sufi's path to direct knowledge (*maʿrifah*) of God. After the 12th century it meant a sheikh's ritual system and then the **order** itself, with a chain of spiritual descent (***silsilah***) back to the Prophet and initiated disciples (***murīd***). — [Britannica: tariqa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tariqa)

## 5. The Timurid period (late 14th – early 16th c.; the "second Renaissance"): Amir Temur, Ulugh Beg and the Samarkand school, logic and science, Jami and Navoi

### Takeaway
Amir Temur (Timur, 1336–1405; born Kesh/Shahrisabz) concentrated captured artisans and scholars in **Samarkand**. Among them were the logician-theologians **al-Taftāzānī** and **al-Sayyid al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī**, who debated before him. He built the Bibi-Khanym mosque, the Gur-e Amir and the Ak-Saray. His grandson **Ulugh Beg (1394–1449)** founded the **Samarkand madrasa (1417–1420)** and **observatory (from c. 1420; Britannica says begun 1428)**. With **Qāḍīzāde al-Rūmī**, **Jamshīd al-Kāshī** and **ʿAlī Qūshjī** he produced the ***Zīj-i Sulṭānī / Zīj-i Gurgānī*** (Zij of Ulugh Beg), whose **star catalogue (for 1437)** is the only large-scale independent star survey of medieval Islam. In Herat under **Sultan Husayn Bayqara (r. 1469–1506)**, **Jāmī (1414–1492)** and **Alisher Navoi (1441–1501)** made it the literary centre; Navoi raised **Chagatai Turkic** to classical status.

**Ulugh Beg's star catalogue: the numbers that get confused**
- BEA: the Samarkand team verified and corrected the coordinates of "**the more than 1,000 stars in Ptolemy's star catalogue**". The catalogue "for the year **1437** represents the **only large-scale observations of star coordinates** made in the Islamic realm in the medieval period". — [BEA: Ulugh Beg](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ulugh_Beg_BEA.htm)
- MacTutor: "Published in 1437, it gives the positions of **992 stars**", "the first comprehensive stellar catalogue since that of Ptolemy". — [MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Ulugh_Beg/)
- Wikipedia: Ulugh Beg redetermined "**992 fixed stars, to which he added 27 stars from al-Sufi's *Book of Fixed Stars* (964)**" that were too far south to observe from Samarkand. That makes about **1,018–1,019** stars in total, which is why Uzbek and Russian textbooks say "**1018 stars**". The catalogue was edited by Thomas Hyde at Oxford in **1665**. — [Wikipedia: Zij-i Sultani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zij-i_Sultani)

## 6. The "first / second / third Renaissance" framing: what reliable sources say vs Uzbek official discourse

### Takeaway
- "**First Renaissance**" (roughly 9th–12th c.: Khwarizmi, Farghani, Farabi, Ibn Sina, Biruni, Bukhari, Tirmidhi, Maturidi) and "**second Renaissance**" (the Timurid 14th–15th c.: Ulugh Beg, Navoi, Jami) is an **Uzbek official and pedagogical periodisation**. President Mirziyoyev uses it, and adds a goal of building a "**third Renaissance**" in today's "New Uzbekistan".
- International scholarship uses overlapping but different labels: "**Islamic Golden Age**" (8th–14th c.), "**Timurid Renaissance**" (late 14th – early 16th c., a term used "not without reservation"), and S. Frederick Starr's "**Lost Enlightenment / Central Asia's Golden Age from the Arab Conquest to Tamerlane**". None of these numbers the Renaissances.

## 7. Medieval Western philosophy: patristics and scholasticism (Augustine, Boethius, Anselm, Abelard, Aquinas, Ockham, the problem of universals)

### Takeaway
- **Patristics** is the philosophy of the Church Fathers, roughly 2nd–8th c. **Augustine (354–430)** is its summit: *Confessions*, *City of God*, grace and free will, and the certainty of self-knowledge against the skeptics.
- **Scholasticism** (11th–14th c., universities) used logic to reconcile faith and reason. Its "father" is **Anselm (1033–1109**; the ontological argument, *fides quaerens intellectum*). Its summit is **Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274**; *Summa theologiae*, the Five Ways, Aristotle + Christianity). Its critical end is **William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347**; nominalism, "Ockham's razor").
- The **problem of universals** (realism vs nominalism vs conceptualism) was framed by **Porphyry's three questions**, transmitted by **Boethius**.

### Cited Findings
- **Patristics** = "the study of the early Christian writers who are designated **Church Fathers**". — [Wikipedia: Patristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristics)
- **Scholasticism** (Britannica): "the philosophical systems and speculative tendencies of various medieval Christian thinkers, who, working against a background of fixed religious dogma, sought to solve anew general philosophical problems (as of **faith and reason, will and intellect, realism and nominalism, and the provability of the existence of God**)". It was "initially under the influence of… patristic philosophy, especially **Augustinianism**, and later under that of **Aristotle**". Its "Golden Age" was the **13th century** (Albertus Magnus, Aquinas). From the Renaissance to the 19th c. "Scholasticism" was used as a term of contempt. — [Britannica: Scholasticism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Scholasticism)

**The problem of universals**
- At bottom, the problem is "how universal cognition of singular things is possible". — [SEP: The Medieval Problem of Universals](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/universals-medieval/)
- **Porphyry's questions** (*Isagoge*), which Porphyry declined to answer: (a) **whether genera and species are real or exist in bare thoughts alone**, (b) whether, if real, they are corporeal or incorporeal, and (c) whether they are separated or exist in sensible things. — [SEP: The Medieval Problem of Universals](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/universals-medieval/)
- **Realism** (Plato: the Forms are more real than sensible things) vs **nominalism**. An early nominalist jibe at Plato is Diogenes of Sinope: "I've seen Plato's cups and table, but not his 'cupness' and 'tableness'." Aristotle rejected separate Forms (moderate realism). — [Wikipedia: Problem of universals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_universals)
- Neoplatonists (Plotinus, Porphyry) and Christians (Augustine, Boethius) saw a "basic concordance" between Plato and Aristotle. — [SEP: The Medieval Problem of Universals](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/universals-medieval/)

## 8. Modern and contemporary Western philosophy: Bacon to Marx, plus 19th–20th-century schools

### Takeaway
- **Modern philosophy** turns to epistemology: **empiricism** (Bacon, Locke, Hume: knowledge from experience) vs **rationalism** (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz: knowledge from reason and innate ideas). **Kant** synthesises them ("Copernican revolution"; the three *Critiques*). **Hegel** builds absolute idealism and dialectics. **Feuerbach** (anthropological materialism) and **Marx** (historical/dialectical materialism) turn Hegel "materialist".
- **Contemporary schools** to know: **positivism** (Comte; Vienna Circle neopositivism), **existentialism** ("existence precedes essence": Kierkegaard → Heidegger, Sartre, Camus), **pragmatism** (Peirce, James, Dewey), **phenomenology** (Husserl, from 1900–01), **analytic philosophy** (Frege, Russell, Moore, Wittgenstein).

## 9. "Commonly confused" list, date discrepancies between sources, and a master timeline

### Takeaway
Most MCQ traps come from look-alike names (two Zenos, two Tirmidhīs, two Maʾmūns, two Bacons), look-alike titles (Ibn Sīnā's *Canon* vs his *Shifāʾ*; Ibn Sīnā's *Qānūn* vs al-Bīrūnī's *Qānūn*; *Kutadgu Bilig* vs *Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk*), look-alike epithets ("Second Teacher" vs "Second ʿAllāma"), and numbers that differ by source (Ulugh Beg's 992/1018/1019 stars; Ibn Sīnā 970 vs 980; al-Fārābī 870/878).
