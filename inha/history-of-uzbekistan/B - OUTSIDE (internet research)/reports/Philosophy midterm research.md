# Master the Hooks That Win Philosophy MCQs

To score well in NTS3032 *Basics of Philosophy*, a student needs three things that outside sources supply better than the slides alone. The first is one verified **"hook" for every thinker, school and concept**: the term, book title, epithet, place or date that a single option will match. The second is a working map of the **look-alike traps** that distractors are built from. The third is the **post-Soviet theory framework** behind the course's ontology half: Engels' "basic question of philosophy", the three laws of dialectics and the paired categories. The history half leans on Central Asia's heritage, which Uzbek official discourse packages as a **"first Renaissance"** (al-Khwarizmi through al-Biruni, plus al-Maturidi's kalām and the Yassawiya and Naqshbandiya Sufi orders) and a **Timurid "second Renaissance"** (Ulugh Beg, Jami, Navoi). The Stanford and Internet Encyclopedias of Philosophy, Britannica, Encyclopaedia Iranica and the *Biographical Encyclopedia of Astronomers* settle most facts. They disagree on a few dates and numbers, such as Ibn Sina's birth year, Ulugh Beg's star count and Zoroaster's era, so this report flags which value a class quiz is most likely to reward. The stakes are lopsided. The weekly five-question, ten-minute MCQs count as attendance, so **missing more than three fails the course outright**, and the 30% midterm falls in week 8, **24–30 October 2026, five days from today**. The study method with the strongest evidence is **practice testing spread over several short sessions**, not rereading or highlighting. For an MCQ exam, practise in recall form: cover the options, produce the answer yourself, then check. Students benefit most from practice tests that require recall from memory, not ones that merely ask them to recognise the right answer ([Dunlosky, *American Educator*](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)). The sections below give the fact tables, a timeline, the trap lists, worked examples for every law and category, a five-day plan, sixty practice questions with an answer key, and the free sources worth opening.

## Engels' "basic question" is the spine the whole course hangs on

"Philosophy" comes from the Greek *philosophia*, **"love of wisdom"** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/philosophy); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)). Today it names "a systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning topics like existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)). Tradition credits **Pythagoras** as the first person to call himself a *philosophos*. The SEP warns, though, that later antiquity glorified him "in a largely unhistorical fashion", so treat this as the textbook answer rather than settled history ([Wikipedia: Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras); [SEP: Pythagoras](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)). Intro questions mostly test the branches through their Greek roots, and the stem's keyword usually gives the answer away: "being" points to ontology, "knowledge" to epistemology, "value" to axiology.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Epistemology** (= **gnoseology**) | *epistēmē* "knowledge" | what knowledge is and how it is acquired | "gnoseology" is the usual post-Soviet term; "epistemology" was coined only in the 19th century | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) |

Post-Soviet curricula, Uzbek ones included, organise the field around a question Friedrich Engels posed in 1886: "The great basic question of all philosophy, especially of more recent philosophy, is that concerning the relation of **thinking and being**" ([Engels, *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy*, Part 2](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm)). The question has **two sides**, and MCQs exploit the difference. The first is **primacy**: those "who asserted the primacy of spirit to nature" formed "the camp of idealism", while those who "regarded nature as primary, belong to the various schools of materialism". The second is **knowability**, which Engels calls "the question of identity of thinking and being": can thought grasp the real world? He counts Hume and Kant among the doubters (the agnostics) and answers them with "practice — namely, experiment and industry", citing chemists who learned to make the plant dye alizarin from coal tar ([same](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm)). Do not confuse the basic question with Kant's three questions, "What can I know? What should I do? What may I hope?" ([Wikipedia: Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)).

Most "-ism" questions become easy once you keep **two axes** apart. "How many substances are there?" yields monism, dualism or pluralism. "Which kind is primary?" yields materialism or idealism. So Hegel is an idealist monist and Marx a materialist monist; Spirkin's Soviet textbook draws this distinction explicitly ([Spirkin, ch. 2.2](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s02.html)). Idealism splits by **whose mind** comes first: the individual perceiver's (**subjective idealism**, Berkeley's *esse est percipi*, "to be is to be perceived") or a supra-individual Idea or Absolute (**objective idealism**, the German idealists culminating in Hegel) ([SEP: Idealism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/idealism/)). Textbooks conventionally put Plato's Forms under objective idealism too ([Wikipedia: Idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism)). Agnosticism answers the knowability side, not the primacy side, so "agnostic" is never a synonym for "idealist".

|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Agnosticism** | knowability | things as they are in themselves cannot be known | Hume and Kant (in Engels' scheme) | [Engels](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm) |
| **Monism / dualism / pluralism** | how many substances | one / two / many | Spinoza's God-or-Nature / Descartes' mind and body / Leibniz's monads and Empedocles' four roots | [SEP: Monism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/monism/); [SEP: Substance](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/substance/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralism_(philosophy)) |

Two topics that often open courses like this one, the **types of worldview** and the **functions of philosophy**, are not covered by the outside sources used here. Take them straight from the slides.

## Every thinker on the syllabus reduces to one testable hook

An MCQ cannot ask you to explain Ibn Sina. It can only ask you to match him with a book, an epithet, a city, a date or a doctrine. The tables below boil each figure down to those matchable facts and put the most-tested hook in **bold**. They draw on the SEP, the IEP, Britannica, Encyclopaedia Iranica, the McGill-hosted *Biographical Encyclopedia of Astronomers* (BEA) and MacTutor. Where reference works disagree on a date, the table shows the spread, and the trap section says which value to pick. Expect Uzbek spellings on slides: official English-language texts write "Khorezmi, Beruni, Ibn Sino, Ulugbek, Navoi" ([president.uz](https://president.uz/en/lists/view/4057)).

### Ancient India, China and Iran are tested through founding texts and paired terms

Indian philosophy grows out of the Vedas and the Upanishads, and its schools split over whether they accept Vedic authority. The orthodox *darśanas* (Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta) accept it. **Jainism, Buddhism and the materialist Charvaka** were "regarded as the unorthodox darsanas" ([IEP: Jain Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/jain/)). Chinese thought of the Spring-and-Autumn and Warring States eras divides into four schools: Confucian ritual ethics, Daoist naturalness, Mohist impartial care and the Legalist (*fa*) art of rule.

For Central Asia the key ancient source is **Zoroaster and the Avesta**. Encyclopaedia Iranica places the Avesta's "geographical locus" in **eastern Iran**. Scholars have proposed three homelands: **Chorasmia (Khwarazm)** (Henning), **Margiana–Bactria** (Humbach) and **Sistan** (Gnoli) ([Iranica: Avesta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book)). Richard Frye "voted for Bactria and Chorasmia" ([Iranica: Zoroaster](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroaster-ii-general-survey)). This is why Uzbek curricula claim Zoroastrianism as regional heritage. Still, the homeland is one hypothesis among several, and nobody knows Zoroaster's dates (see the date table).

### Greek philosophy runs from "what is everything made of?" to schools with addresses

The Presocratics of the 6th and 5th centuries BCE looked for the ***archē***, the first principle of all things. Aristotle names **Thales** as the first to investigate such principles ([IEP: Thales](https://iep.utm.edu/thales/)). The Eleatics then denied that change and plurality are real, and the atomists answered with atoms moving in a void. The Sophists and Socrates turned philosophy toward human affairs, Plato and Aristotle built systems, and the Hellenistic schools sought tranquillity. Three traps recur here. The first is the Milesian first principles: water (Thales), the Boundless (Anaximander) and air (Anaximenes). The second is the **two Zenos**: Zeno of Elea's paradoxes vs Zeno of Citium's Stoa. The third is the four **school addresses**: Academy, Lyceum, Garden and Stoa.

### Central Asia's "first Renaissance" turns on who wrote which book

The setting explains who the key people are. **Qutayba ibn Muslim**, governor of Khurasan from 705 to 715, campaigned across the Oxus every year from 705 and took **Bukhara in 709** ([Iranica: Arab ii](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/arab-ii)). The **Battle of Talas (751)**, an Abbasid victory over **Tang China**, ended Tang influence in Transoxiana ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Talas)). After the conquest, Central Asians led science and philosophy in three settings. The first was Baghdad's **House of Wisdom**, which became a public academy under the Abbasid caliph **al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833)** and where al-Khwarizmi worked ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Wisdom); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/al-Khwarizmi)). The second was the **Samanid** state (819–999), whose capital moved from Samarkand to **Bukhara in 892** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire)). The third was the court of the **Ma'munid Khwarazm-Shahs at Gurganj (Urgench)** from 995. Al-Biruni lived there and **Ibn Sina stayed from 1005 to 1012**, until Mahmud of Ghazna annexed Khwarazm in 1017. Uzbek sources call this circle the "**Ma'mun Academy**" ([Wikipedia: Ma'mun II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma%27mun_II); [BEA: Ibn Sina](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ibn_Sina_BEA.htm)). Do not mix up the two Ma'muns: one is the caliph in Baghdad, the other the Khwarazm-Shah in Gurganj.

A few Islamic basics anchor the kalām and Sufism questions. The **Five Pillars** are *shahādah* (the profession of faith), *ṣalāt* (prayer), *zakāt* (alms), *ṣawm* (the Ramadan fast) and *hajj* (pilgrimage) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pillars-of-Islam)). ***Ijtihād*** is not a pillar. It means the "independent or original interpretation" of problems the sources do not settle, carried out by qualified *mujtahids* ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ijtihad)). *Fiqh* is Islamic jurisprudence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiqh)). The four Sunni law schools (*madhhab*) are **Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab)). The **Hanafi** school of Abu Hanifa (c. 700–767) is "noted for its extensive reliance on systematic reasoning (*ra'y*)" and "currently predominates in Central Asia" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hanafiyah)). The regional formula to remember is **Hanafi in law, Maturidi in creed**.

**Kalām** (*ʿilm al-kalām*, literally "the science of discourse") is "the scholastic, speculative, or rational study of Islamic theology" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam)). Its three schools are a favourite source of distractors:

For the exam, use this safe profile: Maturidism is **linked to the Hanafis, based in Samarkand, moderately rationalist, and stronger on human free will than Ash'arism**.

**Sufism** (*taṣawwuf*) seeks "divine love and knowledge through direct personal experience of God". The word literally means "to dress in wool", from *ṣūf*, after the woollen garments of early ascetics ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sufism)). A ***ṭarīqa*** ("path") first meant one person's way to God. After the 12th century it came to mean an **order**, with a chain of masters (*silsila*) and initiated disciples (*murīd*) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tariqa)). Three orders grew from Central Asian soil:

### The Timurid "second Renaissance" pairs Samarkand's observatory with Herat's poets

**Amir Temur** was born in **1336 at Kesh (Shahrisabz)**. He proclaimed himself sovereign at Samarkand around 1370 and died at Otrar on 19 February 1405 ([Britannica: Timur](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Timur)). He moved artisans and scholars from conquered cities to Samarkand. He built the Ak-Saray palace at Kesh (1390–1405) and the Gur-e Amir mausoleum (1403–04), and the Bibi-Khanym mosque was finished in 1404 ([Wikipedia: Gur-e-Amir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gur-e-Amir); [Wikipedia: Bibi-Khanym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibi-Khanym_Mosque)). In 1397/98 he also built **Ahmad Yasawi's mausoleum** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ahmed-Yesevi)). The period's logic and theology came from two scholars at his court. **Al-Jurjani** was moved to Samarkand after Timur took Shiraz in 1387, and he **debated al-Taftazani "in the presence of Tamerlane"** ([BEA: Jurjani](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Jurjani_BEA.htm)). Both men's commentaries stayed in use as madrasa textbooks for centuries ([Wikipedia: Taftazani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%27d_al-Din_al-Taftazani)). Under **Shahrukh (r. 1405–1447)**, Herat became "the centre of a renewed and artistically brilliant Persian culture" ([Britannica: Timurid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timurid-dynasty)).

The scientific peak is **Ulugh Beg's Samarkand school**. Britannica says that under his rule the dynasty "reached its cultural peak" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ulugh-Beg)). He built his **madrasa on the Registan in 1417–1420** ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Ulugh_Beg/)). His **observatory came in the 1420s**: the BEA says it was founded in 1420, while Britannica says it was begun in 1428. It was a circular building about 46 m across and 30 m high, built around a giant Fakhri sextant with a radius of about 40 m ([BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ulugh_Beg_BEA.htm)). V. L. Vyatkin found its remains in 1908 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulugh_Beg_Observatory)). The team's handbook, the ***Zīj-i Sulṭānī*** (also called the *Zīj-i Gurgānī*), contains a star catalogue for the year **1437**. The BEA calls it "the only large-scale observations of star coordinates made in the Islamic realm in the medieval period" ([BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ulugh_Beg_BEA.htm)). The catalogue gives **992** re-observed stars, plus 27 taken from al-Sufi's *Book of Fixed Stars*, for a total of about **1,018**. Thomas Hyde printed it at Oxford in 1665 ([Wikipedia: Zij-i Sultani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zij-i_Sultani)). In Herat, **Sultan Husayn Bayqara (r. 1469–1506)** was "the last Timurid ruler of major importance in Khorasan" ([Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hosayn-bayqara)). He was patron to **Jami**, "often regarded as the last great mystical poet of Iran", and to **Navoi**, "the greatest representative of Chagatai literature". Navoi studied the mystics under Jami, and Chagatai came to be called "the language of Nava'i" and developed into modern Uzbek ([Britannica: Jami](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jami); [Britannica: Navai](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ali-Shir-Navai); [Britannica: Uzbek literature](https://www.britannica.com/art/Uzbek-literature)). UNESCO lists Samarkand as a World Heritage site (inscribed 2001). It says the city "had its most significant development in the Timurid period from the 14th to the 15th centuries" and names the Registan, Bibi-Khanum, Shakhi-Zinda, Gur-Emir and Ulugh Beg's Observatory ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/603/)).

### "First, second, third Renaissance" is a state periodisation, not a scholarly consensus

The numbered Renaissances come from Uzbek official discourse. At the UN General Assembly in 2017, President Shavkat Mirziyoyev praised "a whole galaxy of outstanding representatives of the **Central Asian Renaissance**" ([president.uz, 2017](https://president.uz/en/lists/view/1063)). In his 29 December 2020 address to parliament he set the goal of "securing the foundation for a **new Renaissance**", and called for conditions that would nurture "new Khorezmis, Berunis, Ibn Sinos, Ulugbeks, Navois and Baburs" ([president.uz, 2020](https://president.uz/en/lists/view/4057)). In January 2021, at Tashkent's Center for Islamic Civilization, he explained: "Many people ask why the President cites **two Renaissances** as an example. This is an idea, our goal for the future". He hoped the centre would become "one of the foundations of the **third Renaissance**" ([UzA, 2021](https://uza.uz/en/posts/center-for-islamic-civilization-harmony-of-religion-science-and-modernity_237881)). The centre's planned galleries were "the First Renaissance (8th–13th centuries)" and "the Second Renaissance (15th century)". Those dates come via a secondary source, so treat them as approximate ([Wikipedia: Islamic Civilization Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Civilization_Center)).

International scholarship labels the same eras differently and never numbers them. The "**Islamic Golden Age**" runs from the 8th to the 14th century ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age)). "**Timurid Renaissance**" covers the late 14th to early 16th century, but its use "has not been without reservation", and some scholars see the period as a "swan song" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_Renaissance)). The main English-language book is S. Frederick Starr's *Lost Enlightenment: Central Asia's Golden Age from the Arab Conquest to Tamerlane* (2013) ([Wikipedia: Starr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._Frederick_Starr)). On the exam, answer with the Uzbek numbering. In an essay, note that "Renaissance" is a term borrowed from Italian history and is part of the "New Uzbekistan" narrative.

### Medieval Christian thought divides into Fathers, Schoolmen and the fight over universals

**Patristics** is "the study of the early Christian writers who are designated Church Fathers", and **Augustine** is its summit ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristics); [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)). **Scholasticism** came later, in the universities. Its thinkers "sought to solve anew general philosophical problems (as of faith and reason, will and intellect, realism and nominalism, and the provability of the existence of God)". It drew first on Augustine and later on Aristotle, and it reached its "Golden Age" in the 13th century ([Britannica: Scholasticism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Scholasticism)). Its signature debate, the **problem of universals**, goes back to three questions Porphyry raised and declined to answer: whether genera and species are real or exist "in bare thoughts alone", whether they are bodily or not, and whether they exist apart from things. Boethius passed these questions on to the Middle Ages ([SEP: The Medieval Problem of Universals](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/universals-medieval/)).

### Modern philosophy argues about knowledge until Kant splits the difference and Marx inverts Hegel

Modern philosophy is organised around how we know. **Empiricists** (Bacon, Locke, Hume) derive knowledge from experience. **Rationalists** (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) derive it from reason and innate ideas. The SEP says the dispute concerns "the extent to which we are dependent upon experience in our effort to gain knowledge" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/)). **Kant**, "the central figure in modern philosophy", synthesised the two ([SEP: Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)). Hegel built absolute idealism and dialectics on top of Kant, and Feuerbach and Marx then turned Hegel's system into materialism. That last step links back to the start of the course: Engels' 1886 essay on Feuerbach is the source of the "basic question of philosophy" ([Engels](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm)).

## Ontology and dialectics reward one definition plus one worked example

The theory half of the course follows the post-Soviet dialectical-materialist textbook template. The closest free English version of that tradition is A. Spirkin's *Dialectical Materialism* (Progress Publishers, 1983). Its primary sources, short chapters by Engels and Lenin, are free on marxists.org ([Spirkin, index](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/index.html)). In the course's theme list, ontology ("Philosophical Interpretation of Being") falls inside the midterm's weeks 1–7. "Philosophical Laws, Categories and Methods" comes next, so it will probably appear in the weekly MCQs after the midterm and on the final; check the syllabus. Each concept below has a definition worth quoting and a worked example worth applying. MCQs test the definition; application questions test the example.

### Being comes in four forms, and matter is defined by its independence from mind

The idea behind ontology goes back to Aristotle's "first philosophy", "the study of being qua being" ([SEP: Aristotle's Metaphysics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-metaphysics/)). The word itself only appeared in the early modern period ([ontology.co](https://www.ontology.co/history.htm)). **Jacob Lorhard's *Ogdoas Scholastica* (1606)** is its earliest printed use. **Rudolf Goclenius's *Lexicon philosophicum* (1613)** defines it as "philosophia de ente" ("the philosophy of being"). Johannes Clauberg (1647) proposed "Ontosophia". **Christian Wolff's *Philosophia prima sive Ontologia* (1730)** made it a system: **general metaphysics**, the study of being as such, as against **special metaphysics** (cosmology, psychology, theology). Wolff systematised the term; he did not coin it.

The course's master concept is **being**. "Being, in the broadest sense, is existence", and the category goes back to Parmenides' "Being is; non-being is not at all" ([ru.wikipedia: Бытие](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%91%D1%8B%D1%82%D0%B8%D0%B5)). The Uzbek term *borliq* is defined as "a philosophical concept expressing objectively existing reality". Uzbek sources also borrow from Islamic philosophy the pair ***vujudi vojib*** (necessary existence) and ***vujudi mumkin*** (possible existence) ([uz.wikipedia: Borliq](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borliq)). The pair matches Ibn Sina's **Necessary Existent**, the being that "has no cause" ([SEP: Ibn Sina's Metaphysics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina-metaphysics/)), which makes it a likely bridge question in an Uzbek course.

Hegel's *Logic* opens by arguing that pure Being, because it is wholly undetermined, "is equally a Nothing", and that the unity of the two is **Becoming** ([SEP: Hegel's Dialectics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-dialectics/)). Four Western contrasts are worth one line each ([SEP: Existence](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/existence/); [SEP: Ontological Commitment](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ontological-commitment/)). Kant held that existence is not a real property of a thing. Frege and Russell made it a property of concepts. Meinong allowed a golden mountain to have being without existing. Quine summed up ontological commitment as "to be is to be the value of a variable".

Post-Soviet textbooks, following Frolov's *Introduction to Philosophy* (*Введение в философию*), teach **four basic forms of being** ([ru.wikipedia: Бытие](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%91%D1%8B%D1%82%D0%B8%D0%B5)). The examples in the last column are practice illustrations, not quotations.

| Form of being | Sub-forms | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Being of things and processes (nature)** | "first nature" (natural things) vs "second nature" (things humans have made) | a wild tulip in the Chatkal mountains (first nature); the Tashkent metro (second nature) |
| **2. Being of the human** | the human as a body among things, and as a member of society | you get hungry like any organism, and you vote as a citizen |
| **3. Being of the spiritual (ideal)** | **individualised** (a person's own consciousness) vs **objectified** (culture that exists outside individuals) | your nerves before an exam (individualised); Navoi's poems, the Uzbek language, a library (objectified) |
| **4. Being of the social** | the individual's being in society vs the being of society as a whole | your life in a family and mahalla vs Uzbek society and its economy |

Uzbek Wikipedia lists the forms differently: organic and inorganic nature, the biosphere, social being, objective ideal being, and human life ([uz.wikipedia: Borliq](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borliq)). Match whichever version your slides use.

**Substance** is the ultimate bearer of properties. Spirkin calls it "the vehicle, the bearer of all properties and relationships of everything that exists"; for dialectical materialism that bearer is **matter** ([Spirkin, ch. 2.2](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s02.html)). The classic positions on substance are worth knowing by name ([SEP: Substance](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/substance/)). For Aristotle, substances are "not predicable of or attributable to anything else": an individual (this dog) is a primary substance, and its kind (dog) a secondary one. Descartes posited two created substances, Spinoza one and Leibniz many. Locke called substance "something I know not what", and Hume reduced things to bundles of properties.

The definition of matter that MCQs quote is **Lenin's**: "matter is a philosophical category denoting the objective reality which is given to man by his sensations, and which is copied, photographed and reflected by our sensations, while existing independently of them" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.2](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s02.html)). Notice what it leaves out: atoms and mass. It defines matter by its **independence from consciousness**, which is why it survives every change in physics. Spirkin lists the attributes of matter as "objective existence, structure, indestructibility, motion, space, time, reflection and information" ([same](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s02.html)).

Positions follow [SEP: Idealism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/idealism/), [SEP: Substance](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/substance/) and [Engels](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1886/ludwig-feuerbach/ch02.htm).

### Motion is matter's mode of existence, and space and time are its forms

Engels defined motion as "the mode of existence, the inherent attribute of matter". It covers "all changes and processes occurring in the universe, from mere change of place right up to thinking", and it is "as uncreatable as indestructible" ([Engels, *Dialectics of Nature*, ch. III](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1883/don/ch03.htm)). Spirkin compresses this to "**To be means to be in motion**" and adds that motion is self-motion. He makes **rest relative**: "Possessing absolute rest means ceasing to exist", and bodies rest only "in relation to a given system of reference" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.3](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s03.html)).

A worked example: a student sitting "still" in a metro carriage is at rest relative to the carriage. She is moving relative to the platform, the turning Earth and the Sun. Inside her, physical, chemical and biological processes are running, and her thinking is the highest form of motion.

Check the slide's list of **forms of motion**. If it gives five (mechanical, physical, chemical, biological, social), treat that as a textbook condensation. Engels' chapter itself shows mechanical, physical (heat, electricity, magnetism) and chemical motion turning into one another. Spirkin's list runs from elementary-particle motion up to social motion, with each higher form containing the lower ones without reducing to them ([Engels, ch. III](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1883/don/ch03.htm); [Spirkin, ch. 2.3](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s03.html)).

Spirkin defines **space** as "a form of coordination of coexisting objects and states of matter" and **time** as their coordination "in their succession". Both are "universal forms of the existence of matter". Space has three dimensions; time has one and is "inevitable, unrepeatable and irreversible" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.4](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s04.html)). These formulas restate **Leibniz's relational view**, in which space is "an order of coexistence" and time "an order of succession", but they insist that these orders are objective. The rival **substantial** view is Newton's absolute space and time, which he defended with his rotating-bucket argument. Mach later mounted "the most sustained, comprehensive, and influential attack on absolute space" ([SEP: Absolute and Relational Space and Motion](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-theories/)). Kant gave a third answer, treating space and time as forms of our own sensibility ([SEP: Idealism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/idealism/)).

| | Substantial (absolute) conception | Relational conception |
|---|---|---|
| Core claim | space and time are independent entities, "containers" | space and time are orders of relations among things (coexistence, succession) |
| Names | Newton (the *Principia* Scholium; the bucket) | Leibniz; Mach; relativity theory |
| If all matter vanished | empty space and time would remain | "with the disappearance of matter space and time would also disappear" (Spirkin) |
| Dialectical materialism | rejects it | adopts it, adding that space and time are **objective** |

The classic ancient contrast on being often appears as a paired question. **Parmenides** held that what is must be "ungenerated and deathless, whole and uniform, and still and perfect", so change and plurality are not truly real ([SEP: Parmenides](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/parmenides/)). **Heraclitus** stressed the opposite. In fragment B12, "On those stepping into rivers staying the same other and other waters flow". Opposites form a unity, and the cosmos is "an everliving fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures" ([SEP: Heraclitus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heraclitus/)). Zeno's paradoxes defended the Eleatic view against motion. Engels gave the classic dialectical reply: "Motion itself is a contradiction" ([Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, ch. XII](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1877/anti-duhring/ch10.htm)).

| | Parmenides | Heraclitus |
|---|---|---|
| Place, date | Elea (southern Italy), early 5th c. BCE | Ephesus (Ionia), c. 500 BCE |
| Being | one, ungenerated, imperishable, motionless | an ever-changing process held together by the Logos |
| Change | not truly real; it belongs to the "way of opinion" | constant; identity persists *through* change (river fragment B12) |

### The three laws answer why, how and where development goes

Engels listed "the law of the transformation of quantity into quality and *vice versa*; the law of the interpenetration of opposites; the law of the negation of the negation". He traced all three to Hegel, who had developed them "in his idealist fashion as mere laws of *thought*" ([Engels, *Dialectics of Nature*, ch. II](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1883/don/ch02.htm)). In Hegel's *Logic* the first law comes from the Doctrine of Being; the second fills the Doctrine of Essence; and the third is "the fundamental law for the construction of the whole system". Engels' fix was to "turn the thing round" and treat them as laws of nature, history and thought. He defined dialectics itself as "the science of the general laws of motion and development of nature, human society and thought" ([Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, ch. XIII](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1877/anti-duhring/ch11.htm)). *Dialectics of Nature* was written in 1873–1886 and first published in the USSR in 1925 ([marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1883/don/index.htm)).

Engels lists the quantity–quality law first. Lenin, however, called "the splitting of a single whole and the cognition of its contradictory parts" the "essence of dialectics" ([Lenin, 1915](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1915/misc/x02.htm)), and courses that follow him put the unity of opposites first. Follow your slides' order.

The standard teaching device pairs each law with one question about development; memorise it as **WHY, HOW, WHERE**. No single source states all three pairings in one sentence, but each appears explicitly somewhere. Contradictions are the "internal sources of development" ([ru.wikipedia: unity of opposites](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BD_%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B2%D0%B0_%D0%B8_%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%80%D1%8C%D0%B1%D1%8B_%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B8%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%B6%D0%BD%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D0%B9)) and the key to "self-movement" ([Lenin](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1915/misc/x02.htm)). Development "takes place through the accumulation of quantitative changes" that exceed the measure and end in a leap ([ru.wikipedia: quantity into quality](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BD_%D0%BF%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%B5%D1%85%D0%BE%D0%B4%D0%B0_%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%B8%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%BD%D1%8B%D1%85_%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%B9_%D0%B2_%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%BD%D1%8B%D0%B5)). The third law reflects development's "directionality" along a spiral ([ru.wikipedia: negation of the negation](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BD_%D0%BE%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%86%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F_%D0%BE%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%86%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F)).

| Law | Answers | Key concepts | Classic example | Where it sits in Hegel's *Logic* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unity and struggle of opposites** (Engels: "interpenetration of opposites") | **WHY**: the *source* of development, internal contradiction driving self-movement | opposite; contradiction (internal or external, antagonistic or non-antagonistic); unity is relative, struggle is absolute | + and − charge; action and reaction; heredity and variability | the Doctrine of Essence |
| **Transformation of quantity into quality** (and vice versa) | **HOW**: the *mechanism*, accumulation that breaks the measure in a leap | quality, property, quantity, **measure**, nodal point, **leap** (momentary or gradual) | water at 0 °C and 100 °C; Napoleon's cavalry vs the Mamelukes; O₂ vs O₃ | the Doctrine of Being |
| **Negation of the negation** | **WHERE**: the *direction and form*, an ascending spiral with a partial return on a higher level | dialectical negation, sublation (*aufheben*), three stages, the spiral | barley grain → plant → many grains; *a* → −*a* → *a*² | the construction principle of the whole system |

The vocabulary of the quantity law is tested word by word ([Spirkin, ch. 2.9](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s09.html)). **Quality** is "an integral definition of the functional unity of an object's essential properties". A **property** is how one aspect of that quality shows itself in relation to other objects. **Quantity** "expresses the external, formal relation" of objects: number, size, degree. **Measure** is "the quantitative limit of a given quality". A **leap** is "a resolving of contradictions" and comes in two types, **momentary** and **gradual** ("a process of a certain duration"), so "a leap is always sudden" is a false option. Engels' examples are the ones that keep recurring ([Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, ch. XII](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1877/anti-duhring/ch10.htm)). Water "changes at 0° C from the liquid into the solid state, and at 100°C from the liquid into the gaseous state". And while "two Mamelukes were undoubtedly more than a match for three Frenchmen", "1,000 Frenchmen invariably defeated 1,500 Mamelukes".

The opposites law turns on the **opposite**, "the extreme case of difference", and on **contradiction**, "the unity of opposites and their struggle" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.11](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s11.html)). A contradiction is internal (within one system) or external (between systems). It is also antagonistic (between "implacably hostile classes") or non-antagonistic. Lenin's line is the most quoted stem on this topic: "The unity ... of opposites is conditional, temporary, transitory, **relative**. The struggle of mutually exclusive opposites is **absolute**" ([Lenin, 1915](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1915/misc/x02.htm)).

The negation law depends on **dialectical negation**, which "does not mean simply saying no, or declaring that something does not exist, or destroying it in any way one likes". One must "not only negate, but also sublate the negation", so that a second negation remains possible ([Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, ch. XIII](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1877/anti-duhring/ch11.htm)). Hegel's word for this is ***aufheben***, which means "both to cancel (or negate) and to preserve at the same time" ([SEP: Hegel's Dialectics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-dialectics/)). Development is therefore "not a straight line and not motion in a circle, but a **spiral**" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.10](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s10.html)).

Two traps sit here. The famous "**thesis–antithesis–synthesis**" formula is Fichte's language, which Hegel did not adopt for his own method; the SEP treats the triad as the traditional textbook reading ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-dialectics/)). And Stalin's 1938 summary of the dialectical method left out the negation of the negation entirely ([Stalin, 1938](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1938/09.htm)).

The table below runs all three laws through worked examples step by step. The water and barley cases are Engels' own, and the heredity–variability case comes from Russian Wikipedia's article on the opposites law; the last row is a set of everyday illustrations.

| Step | Quantity into quality: water (Engels) | Unity and struggle of opposites: heredity vs variability | Negation of the negation: barley (Engels) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting point | liquid water, one quality | a species, existing as the unity of **heredity** (which conserves traits) and **variability** (which produces new ones), each "as necessary as another" (Spirkin) | a grain of barley |
| What builds up or pulls apart | the temperature rises from 20 °C to 99 °C; it is still water, because the change stays within the **measure** (0–100 °C at normal pressure) | the two sides pull toward stability and toward change | the grain germinates |
| Turning point | the **nodal point** at 100 °C (or 0 °C): a **leap** | selection fixes useful variants | **first negation**: the plant appears and the grain "ceases to exist" |
| Result | steam, a new quality with a new measure of its own ("and vice versa") | a new form that carries a new contradiction | **negation of the negation**: grain again, "ten-, twenty- or thirtyfold", a return on a higher level |
| Question answered | **HOW** (mechanism) | **WHY** (source: the impulse is internal) | **WHERE** (direction: an ascending spiral) |
| Non-example | — | — | grinding the grain into flour, which makes the second negation impossible |
| Everyday illustration | the Uzbek proverb *"Tomchi-tomchi ko'l bo'lur"* ("drop by drop, a lake forms"); daily vocabulary work until you suddenly "speak", a gradual leap | what you already know vs a problem you cannot yet solve; solving it lifts you to a level with new problems | handwritten letters → phone calls (writing negated by voice) → texting and email (writing returns, instant and global) |

### Paired categories become easy once each pair has a concrete case

Spirkin defines **categories** as "extremely general, fundamental concepts reflecting the most essential, law-governed connections and relationships of reality". He adds that they are also "the forms and stable organising principles of the thought process" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.1](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02.html)). Dialectical materialism teaches them as correlated pairs, in which neither side exists without the other. Spirkin also ties **law** to essence: a law is "a type of relations between objects", namely "stable, repetitive, essential, necessary relations" ([Spirkin, ch. 2.8](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s08.html)).

The definitions below are Spirkin's. The worked examples are illustrations unless a source is named, and the sources are listed after the table.

| Pair | Definitions | How the sides relate | Worked example | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Singular – particular – universal** | singular: "an object taken in its distinctness from everything else"; particular: "the realised general"; universal: "**the singular in the many**" | the universal exists only in and through singulars; Lenin: in "Ivan is a man", "the individual is the universal" | Tashkent (singular) → the cities of Central Asia (particular) → city (universal) | the singular is not the particular: the particular is the middle term, a kind or group |
| **Essence – phenomenon** (and **semblance**) | essence: what a person "cannot lose without ceasing to be himself"; phenomenon: "a manifestation of essence"; semblance: essence shown one-sidedly, a mismatch between phenomenon and essence | knowledge moves from phenomenon to essence | a doctor reads fever and cough (phenomena) to find the infection (essence); the sun "rising" is a semblance of the Earth's rotation | a phenomenon is not a semblance; and unlike Kant's thing-in-itself, essence can be known |
| **Content – form** | content: the elements "in their qualitative determinacy, their interaction and functioning"; form: "the boundaries of the given content, its outward posture" | **content determines**, but form is active: it can "lag behind" or speed development up | a course's knowledge and skills (content) vs its lectures, seminars and online modules (form); pure dictation is a form that lags | content–form is not essence–phenomenon |
| **Cause – effect** (with **condition** and **occasion**) | causality: "a genetic connection of phenomena through which one thing (the cause) under certain conditions gives rise to ... something else (the effect)"; conditions "cannot in themselves give rise to the effect, but the cause is also powerless without them"; occasion: "the external push" | the cause is "active and primary" | a dormitory fire: a short circuit (cause), oxygen and flammable materials (conditions), switching on an overloaded heater (occasion). Spirkin's own case: a head cold that triggers an underlying illness (occasion) | the occasion is not the cause |
| **Necessity – chance** | necessity: "the impossibility of something being otherwise"; chance: "external, contingent, inessential" events, yet "a form of manifestation of necessity itself" | "The necessary carves a road for itself through an infinite number of accidents" | each coin toss is chance, but about 50% heads over thousands of tosses is a regularity. Engels: that "just that particular Corsican" became dictator was an accident, but "if a Napoleon had been lacking, another would have filled the place" | chance events still have causes; they are inessential to the process, not uncaused |
| **Possibility – reality** | real possibility: a "law-governed tendency in development"; formal possibility: lacks sufficient conditions, "very remote"; reality: possibility realised | possibility is "a unity of existence and non existence" | an Inha student becoming an engineer (real) vs winning a lottery jackpot (formal). Spirkin: space flight "was once only formal but has now become reality" | close to Aristotle's potentiality–actuality (acorn → oak), but not identical with it |

Borgius, 1894](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1894/letters/94_01_25.htm); potentiality from [SEP: Aristotle's Metaphysics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-metaphysics/).

Spirkin adds three more pairs that exam questions use ([Spirkin, ch. 2.7](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch02-s07.html)). A **system** is "an internally organised whole where elements are so intimately connected that they operate as one". An **element** is "the minimal unit performing a definite function in the whole". **Structure** is "the type of connection between the elements of a whole". Part and whole express how one complex object is a unity of other objects.

Two other category systems appear as distractors ([SEP: Categories](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/categories/)). The commonest trap is putting Kant's causality under modality; it belongs under **relation**.

|---|---|---|
| Number | 10 | 12 (4 headings × 3) |
| What they are | the highest kinds of being and of predication | a-priori concepts of the understanding, valid for all possible experience |
| How they were found | by asking which questions can be put to a thing | derived from the four forms of judgment |
| The list | substance (man, horse), quantity (four-foot), quality (white), relation (double, half), place (in the Lyceum), time (yesterday), posture (lying, sitting), state (has shoes on), action (cutting), passion (being cut) | **quantity**: unity, plurality, totality; **quality**: reality, negation, limitation; **relation**: substance and accident, cause and effect, community; **modality**: possibility, existence, necessity |

### "Metaphysics" also names the method dialectics opposes

In the Hegelian–Marxist vocabulary, "metaphysics" is not only Aristotle's discipline but also a **way of thinking**. Engels describes the metaphysician this way ([Engels, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, ch. II](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1880/soc-utop/ch02.htm)): "things and their mental reflexes, ideas, are isolated, are to be considered one after the other and apart from each other, are objects of investigation fixed, rigid, given once for all". Such a thinker reasons in "yea, yea; nay, nay" and "cannot see the woods for the trees". Engels traced the habit to Bacon and Locke, who carried the isolating method of natural science into philosophy. Dialectics instead grasps things "in their essential connection, concatenation, motion, origin and ending". Stalin's 1938 pamphlet turned the contrast into four features that textbooks still repeat ([Stalin, 1938](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1938/09.htm)).

| Feature | Dialectical method | Metaphysical method |
|---|---|---|
| Connection | "no phenomenon in nature can be understood if taken by itself, isolated from surrounding phenomena" | nature as "an accidental agglomeration of things" |
| Change | "continuous movement and change, of continuous renewal and development" | "rest and immobility, stagnation and immutability" |
| Development | small quantitative changes lead to qualitative ones, "rapidly and abruptly, taking the form of a leap" | "simple growth, where quantitative changes do not lead to qualitative changes" |
| Source | internal contradictions: "the struggle between these opposites ... constitutes the internal content of the process of development" | no internal contradiction; any change comes from outside |

In Spirkin's account, cognition works through three **forms of thought** ([Spirkin, ch. 4.5](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch04-s05.html)). A **concept** reflects the essential properties of objects. A **judgment** is a form of thought "in which something is asserted or denied about something". An **inference** reaches a new judgment from premises. Cognition moves from an **empirical level** to a **theoretical level**. It "begins with the empirical, with the observation and establishing of facts, their analysis and classification", then moves "to their generalisation, the making of hypotheses ... and finally, the construction of theories" ([same](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch04-s05.html)).

The table groups the general scientific methods by level. The definitions are Spirkin's unless another source is named. The grouping into levels reconstructs the usual textbook scheme, and the examples are illustrations.

| Method | Definition | Level | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | "an intentional, planned process of perception" | empirical | logging Tashkent's daily air-quality readings |
| **Experiment** | the object is "artificially reproduced or placed in certain conditions" | empirical | heating water at controlled pressures |
| **Comparison** | "the mother of knowledge": finding resemblance and difference | empirical (used at both levels) | comparing the yields of two cotton varieties |
| **Analysis / synthesis** | breaking an object into its parts / "the practical, mental integration of the elements" | both levels | taking an engine apart / putting it back together |
| **Abstraction** | singling out one attribute from its connections | mainly theoretical | studying a body's mass while ignoring its colour |
| **Generalisation** | moving from individual concepts to general ones | both levels | apple, pear → fruit |
| **Induction** | the conclusion is "merely probable"; arguments are strong or weak ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/deductive-inductive-arguments/)) | from empirical toward theoretical | red-winged blackbirds have returned every March, so they generally return in March ([OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-4-types-of-inferences)) |
| **Deduction** | the premises "logically entail" the conclusion; arguments are valid or invalid ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/deductive-inductive-arguments/)) | theoretical | "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is mortal" |
| **Abduction** (a Western term) | "inference to the best explanation" ([OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-4-types-of-inferences)) | forming hypotheses | the car will not start and the lights are dead, so the battery is probably flat |
| **Analogy** | applying what was learned about one object to a similar object | both levels | inferring a drug's effect in humans from its effect in similar mammals |
| **Modelling** | "the practical or theoretical **replacement** of the object of research by some natural or artificial analogue" | both levels | a wind-tunnel model; a computer climate model |
| **Idealisation** | concepts whose real prototypes exist only approximately | theoretical | "ideal gas", "material point" |
| **Hypothesis / theory** | "a supposition based on facts" / knowledge that, "unlike the hypothesis", is reliable | theoretical | Mendeleev predicted "eka-aluminium" (gallium) before it was found ([Engels](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1883/don/ch02.htm)) |
| **Historical / logical** | the concrete course of development / its "general line" | theoretical | a year-by-year history of money vs the necessary stages barter → general equivalent → money |
| **Ascent from the abstract to the concrete** | cognition "rolls forward from content to content", growing richer | theoretical | building a theory of an economy step by step from its simplest concept |

Three points decide several questions. First, the IEP rejects the classroom slogan that deduction runs "from general to particular" and induction "from particular to general". Some arguments contain only universal statements and others only particular ones, so the real difference is **entailment vs probability** ([IEP: Deductive and Inductive Arguments](https://iep.utm.edu/deductive-inductive-arguments/)). If a class quiz offers the slogan as the only fitting option, though, it is the intended answer. Second, the historical and logical methods work together. Engels called the logical method "nothing but the historical method, only stripped of the historical form and diverting chance occurrences" ([Engels, 1859](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1859/critique-pol-economy/appx2.htm)). Third, the empirical *level* of knowledge is not empiricism. Empiricism is a philosophical *doctrine* (Bacon, Locke) that "gave priority to experience and induction", against rationalism's priority on deduction ([Spirkin, ch. 4.5](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spirkin/works/dialectical-materialism/ch04-s05.html)).

### When sources disagree, the slide's value wins

Four rules settle most date conflicts. First, whatever your slides say is the answer on a class quiz. Second, if the slides are silent, take the traditional or textbook value: Ibn Sina born 980, Temur born 1336, Ulugh Beg's catalogue about 1,018 stars, Zoroaster "traditionally" in the 7th–6th century BCE. Third, take the modern-scholarship value only when the question asks for it ("modern scholars date ..."). Fourth, where the gap is small (al-Farabi 870 vs 878, al-Biruni's death in 1050 vs 1052), MCQ distractors usually differ by far more, so anchor on the century and the life events. Regional textbooks sometimes print exact years for al-Khwarizmi, al-Farabi, al-Biruni and al-Maturidi that none of the reference works confirm. Learn the reference ranges and let the slides settle the last digit.

## Recall practice spread over five days beats rereading

### What the evidence says, and how it maps onto five-question quizzes

The best-supported study advice comes from John Dunlosky's review of learning techniques. It rated **practice testing and distributed practice** as "the most effective of those we reviewed because they can help students regardless of age, they can enhance learning and comprehension of a large range of materials, and, most important, they can boost student achievement" ([Dunlosky, *American Educator*, Fall 2013](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)). It rated **interleaved practice** as promising. By contrast, rereading, highlighting, summarisation and imagery have "not fared so well when considered with an eye towards effectiveness".

Testing works in two ways. "When students correctly retrieve an answer from memory, the correct retrieval can have a direct effect on memory", and testing also shows which material still needs work. Two details matter for MCQ preparation. Students "will benefit most from tests that require recall from memory, and not from tests that merely ask them to recognize the correct answer". They should also keep testing "until they correctly recall each concept at least once from memory", and ideally get it right "on more than one occasion". Spacing means several short study blocks instead of "an all-night cram session". Each block should cover both newly taught material and material from earlier sessions ([same](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)).

This course's format turns those findings into a concrete method. Five questions in ten minutes gives two minutes per question, so speed is not what decides your score; telling look-alikes apart is. Turn every table row above into a two-way recall prompt (name → hook, then hook → name) and answer it before you look at any options.

Interleaving needs one caveat. Dunlosky notes that its benefits "do not extend to all disciplines": it did not help with learning French vocabulary. Pure fact lists, then, are best handled by spacing and testing. Interleaving pays off where you have to tell look-alikes apart: "massed practice robs students of the opportunity to practice identifying problems, whereas interleaved practice forces students to practice doing so" ([same](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)). That is exactly the two-Zenos, two-*Qānūns* problem. So drill each trap family as a set in one sitting: all three kalām schools, all three Sufi orders, all four Milesians, all three laws. Keep an error log, and re-test every miss the next day until you get it right twice.

In the exam room, read the stem for its keyword and its qualifier, such as "traditionally", "according to Engels" or "NOT". Predict the answer before reading the options. Then eliminate options from the wrong family or the wrong axis; "agnosticism", for example, is never the answer to a primacy question. When two options differ by only a few years, apply the date rules above. Change an answer only when you can name the specific fact that makes the other option right.

Two further points. First, a missed quiz is far more costly than a wrong answer. You can miss at most three quizzes all semester, so each absence uses a third of your safety margin. Sit every quiz, even underprepared. Second, if the midterm includes short written answers, a fixed structure covers the marking points:

| Question type | What to include, in order |
|---|---|
| A law of dialectics | 1. state the law; 2. define its key concepts; 3. give Engels' classic example; 4. give your own example; 5. say whether it answers *why*, *how* or *where* |
| A pair of categories | 1. define both sides; 2. say which side determines the other and how they pass into each other; 3. give an example; 4. name the confusion you are avoiding |

## Conclusion

The course looks like a survey of three thousand years, but its MCQs test a small, stable set of distinctions: one hook per name, one axis per "-ism", one question per law. That changes what efficient preparation looks like. The unit of study is not the single thinker but the **family**: the three kalām schools, the three Sufi orders, Ibn Sina's five books, the four Milesian principles, the three laws. Distractors are drawn from inside the family, and knowing it as a set is what lets you eliminate options under time pressure. The theory half compresses even further. Engels' short chapters, Lenin's two pages and Spirkin's definitions are enough to rebuild almost every definition and example the post-Soviet template uses, so one definition plus one worked example per concept covers it.

Two less obvious points follow. First, source disagreements are signals about what a quiz will reward, not noise to ignore. The course's framing favours traditional and regional values (980, 1336, 1,018, "Second Renaissance = Timurid"). Knowing both the textbook value and the reference value, and reading the question's qualifier, turns a trap into a free point. Second, the attendance rule changes the arithmetic of the weekly quizzes. A wrong answer costs one-fifth of a quiz; a missed quiz uses a third of the semester's safety margin. Showing up matters more than acing any single quiz. Finally, everything here comes from outside sources, without access to the course's slides or question bank. Where the slides use a different date, order of laws or list of forms, follow the slides.
