**The spine in one line:**
1. Myth → Vedas (c. 1500 BC) → the Greeks (Thales → Pythagoras → Heraclitus → Socrates → Plato → Aristotle → Epicurus).
2. Christianity (early 1st c.) → the caliphates (632 →) → the Golden Age (8th–13th c.): Farabi, Beruni, Ibn Sina, kalam, the Sufi orders.
3. Aquinas and Roger Bacon (13th c.) → the Timurids (1370 → 15th c.): Taftazoni, Jurjoniy, Ulugbek, Jomi, Navoi.
4. The Renaissance and Copernicus (14th–16th c.) → rationalism and empiricism (17th c.) → Marx & Engels (1840s–50s) → the Contemporary period (1945).

## 8. Short-answer / essay practice

Eight prompts, different from the essay lists in the notes files. Most cut across themes, as a midterm essay can.
- **Source rule:** every point in the outlines is a lecture fact (refs in brackets). Where an outline *links* facts that class never links explicitly, it says so: the facts are class facts, the link is your argument.
- **Method:** outline in about 5 minutes, write in about 15. Prompts 1–7 cover Themes 1–6; prompt 8 is Theme 7 (possible).

### 8.1 (T1, with links to T2–T6) The basic question of philosophy
**Prompt:** What is the basic question of philosophy? Explain how monism, dualism and pluralism answer it, and how sensualism, rationalism and irrationalism explain knowledge. Give course examples.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Definition (1–2 lines):** philosophy is "love of wisdom" (Greek *phileo* + *sophia*). It studies "the most common essential characteristics and fundamental principles" of existence (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), values (ethics), reason (logic), mind and language; each is a separate branch [T1-doc].
- **The basic question:** "what is primary: the spirit or matter, ideal or material?" The general understanding of being depends on the answer, because the material and the ideal are being's "ultimate characteristics" [T1-doc; T1-s6].
- **Monism** (Greek *monos*, "one, only"): the world has one beginning [T1-s7].
  - *Materialistic*: the ideal derives from the material; based on natural-science data (docx).
  - *Idealistic*: the material is due to the ideal; it must prove that spirit (consciousness, idea, God) created the world, which "cannot be positively solved within the framework of modern science".
- **Dualism** (Latin *dualis*): two equal principles, matter and consciousness. Descartes: the thinking (spirit) and the extended (matter) [T1-doc].
- **Pluralism** (Latin *pluralis*): several or many bases, e.g., earth, water, air, fire [T1-s7].
- **Course examples (your links):**
  - One material basis: Thales's water, Anaximenes's air [T2]. Atoms as "indivisible particles of matter" [T7].
  - One spiritual source: Farabi's Wujudu wājib, God as the first reason [T3]. The monotheistic "sole creator" [T7].
  - Many bases: Lao-Tzu's "Tsi" (five elements) [T2]; Jurjoniy's four elements [T4].
  - A *different* dualism: the Gnostics' good Spirit vs evil matter [T5].
- **Knowledge:**
  - **Sensualism** (Latin *sensus*): the senses. **Rationalism** (Latin *ratio*): the mind. **Irrationalism**: reason denied or limited.
  - The docx calls them "doctrines", the slide "methods of epistemology" [T1].
  - Later: continental rationalism (Descartes, Pascal, Leibniz: reason alone) vs British empiricism (F. Bacon, Locke: experience). Both put the human at the center [T6].
- **Method:** *methodos* = "the path of knowledge". It originates in people's practical activity and reflects the laws of objective reality [T1].

### 8.2 (T2) East and West in antiquity
**Prompt:** Compare ancient Eastern and ancient Greek philosophy: their common origin, their answers to "what is the world based on?", and their views on change, the human being and the state.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Common origin:** mythology, "the first form of social consciousness" [T2].
  - The term is Middle French, "exposition of myths", from the 15th c.
  - Mythology explains the world by the action of gods and heroes.
  - It first asked how the world arose and develops, and what existence and death are.
- **The East:**
  - **Egypt and Babylon** (Babylon c. 2300 BC; independent in the 19th c. BC): belief in gods *plus* secular knowledge, "distinctive for all the civilizations in ancient times". Works: "The Dialogue between Master and his Slave on the Essence of Life", "The song of Arfist". Slide: polytheism, magic, divinity of the ruler.
  - **India:** the Vedas (c. 1500 BC; four samhites; the words of Brahma) and the Upanishads ("mysterious knowledge"). **Astika** (Vedanta, yoga, vaysheshika, mimansa) accept the Vedas; **nastika** (Charvaka-Lokayata, materialist; Buddhism; Jainism) do not.
  - **China:** Confucius (heaven rules; the will of heaven = destiny; "Li" = the essence of order). Lao-Tzu (the Dao's law; "Tsi", five elements; In and Yan).
- **Greece:**
  - Span: 8th c. BC – 4th c. CE (docx) / 6th c. BC – 3rd c. AD (slide).
  - Three stages: **Pre-Socratic** (the first principle: water, apeiron, air, fire, numbers), **Classical** (Socrates, Plato), **Hellenistic** (after Aristotle; Epicurus).
- **Change:** Daoism: everything moves from one state to another; In and Yan drive the universe. Heraclitus: everything moves, "eternal action is eternal change", transition into opposites (seawater: bad for people, good for fish).
- **The human being:** Daoism: side with good and love nature. Socrates: "not physics, but ethics"; the soul is man's essence; the body is its instrument.
- **The state:**
  - Confucius: the order is sent by the ruler of heaven.
  - Pythagoras: aristocracy; democracy violates order.
  - Plato: four forms (theocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny); the ideal state rests on justice; theory of ideas.
  - Aristotle: "The State", "The Politics".
  - Epicurus: mutual understanding and social agreement.
- **Materialist strands on both sides (your link):** Charvaka-Lokayata and atomism (Democritus → Epicurus).

### 8.3 (T3, with T4/T7 echoes) Farabi, Beruni and Ibn Sina: the Greek thread
**Prompt:** Explain the contributions of Farabi, Beruni and Ibn Sina, and show how they carried Greek, especially Aristotle's, philosophy into Central Asian thought.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Context:**
  - The Islamic Golden Age (8th–13th c.) began under Harun al-Rashid (786–809), whose House of Wisdom in Baghdad translated the world's classical works into Arabic.
  - Early Islamic philosophy ran from the early 9th to the late 12th c.
  - Its achievements influenced modern philosophy and science and the European Renaissance [T3].
- **Farabi (873–950):**
  - "The Second Teacher" after Aristotle; 160+ works; one of the first to classify the sciences.
  - Works: "The Great Book of Music", "The Virtuous City", "On the Introduction of Knowledge", "Classification of Rhythms".
  - **Wujudu wājib** (God, the first reason) vs **wujudi mumkin** (all material and spiritual beings); "no result without reason, as well there is no reason without result".
  - Slide: invited to Baghdad; philosophy is "the only route to true knowledge"; a Platonic utopia in *al-Madina al-fadila* [T3-doc; T3-s14].
- **Beruni (973–1048; docx only):**
  - Studied all the sciences; 150+ works, 28 survive; the universe was created by Allah.
  - Cognition starts with the sense organs, which support mental cognition; the truth of knowledge is checked by observation and experimentation; knowledge serves people's needs.
- **Ibn Sina (980–1037):**
  - Born in Afshona near Bukhara, died in Hamadon; "Avicenna"; the Samanid court.
  - 280+ works (docx; slide 200+): "The Book of Healing", "The Canon of Medicine"…
  - Followed Farabi on the universe.
  - **Three forms of motion:** accidental, forced, natural; "motion is bound by space and time". Slide: the soul is distinct from the body.
- **The Greek thread (class statements):** Farabi is the "Second Teacher" after Aristotle; his utopia is Platonic; Ibn Sina was an "adherent of Aristotle"; al-Maturidi's thought went together with "Aristotle's and his followers'" philosophy (slide).
- **Echoes:** Ulugbek was influenced by "Aristotle, Ptolemy, Muhammad Khorezmi, al-Farghani, Farobi, Ibn Sino, Beruni" [T4]. Jurjoniy wrote commentaries on Ibn Sinā [T4]. The medieval-ontology slide lists "the legacy of Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd" [T7].

### 8.4 (T3 → T4) The Sufi orders and their ethic of work, knowledge and the inner life
**Prompt:** Compare the three Sufi orders founded by Central Asian scholars. What did they teach about work, knowledge and the inner life, and how did this tradition continue in the Timurid period?

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Sufism (Tasawwuf):** "Islamic mysticism"; its practitioners are Sufis; its orders are the turuq; a main issue is **dhikr**, the remembrance of God [T3].
- **Yassawiya:** Khoja Ahmad Yassawi (born Sayram 1093, died Turkestan 1166).
  - Basics: Tawhid; absolute dependence on the Sunnah and Sharia (docx; slide: the Qur'an and the Sunnah); based on Sharia; khilva and remembrance.
  - Values: acquiring knowledge, generosity, struggle against the nafs, **halal labor**.
  - **Vocal** dhikr; "Yassawi hikmatlari".
  - Man's true origin is the universe, to which he must be brought back.
- **Kubrawiya:** Najmiddin Kubra (1145–1221, born in Khiva).
  - Analysis of the **visionary experience**.
  - Four ways: face God by one's own will · abstain from all tastes · gain God's satisfaction · love the truth.
  - Popular only after his death; spread outside Central Asia; overshadowed by the Naqshbandiya by the 15th/16th c.; Kubra died during the Mongol invasion.
- **Naqshbandiya:** Bahauddin Naqshband (1318–1389, near Bukhara).
  - **"Dil ba yoru dast ba kor"**: "Heart with God, and hands at work"; man "should not forget the world".
  - Dhikr **orally and mentally**.
- **Comparison (your synthesis):**
  - Work: Yassawi's halal labor and Naqshband's "hands at work".
  - Knowledge: Yassawi's acquisition of knowledge.
  - Inner life: dhikr (vocal vs oral and mental), Kubra's visionary experience, the struggle against the nafs.
- **Continuation in the Timurid period [T4]:**
  - **Jomi** followed the Naqshbandiya and developed its theory and practice. His Sufi must be honest and pure, **have a profession**, be reasonable and noble, and overcome his desires. God is a "violent lover", and life's aim is the meeting with God. He was influenced by Ibn al-Arabi.
  - **Navoi:** love is the means of moral refinement and of approaching God.
  - **Koshifiy:** good manners, the acquisition of science and a profession; justice as equality.

### 8.5 (T4) The Timurid "highest peak": Samarkand and Herat
**Prompt:** Why is the Timurid era called "the highest peak of the civilization and culture of the peoples of Central Asia"? Build the answer around two circles, Samarkand (logic and astronomy) and Herat (literature and ethics), and show the links between the scholars.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Context:**
  - Amir Temur became ruler of Central Asia in **1370** (docx: after the battle with Amir Husayn).
  - Movarounnahr and Khurasan progressed in all spheres; the epoch was unique "not only within the region, but also on a global scale".
  - The rulers **sponsored science and culture**. In the 14th–15th c., logic, natural sciences (especially astronomy), philosophy and ethics developed [T4].
- **Samarkand circle:**
  - **Taftazoni** (1322–1390): came to Samarkand at Temur's invitation; 40+ works.
    - Cause and effect exist in nature.
    - Three stages of cognition: senses and objects → acceptance by the human spirit → mental knowledge.
    - "Imagination and approval are forms of logic".
    - Developed Ilm al-Kalom and influenced Ulughbek's academic school.
  - **Jurjoniy** (taught from 1387; 50+ works incl. "Al-Ta'rifāt"; commentaries on Ibn Sinā, Chaghminiy, Tusi).
    - Four elements.
    - Syllogism, the basis of theoretical knowledge; induction, not always true; analogy.
    - Logic tied to law and language, so madrasas taught logic with law and linguistics.
  - **Ulugbek** (1394–1449): Temur's grandson.
    - Instructors: Qozizoda Rumi, Ghiyosiddin Jamshid. Observatory 1424–1429.
    - "Zij-i Jadid-i Kuragāniy" (2 chapters, preface; 1118 stars docx / 1018 slide).
    - Methods: observation, experimentation, proof, comparison, induction, deduction.
    - Copernicus and Galileo developed his ideas from the 16th c.
  - **Ali Qushchi** (1403–1474): Ulugbek's student; explained eclipses ("The Book on Astronomy"); motion from simple to complex.
- **Herat circle** (from the 2nd half of the 15th c.):
  - **Jomi** (1414–1492): "Haft Avrang" (7 eposes); humanism; a perfect (docx) / virtuous (slide) society of equality, liberty and conscience.
  - **Navoi:**
    - Founder of Uzbek literature; Minister of Husayn Boyqaro in 1472; built schools, madrasas, mosques, bridges, hospitals.
    - Works: "Hamsa" (5 eposes); "Khazoyinul maoniy" (~45,000 misras); "Muhokamatul-lug'atayn" (Chagatai superior to Persian).
    - Ideas expressed metaphorically and symbolically.
  - **Koshifiy** (1440–1505): came to Herat at Jomi's invitation; "Ahloq-i Muhsiniy"; wise state management; justice = the sense of equality.
- **Links:**
  - Temur → Taftazoni → Ulugbek's school → Ali Qushchi.
  - Jomi → Navoi (his teacher, docx) and Jomi → Koshifiy (invitation); the three were friends.
- **Conclusion:** sponsorship by the rulers plus achievements in logic, astronomy, literature and ethics with influence beyond the region (Copernicus, Galileo) explain the label.

### 8.6 (T5, with T3) Religion and philosophy in the medieval West, and the Islamic parallel
**Prompt:** How did religion shape philosophy in the medieval Christian West? Describe its schools and traditions, compare Thomas Aquinas with Roger Bacon, and relate scholasticism to Islamic kalam.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Period:** 5th–15th c. Religion affected every aspect of life: Christianity became a state religion and ideology, and the **Inquisition** (founded in the early 13th c. against heresy in France) became a priority [T5].
- **Christianity:**
  - *Christos*, "the anointed one"; Jesus Christ, the God-man; arose in the early 1st c.; dominant in the Roman Empire by the 4th c.
  - Catholicism, Eastern Orthodox, Protestantism; the Bible, especially the New Testament.
- **Traditions:** the docx lists Gnosticism, Apologetics, Patristics, Realism, Nominalism; the slide lists Scholasticism in place of the last two. Both describe Scholasticism.
  - **Gnosticism** (1st–5th c., "the knower"): dualism, matter is evil, so God cannot create the material world; condemned as heresy; term from Henry More (17th c.).
  - **Apologetics** (1st–3rd c., "protect"): defended Christianity; denial and confirmation; Saint Justin's "First Apology", "Second Apology", "On the Soul".
  - **Patristics** (2nd–8th c., "father"): the Church Fathers shaped dogmatics, hierarchy and worship, and the canon.
  - **Scholasticism** ("school, scientist"): theological-dogmatic premises + a rationalistic methodology + formal-logical problems; the Court school of Great Charles (742–814).
- **Aquinas vs Roger Bacon:**
  - **Aquinas** (1225–1274): moderate realism; the greatest scholastic; united Aristotle with theology; "Summa Theologiae" (3,000 articles, 600 issues); the world as a hierarchical ladder.
  - **Roger Bacon** (1214–1292, "Doctor Mirabilis", Oxford): the first to speak against scholasticism.
    - "Opus majus" (1268): abstract dialectics is useless; study nature by observation and mathematics, the most reliable science.
    - Spiritual experience anticipated intuition in science.
- **The Islamic parallel [T3, T5]:**
  - Kalam arose from the 8th–11th c. debates "within the framework of Muslim scholasticism". The Mutazilites began it with "a principled rational position"; al-Maturidi (Samarkand), al-Ashari (Iraq) and al-Tahawi (Egypt) "tried to reconcile contradictory ideas in the rational direction of the kalam".
  - Asharism (X–XI c.) founded orthodox kalam.
  - Theme 5 names Islamic Ilm al-Kalām and Jewish Kalam as the **forerunners (and later companions)** of Christian scholasticism.
- **Conclusion (your synthesis):** in both worlds, philosophy worked inside religion, combining faith with reasoning, and rational positions met critics. In the East, al-Maturidi criticized the Mu'tazilite doctrine and Asharism set orthodox kalam against "the rationalistic kalama of the mutazilites". In the West, Roger Bacon "first spoke against scholasticism".

### 8.7 (T6, with T4/T5 links) From the Middle Ages to the Modern period
**Prompt:** Explain the transition to the Modern period: the periodization, the Renaissance and its view of man, the new picture of the universe (Copernicus, Bruno), the rationalism–empiricism debate, and the two later schools named in class.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Periodization:** the Modern period follows the Middle Ages and precedes the Contemporary era [T6-s3].
  - **Early modern** (early 16th – mid-18th c.): Renaissance, Age of Discovery, Protestant Reformation.
  - **Late modern** (mid-18th c. – 1945): Age of Enlightenment.
  - **Contemporary**: from 1945; some count it as a third stage.
- **"Repositioning of the world" (docx):**
  - World trade and commercialization.
  - Rationalized politics: bureaucrats enforced central edicts; the 17th-c. French monarchy created a language institution, prisons, army uniforms, logistics and pensions.
  - Culture: printing, the Reformation leading to religious toleration, the scientific revolution.
- **Renaissance:**
  - Western and Central Europe; secularism, a humanistic world view, a "rebirth" of antiquity; a bridge from the Middle Ages; Italy 14th–16th c., elsewhere 15th–16th c.
  - **Anthropocentric** (Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things"). Man is the creator of himself, needs no divine grace for salvation, and is an unlimited lord over nature.
- **The universe:**
  - **Ptolemy's** geocentrism (Earth at the center, complex orbits), about 1500 years before Copernicus.
  - **Copernicus** (1473–1543, Polish): heliocentrism after studying the planets' motion; "On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres" (1543); it "scientifically proved" the weakness of Christian doctrine on the structure of the world.
  - **Bruno** (1548–1600): supported heliocentrism; true philosophy rests on scientific experience; the universe is unique, material, infinite and eternal; stars are suns of other planets. Arrested by the Inquisition in 1592 and burned in Rome in 1600.
  - Link [T4]: Ulugbek's astronomy was developed by Copernicus and Galileo from the 16th c.
- **Knowledge:**
  - 17th-c. continental **rationalism** (Descartes, Pascal, Leibniz, also mathematicians): knowledge from reason alone.
  - British **empiricism** (F. Bacon, Locke; 17th–18th c.): knowledge from experience.
  - Common ground: both put the human at the center.
- **Later schools:**
  - **Neo-Thomism** rebuilt Aquinas: harmony of science and belief; belief applies where there is no knowledge, but through logical perception; "philosophy is to serve religion".
  - **Hermeneutics** ("art of interpretation"; a 20th-c. tradition; first generalizing work by Augustine, 354–430): understanding, explanation, analysis; society rests on dialogue.
- **Conclusion (your synthesis):** from God at the top of Aquinas's ladder [T5] to man at the center (Renaissance, rationalism, empiricism).

### 8.8 (T7, possible) Ontology: being and non-being through history
**Prompt:** What is ontology? Explain the two senses of "being" and trace how ideas of being and non-being changed: Parmenides, Heraclitus, the atomists, the shift to monotheism, Marx and Engels. End with the areas of existence.

**Model-answer outline:**
- **Ontology:** "the doctrine of existence and being". It studies being's fundamental principles, most common entities and categories, structure and patterns, together with the theory of knowledge and logic. Jacob Lorhard first used the term, in "Ogdoas Scholastica" (1606) [T7]. Theme 1 lists it as the branch about existence.
- **Being:**
  - "One of the most important categories of philosophy"; from the verb "to be".
  - **Narrow sense:** the objective world independent of consciousness. **Wide sense:** everything that exists, including consciousness, ideas, feelings and fantasies.
  - Being as objective reality is called **matter**. **Non-being** is everything that is not.
- **Parmenides** (Eleatic school, "On Nature"): only being exists, and thinking is being. Being is not generated and not destroyed; it is only the present, motionless, homogeneous, perfect and limited. "There is a Being and there is no non-existence."
- **Heraclitus** ("in contrast to Parmenides"): the cosmos, made by no god or person, is an eternal living fire in perpetual motion; motion comes from the mutual transition of opposites; being and non-existence are inseparable.
- **Atomists:**
  - Leucippus and his disciple Democritus (c. 460 – c. 370 BC): atoms are indivisible particles of matter.
  - Epicurus and Lucretius developed atomism, which revives in modern times.
  - From the end of the 5th c. BC, idealistic systems arose with a different doctrine of being.
- **The 3rd century:** a transitional period; polytheistic gods (parts of nature) gave way to one creator God, "a revolution in the worldview"; as the state religion of the Roman Empire, Christianity led to the exclusion of other views.
- **Medieval (slide):** a scholastic approach; reconsideration of Aristotelianism; Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd; Aquinas ("The world has beginning, but God could have created in a such a way that it existed eternally"); heliocentrism.
- **19th – early 20th c.:**
  - Society was long viewed idealistically. In the 1840s–1850s **Marx and Engels** laid the foundations of dialectical materialism and the materialist understanding of history, introducing "**social (public) being**": society's internal foundation, not identical with its natural basis.
  - Forms of being: real (nature, society, man) vs imaginary (absolute essences, God) (docx).
  - At the end of the 19th – early 20th c., gnoseology became dominant. Slide: the Big Bang theory, 1920s.
- **Areas of existence:** nature, society, consciousness. Natural being = eternal nature + "second nature" (culture, made by human labor), whose forms are human, spiritual and social-being existence.
