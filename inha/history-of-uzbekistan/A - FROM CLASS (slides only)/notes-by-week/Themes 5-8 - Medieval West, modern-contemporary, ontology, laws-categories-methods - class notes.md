## 1. Big picture

- ⭐ **Middle Ages in Europe (5th–15th c.):** religion shaped every part of life. Christianity became the state religion and ideology, and the Inquisition became a priority. Philosophy developed *inside* Christianity through four traditions, in the order taught: **Gnosticism, Apologetics, Patristics, Scholasticism** [T5-s3, s5].
- ⭐ **Two medieval thinkers, opposite approaches:**
  - **Thomas Aquinas** joined Aristotle's philosophy with theology. He stood for moderate realism, and his main work is the *Summa Theologiae* [T5-s12–13].
  - **Roger Bacon** attacked scholasticism and argued for observation, mathematics and experience. His main work is the *Opus majus* [T5-s14–15].
- ⭐ **The Modern period follows the Middle Ages:**
  - early modern, early 16th to mid-18th c. (Renaissance, Age of Discovery, Reformation);
  - late modern, mid-18th c. to 1945 (Enlightenment);
  - **Contemporary period, from 1945** [T6-s3].
- **Renaissance:** secular and humanistic, with an **anthropocentric** revival of antiquity (Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things"). Man becomes the creator of himself [T6-s4–6].
- **Science against Church doctrine:** Copernicus's **heliocentrism** replaced Ptolemy's geocentrism. Bruno taught an infinite, eternal, material universe and was burned in Rome in 1600 [T6-s7–10].
- **Where knowledge comes from (17th–18th c.):**
  - continental **rationalism** (Descartes, Pascal, Leibniz): knowledge comes from reason;
  - British **empiricism** (Francis Bacon, Locke): knowledge comes from experience;
  - both put the human being at the center [T6-s11–12].
- **Later schools (second half of the 19th c. and the 20th c.):**
  - **Neo-Thomism:** science and faith are in harmony, and philosophy serves religion;
  - **Hermeneutics:** the art of interpretation, built on dialogue; Augustine wrote the first generalizing work [T6-s13–16].
- ⭐ **Ontology is the doctrine of being.** Jacob Lorhard first used the term in 1606. Ideas about being and non-being changed over time:
  - Parmenides: being is, and it is motionless;
  - Heraclitus: motion and opposites;
  - the atomists;
  - Christian monotheism;
  - Marx and Engels: "social being";
  - gnoseology became dominant.

  There are three areas of existence: nature, society and consciousness. Culture is "second nature" [T7].
- **Theme 8 (likely post-midterm) covers philosophy's three tools:**
  - **laws:** stable, recurring relationships;
  - **categories:** the most general concepts (Aristotle's 10, Kant's 4 types, Hegel's 3 groups);
  - **methods:** dialectics vs metaphysics, plus sophism [T8].

#### 5.1 Characteristic features of the period [T5-doc; T5-s3]
- ⭐ **The medieval period in Europe runs from the 5th century AD to the 15th century.**
- Its main feature is that religion affected **every aspect of human life**. The lecture gives two signs of this:
  1. Christianity was formed and developed as the **state religion and ideology**;
  2. the **Inquisition** became a priority.
- ⭐ **Inquisition:** "the institution of the Roman Catholic Church for the search and punishment of heretics and other enemies of the Catholic Church."
  - It was set up in the **early 13th century** to fight heresy **in France**.
  - Its roots lie in earlier medieval church actions against heretics.
- ⚠️ The doc has only "2. The Inquisition…" because the "1." was lost in conversion. The slide says "Firstly… Secondly…".

#### 5.2 The basics of Christianity [T5-doc; T5-s4]
- ⭐ **Christianity** comes from Greek *Christos*, literally **"the anointed one."** It is a world religion named after its founder, **Jesus Christ**. The doc adds "along with Buddhism and Islam" [doc-only].
- **Jesus Christ** is described as the **God-man**, who:
  - came down from heaven to earth;
  - accepted suffering and death to save people;
  - then rose again and ascended into heaven.
- ⭐ **Timeline:** Christianity arose in the **early 1st century** and became the **dominant religion of the Roman Empire in the 4th century**.
- ⭐ **Three main branches:** Catholicism, Eastern Orthodox, Protestantism.
- **What unites all Christian faiths and sects:** faith in Jesus Christ as God-man and **savior of the world**.
- **Main source:** the **Bible**, especially the **New Testament**.
- ⭐ **Indulgence** [doc-only; last paragraph of T5-doc, spelled "INDULGENCY"]:
  - from Latin *indulgentia* = "mercy";
  - in the Catholic Church, the full or partial **remission of sins**, and also the **certificate** for it;
  - the widespread **trade in indulgences (12th–13th centuries)** was a way of enriching the clergy.

#### 5.3 Western philosophical schools and traditions of the medieval period [T5-doc; T5-s5–s11]
- Christianity shaped medieval European philosophy, and several schools and traditions arose from it.
- ⚠️ **The doc and the slide list different schools:**
  - slide 5: **Gnosticism, Apologetics, Patristics, Scholasticism**;
  - doc: Gnosticism, Apologetics, Patristics, **Realism, Nominalism**.
  - Both texts then describe Scholasticism, not realism or nominalism. Realism comes up again only in Aquinas's "moderate realism", and nominalism is never explained. 🔎 Look up in Track B: realism vs nominalism.

**(a) Gnosticism** [T5-s6–s7]
- From Greek *gnostikos* = **"the knower."**
- ⭐ A **religious dualistic doctrine of late antiquity (1st–5th centuries)**. It mixed parts of Christian doctrine, popular Greek philosophy and Eastern religions.
- ⭐ The word does not appear in ancient sources. **Henry More first used it in the 17th century.**
- Gnostics claimed "true" knowledge of God and of the ultimate secrets of the universe.
- **Dualism** is at its core:
  - the **Supreme Being / Supreme Spirit** is an independent source of **light and good** and of spiritual life;
  - **matter** is an independent source of material life and **evil**, and is *always* a form of evil;
  - ⭐ so **God can never create the material world.**
- Christian clergy condemned the Gnostics as **heretics**.
- **Manichaeism** is linked to the rise of Gnosticism [T5-s7]. Details found only in the doc:
  - a religion born in the **3rd century** in the area **between Mesopotamia and Persia**, under the **Sassanid dynasty**;
  - its founder, **Mani**, saw himself as the creator (or messenger) of a universal religion combining **Zoroastrianism, Christianity and Buddhism**;
  - Mani claimed to be the "declared Spirit of Jesus Christ".

**(b) Apologetics** [T5-s8–s9]
- From Greek *apologeomai* = **"protect"**. The doc glosses apology as "defense, protection".
- ⭐ The collective name for **early Christian writer-philosophers who defended Christianity** in writing against its critics: non-Christians, pagan rulers and scientists.
- ⭐ Formed in the **1st–3rd centuries**.
- The apologists argued that Christian moral principles were superior and that Christianity could become a **state religion**.
- Their works follow two lines:
  1. **denial or refusal:** rejecting the polytheists' charges against Christianity ("unreasonable actions and luxury");
  2. **confirmation:** affirming the **purity** of Christianity.
- ⭐ **Key representative: Saint Justin (Martyr).** His works are ***First Apology*, *Second Apology* and *On the Soul***.

**(c) Patristics (patrology)** [T5-s10]
- From Greek *pater (patros)* = **"father."**
- ⭐ The philosophical-theological tradition of the **2nd–8th centuries**, tied to the early Christian authors known as the **Church Fathers**.
- **Church Fathers** is an honorary title, used **from the end of the 4th century**, for leading church figures and writers of the past. Their authority carried special weight in:
  - shaping **dogmatics**;
  - the **hierarchical organization and worship** of the Church;
  - compiling the **canon**, the list of the Holy Books of the Bible. The doc adds that this meant separating inspired books from the **apocrypha**.
- They set out the principles of Christian theology and philosophy, and their teachings became the basis of the Christian religious outlook.
- Patristics contributed greatly to the **ethics and aesthetics** of late-antique and medieval society.
  - ⚠️ Both texts say "Patricia made an enormous contribution", a typo for Patristics.
- Theme 5 names no individual Church Father; Augustine appears only in Theme 6, under hermeneutics. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the main Church Fathers.

**(d) Scholasticism** [T5-s11]
- From Greek *scholastikos* = **"school, scientist."**
- ⭐ **Definition:** a **type of religious philosophy** that combines **theological-dogmatic premises** with a **rationalistic methodology** and an interest in **formal-logical problems**.
- It reached its greatest development in **Western Europe in the Middle Ages**.
- **Who were called "scholastics":**
  - teachers in the **Palace of Great Charles (742–814)**, i.e., the **Court school**;
  - also medieval scholars who used philosophy to study religion.
  - 🔎 Look up in Track B: the usual English name of "Great Charles" (742–814).
- ⭐ **Forerunners (and later companions) of Christian scholasticism: Islamic *Ilm al-Kalām* and Jewish philosophy, or Kalam.** The slide words it as "Ancestors… Islamic Ilm al-Kalām and Jewish Kalam".
  - Link: Theme 3 calls *ʻIlm al-kalam* a discipline "within the framework of Muslim scholasticism" [T3-doc].

#### 5.4 Scholars on theology and philosophy

**Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)** [T5-s12–s13]
- ⭐ A major representative of **moderate realism** and "the **greatest figure in the scholasticism**".
- ⭐ He tried to **unite Aristotle's philosophy with theology**. In his view, Aristotle's natural philosophy and logic can reach the truth about **the existence of God**.
- ⭐ **Main work: *Summa Theologiae*.** The slides and the cover image call it *Summa Theologica*.
  - It has **3,000 articles covering 600 issues** and is a systematic explanation of theology.
  - **Part 1** covers the **existence of God**; **Part 2** covers **"Moving Towards God"**.
- He saw the world as a **hierarchical ladder**:
  - at the bottom is the **Earth**, with the **4 elements** that make up all bodies;
  - the **human soul** sits between God and the material world;
  - at the top of secular society stands the **pope**, ruler of the divine state;
  - the further from the Earth, the more divine; an **angelic world** lies above the Earth.
- **Elsewhere in the course:**
  - Theme 7 quotes him: "The world has beginning, but God could have created in a such a way that it existed eternally" [T7-s10];
  - **Neo-Thomism** rebuilt his teaching in modern times [T6-s13].

**Roger Bacon (1214–1292)** [T5-s14–s15]
- ⚠️ **Dates:** the text says **1214–1292**, but the portrait caption on slide 14 reads **(1214–1294)** [T5-s14 img]. Use the text's 1214–1292 unless the professor says otherwise.
- ⭐ Known as the **"Amazing Doctor" (Latin *Doctor Mirabilis*)**.
  - English philosopher and naturalist, and professor of theology at **Oxford**.
  - He worked in mathematics, chemistry and physics.
- He created no new teaching, but he **criticized the methods and theories of his time**:
  - he was the first to speak out against **scholasticism**;
  - he sharply criticized the leading authorities of the day (**Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas**, etc.).
- ⭐ ***Opus majus* (1268)** argues that:
  - abstract dialectics is **useless**;
  - nature must be studied **through observation**;
  - nature must be subordinated to the **laws of mathematical calculation**.
- ⭐ **On mathematics:**
  - it is the only fully reliable and unquestionable science;
  - it can check the data of all other sciences;
  - it is the **easiest** science and **open to everyone**.
- **Experience.** He distinguished "two types":
  1. real **life experience**, gained only through living;
  2. **evidence from the external senses**, which deals only with material objects.
  - He also spoke of **spiritual experience**, open only to "the chosen" through a **mystical state or inner insight**. This anticipated later ideas of **heuristic insight** and the **role of intuition in science**.
  - ⚠️ The text says "two types" and then adds spiritual experience. If asked how many types, the class wording is **two**. 🔎 Look up in Track B: how Bacon's kinds of experience are usually classified.
- ⭐ **MCQ trap:** **Roger Bacon** (13th c., Theme 5, *Doctor Mirabilis*) is not **Francis Bacon** (empiricist, Theme 6).
- **Albert the Great** is mentioned only as one of the authorities Bacon attacked. No dates or ideas are given.

#### 6.1 Periodization [T6-doc; T6-s3]
- The **Modern period** is the part of world history that comes right **after the Middle Ages** and **before the Contemporary era**. It has two stages:

| Stage | Dates (slide 3) | Events listed |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Early modern** | **early 16th – mid-18th c.** | European Renaissance · Age of Discovery · Protestant Reformation |
| ⭐ **Late modern** | **mid-18th c. – 1945** | Age of Enlightenment [slide-only] |
| ⭐ **Contemporary** | **from 1945** to the present | events relevant to the present time |

- Some scholars treat the Contemporary period as a **third stage of the Modern era**.
- ⚠️ The doc gives only starting points: the early modern period "begins in early 16 century", and the late modern period "starts from the mid-18th century and lasts until Contemporary era". The date ranges and the "Age of Enlightenment" come from the slide.

#### 6.2 "Repositioning of the world": how the West changed [doc-only; T6-doc]
- The lecture calls this a **fundamental change in world history**: the West had been a backward society but changed a great deal. It names three big changes:
  1. **World trade.** Commercialization reached a new level: Europeans produced for the market, and trade kept growing.
  2. **Rationalized politics.**
     - Until the 16th c. European politics was still feudal.
     - By the 17th c. states were taking on new kinds of activity and showed a "new sense of rationalism".
     - Central governments sent more and more bureaucrats to the provinces to make sure central edicts were enforced.
     - Example: in the 17th c. the **French monarchy** set up an institution to **monitor the French language**, built prisons (criminal justice), and gave armies uniforms, military logistics and military pensions.
  3. **Culture:**
     - printing and newspapers;
     - the **Protestant Reformation, which led to religious toleration**;
     - the **scientific revolution**.

#### 6.3 European Renaissance and its legacy [T6-doc; T6-s4–s6]
- The **Renaissance** (French *Renaissance*; Italian *Rinascimento*, per the doc) was an era of cultural and ideological development in **Western and Central Europe**.
- ⭐ **Distinctive features:**
  - **secularism** (its "secular character");
  - a **humanistic world view**;
  - an appeal to the **ancient cultural heritage**, an attempt to re-establish it: a "revival" or "rebirth", which gives the era its name.
- It is seen as a **transition, or "bridge"**, from the Middle Ages to the Modern era.
- ⭐ **Its dates are hard to fix:** **14th–16th c. in Italy**, **15th–16th c. in other countries**.
- ⭐ **Renaissance philosophical thinking was anthropocentric.**
  - Its intellectual basis was **humanism** together with the rediscovery of classical Greek philosophy, such as **Protagoras**: ⭐ **"Man is the measure of all things."**
  - This outlook showed up in art, architecture, politics, science and literature.
- **A new self-awareness.** The individual gained much more independence, and with it:
  - **pride and self-assertion**, and awareness of one's own **strength and talent**;
  - man becomes **the creator of himself**, and of his own life and destiny;
  - man **no longer needs divine grace for his salvation**;
  - man is the **unlimited lord over nature**.

#### 6.4 Western scholars on astronomy and philosophy

**Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543)** [T6-s7–s8]
- A **Polish** scientist, **born in Poland**, and one of the greatest thinkers of the Renaissance. He contributed most to philosophy and science; the slide says "especially astronomy".
- ⭐ **Main work: *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*.** He wrote it just before his death in **1543**, and it was a major event in the history of science.
- ⭐ **Heliocentrism** puts the Sun at the center of the universe. It replaced the generally accepted **geocentric system**: the Earth at the center, with a complex system of orbits around it, proposed by **Ptolemy about 1500 years earlier**.
- He reached the heliocentric theory by **studying the motion of the planets**. The lecture calls this a major achievement of natural science.
- ⭐ **Why it mattered, as taught:** it "scientifically proved" that **Christian doctrine on the structure of the world** was inconsistent and weak.
- Link: Theme 4 says Ulughbek's ideas on astronomy were developed in Europe from the 16th c. by Copernicus, Galileo and others [T4-doc].

**Giordano Bruno (1548–1600)** [T6-s9–s10] (the doc spells it "Jordano")
- Born **near Naples, Italy**.
- **His life:**
  - he was accused of **heresy** for his advanced ideas and expelled from the church;
  - he fled Italy and lived for a long time in exile in **Switzerland, France, England and Germany**;
  - ⭐ in **1592** he returned to Italy and was arrested and imprisoned by the **Church Inquisition**;
  - he did not give up his views despite torture, and ⭐ was **burned in Rome in 1600**.
- He **supported Copernicus's heliocentric theory**.
- **His ideas:**
  - he drew theoretical conclusions from naturalist experience, which enriched philosophy;
  - ⭐ **true philosophy must be based on scientific experience**;
  - he **opposed scholastic doctrine**.
- ⭐ **The universe is unique, material, infinite and eternal:**
  - many worlds exist beyond the solar system;
  - our planet is the smallest part of the universe, and the Earth is a small particle of the infinite world;
  - **the stars are the suns of other planets.**
- **MCQ trap:** Copernicus *developed* heliocentrism; Bruno *supported* it and drew wider conclusions from it.

**17th-century continental rationalism** [T6-s11]
- Many leading 17th-century European philosophers were also mathematicians: **René Descartes** and **Blaise Pascal** in France, and **Gottfried Leibniz** in Germany.
- They held that the **method of reasoning** was the best model for gaining knowledge. That made them followers of ⭐ **rationalism**: **knowledge comes from reason alone**.
- This became the dominant belief in **continental Europe** for the next century.
- [slide image] Descartes is shown with the title ***Discourse on the Method*** [T6-s11 img]. The text does not discuss it.
- ⚠️ The doc switches to the singular ("he was considered as the follower of rationalism… He believed…"), while the slide says "they".
- Link: Theme 1 defines rationalism (from Latin *ratio* = mind) and presents Descartes's two equal substances, the thinking (spirit) and the extended (matter) [T1-doc].

**British empiricism** [T6-s12]
- At the same time a **very different tradition** arose in **Britain**. **Francis Bacon** and **John Locke** concluded that our knowledge of the world comes **not from reason but from experience**.
- This view, ⭐ **empiricism**, was typical of British philosophy in the **17th and 18th centuries**.
- ⭐ **What both traditions share:** they put the **human being at the center**. It is a human's reason *or* experience that leads to knowledge.
- [slide images, T6-s12 img]
  - "Francis Bacon 1561–1627";
  - "John Locke (1632–1704)… challenged the traditional form of government: Monarchy & Divine Right. Instead, he proposed: Social Contract".
  - These appear only as picture text.

#### 6.5 Philosophical schools and traditions of the Contemporary period [T6-doc; T6-s13–s16]
- The doc notes that several philosophical schools and traditions formed in the **second half of the 19th and in the 20th centuries** [doc-only sentence].

**Neo-Thomism (Neo-Scholasticism)** [T6-s13]
- ⭐ "The **largest religious and philosophical tradition** of Modern and Contemporary periods".
  - It rebuilt the teaching of **Thomas Aquinas (13th century)**.
  - It is Aquinas's "new, modern doctrine".
- ⭐ **There should be complete harmony between science and belief.** They complete each other, and **both are God's truth**.
- **Belief and logic:**
  - belief applies **where there is no knowledge**;
  - but belief must be accepted not only on faith; it also needs **logical perception**;
  - the philosophy of God must be **proved by various logical means**.
- ⭐ So **"philosophy is to serve religion."**
- The world (slide: "universe") and society are **created by God**, and social progress rests on **God's will**.

**Hermeneutics** [T6-s14–s16]
- The term comes from the Greek for **"art of interpretation"**.
- ⭐ It is the **theory of interpretation and understanding of texts**, including the texts of classical antiquity.
- ⭐ It is also one of the **philosophical traditions that emerged in the 20th century**.
- Its followers are theorists and practitioners of **biblical (theological), philosophical or philological** interpretation.
- The **myth of Hermes** most likely has nothing to do with the idea, though people sometimes connect the two.
- **Three uses of the term:**
  1. most often, for **biblical texts**;
  2. the doctrine of **restoring the original meaning of literary monuments** that cannot be understood without commentary (the doc adds: monuments surviving "in a distorted and partial form");
  3. the **interpretation of any work**, such as authors' explanatory publications.
  - In this sense hermeneutics is a **discipline of philological criticism**.
- **Core ideas: understanding, explanation, analysis.**
  - ⭐ Society rests on **dialogue**, the communication between people. Cultural, historical and scientific values are created in dialogue.
  - ⭐ So the main purpose of philosophy is to seek the **essence and meaning of life in dialogue** between people.
  - Analysing any **historical source** correctly depends on the researcher fully understanding the **features of that period**. The doc says "contemporary followers" hold this view.
- ⭐ **First generalizing work on hermeneutics:** by the Christian thinker **Aurelius Augustine (354–430)**, titled *"Christian Science, or Foundations of Sacred Hermeneutics and the Art of Church Oratory."* 🔎 Look up in Track B: the usual English title of this work.
- Hermeneutics has many supporters in modern times.
- [slide image] A banner reads "Hermeneutics — rules & guidelines for rightly interpreting the Bible" [T6-s14 img].
- ⭐ **MCQ trap:** hermeneutics is a **20th-century** philosophical tradition, yet its **first generalizing work** is Augustine's, from the 4th–5th c.

### THEME 7 — Ontology: Philosophical Interpretation of Being (week 7 · midterm; ⚠️ see the scope note at the top)

**Lecture outline** [T7-s2]:
1. Etymology of the term
2. The notion of being in philosophy
3. Views of scholars on the concept of being
4. The areas of existence

#### 7.1 The term "ontology" [T7-doc; T7-s3]
- ⭐ **Ontology is "the doctrine of existence and being."** As a branch of philosophy it studies:
  - the **fundamental principles of being**;
  - its most common **entities and categories**, and its **structure and patterns**;
  - existence **together with the theory of knowledge and logic**.
- ⭐ The **German philosopher Jacob Lorhard** first used the term, in ***Ogdoas Scholastica* (1606)**.
- [slide image] Slide 3 shows the 1606 title page of *Ogdoas Scholastica*, where the word "Ontologia" is printed next to "Metaphysices" [T7-s3 img].
- Link: Theme 1 lists ontology as the branch that deals with existence [T1-doc].

#### 7.2 The notion of being [T7-doc; T7-s4–s5]
- ⭐ **Being** is "one of the most important categories of philosophy". It expresses **the problem of existence in its most general form**.
- The word comes from the verb **"to be"**. It became a philosophical category only when philosophers raised the problem of existence and began to analyse it.
  - ⚠️ The doc says "as a philosophical **branch**"; the slide says "as a philosophical **category**".
- **Philosophy's subject** is the world as a whole, the relation between the **material and the ideal**, and man's place in society and the world. In other words, it tries to clarify the **existence of the world and the existence of man**.
- ⭐ Modern philosophical literature uses **two main meanings of "being"**:

| Sense | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Narrow** | the **objective world that exists independently of consciousness** |
| **Wide** | **everything that exists**: not only matter but also consciousness, ideas, feelings and even people's fantasies |

- ⭐ Being as **objective reality** is called **"matter"**.
- ⭐ **Non-being** is the opposite concept. If being is everything that exists, non-being is **everything that is not**.
- How being and non-being relate is a purely philosophical question, and the lecture traces how it was answered through history (below).

#### 7.3 Views of scholars on being

**Parmenides (c. 540 BC or 515 BC – c. 470 BC)** [T7-s6]
- An ancient Greek philosopher of the **Pre-Socratic** period.
- ⭐ **Founder and chief representative of the Eleatic school**, named after the town of **Elea**.
  - ⚠️ The bracket "(the town of Elea; the possibility of motion)" is garbled; his own thesis 4 below says being is *motionless*. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the Eleatic view of motion.
- ⭐ **Main work:** the metaphysical poem ***On Nature***.
- **His four theses on being:**
  1. There is **nothing besides being**, and **thinking is also being**.
  2. Being is **not generated**; otherwise it would have come from non-being, and there is no non-being.
  3. Being **cannot be damaged or destroyed**; otherwise it would turn into non-being, which does not exist.
  4. Being has **no past or future: it is the present**. It is **motionless, homogeneous, perfect and limited**.
- ⭐ **His slogan:** **"There is a Being and there is no non-existence."**

**Heraclitus (535–475)** [T7-s7]
- ⭐ **Unlike Parmenides, he focuses on motion.**
- For him the universe is a **cosmos** that no god and no person created. It "was, is and will be forever" as a ⭐ **living fire**.
- The universe is in **perpetual motion**, which sets him apart from Parmenides's immovable being.
- ⭐ He goes further: motion itself comes from the **mutual transition of opposites**. **Being and non-existence are inseparable**: each gives rise to the other and passes into it.
- ⚠️ **Dates disagree across the course:** Theme 2 gives Heraclitus as **520–460 BC** [T2-doc], while Theme 7 gives **535–475** (without "BC"). Note both.
  - Theme 2 also says he "founded the cognitive dialectics" and saw fire as the foundation of the universe.

**The atomists** [T7-s8]
- ⭐ **Leucippus** (the doc says his "years of life are unknown") and his disciple **Democritus (c. 460 – c. 370 BC)** were later ancient **materialist** philosophers.
  - They tried to overcome the contradictions in the doctrine of being and developed ⭐ **atomism**.
  - **Atoms are indivisible particles of matter**, and all visible bodies are made of atoms.
- Materialists of Ancient Greece and Rome took up atomism, above all **Epicurus (341–270 BC)** and **Titus Lucretius Car (c. 99 – c. 55 BC)**. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard spelling of "Lucretius Car".
- Atomism **revives in the philosophy of modern times**.
- From the **end of the 5th century BC**, Greek philosophy also developed very different, **idealistic** systems, with a completely different doctrine of being.

**The 3rd-century turn: from polytheism to Christian monotheism** [T7-doc; T7-s9]
- ⭐ The **3rd century** was a **transitional period** in Europe. The ancient world fell apart, **feudal Europe** arose, and a new religion, **Christianity**, appeared and spread.
- The old Greek and Roman gods were **polytheistic**:
  - they stood for **parts or elements of nature** (gods of heaven and earth, the sea, the underworld);
  - they lived close to people and dealt with them directly, deciding their fate and helping one side against another in war;
  - they were a necessary addition to nature and social life.
- The **monotheistic** worldview had **one God, the sole creator** of heaven and earth, plants, animals and man. The lecture calls this ⭐ "a **revolution** in the worldview".
- Legalizing Christianity and making it the **state religion of the Roman Empire** began the **exclusion of all other views** from social life.
- Slide 9 also carries the label "Patterns of the periods", which leads into the next slides.

**Ontology in the medieval period** [slide-only; T7-s10]. The slide lists:
- the **scholastic approach** to ontology;
- a **reconsideration of Aristotelianism**;
- the **legacy of Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd**;
- Thomas Aquinas: ⭐ "The world has beginning, but God could have created in a such a way that it existed eternally";
- **heliocentrism**.
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: Ibn Rushd, who is named only here, without details.

**19th to early 20th centuries** [T7-doc; T7-s11]
- Religious ideology dominated for a long time, and materialist teachings were weak and limited in reach. As a result, the existence of **society** was understood **idealistically**: ideas were treated as primary and decisive.
- ⭐ A fundamentally new situation arose in the **1840s–1850s**. The foundations of **dialectical materialism** were laid and the **materialist understanding of history** was formulated, by ⭐ **Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels**.
- ⭐ They introduced a new concept, **"social or public being"**: "the intrinsic, internal foundation of the existence and development of society, **not identical with its natural basis**."
- [doc-only] In the history of knowledge, forms of being were seen either as **objectively real** (nature, society, man) or as **imaginary** (the world of absolute essences, God).
- ⭐ **End of the 19th to early 20th c.:**
  - philosophy paid great attention to **problems of cognition**, and **gnoseology took the dominant position**;
  - some doctrines even denied the value of general philosophical concepts and called for rejecting **matter, spirit and being**.
- [slide-only] "**Big Bang theory** was introduced in **1920s** about the metaphysics that dominates till present" [T7-s11]. ⚠️ The wording is garbled; just remember "Big Bang theory, 1920s".

#### 7.4 The areas of existence [T7-doc; T7-s12]
- ⭐ Traditional philosophy distinguishes **three areas of existence: nature, society and consciousness** ("the state of awareness"). What they all share is that they **exist**.
- **Natural being** means the bodies and processes of nature. It has two parts:
  1. the existence of **eternal nature**, the natural world that existed **before and without human participation**;
  2. being **produced by human labor**: ⭐ the **"second nature", that is, culture**.
- ⭐ "Second nature" takes three forms:
  - **human existence**;
  - **spiritual existence**;
  - **the existence of social being**.
- ⚠️ The wording is a little confusing: these three forms sit under "second nature", which itself sits under "natural being". Learn the list as given.

### THEME 8 — Philosophical Laws, Categories and Methods (posted for week 8 · **likely post-midterm**; the syllabus lists it in Week 9)

**Lecture outline** [T8-s2]:
1. Philosophical laws
2. Categories of philosophy
3. Philosophical methods

#### 8.1 Starting point: the debate on objects and events [T8-doc; T8-s3]
- Since ancient times, scholars have debated the state of objects and events in the universe:
  1. their **movement, transformation and development**;
  2. how they **change from one state to another**;
  3. their **interrelations and interactions**.
- Without accurate knowledge of what objects essentially are, it is hard to understand scientifically:
  1. the universe and its development;
  2. the **source** and **driving forces** of development;
  3. above all, **how to predict the future**.
- ⭐ Everything is **always in motion, change and development**, from the smallest particles to the Earth, the Sun, the universe and human beings.
  - All of it is endlessly interconnected.
  - These changes **do not happen randomly or by chance**.

#### 8.2 Motion and development [T8-s4]
- ⭐ **Motion (movement) is "a change in the universe."**
- ⭐ **Development** is movement **from the bottom to the top**, or progressive movement **from the simple to the complex**.
- Development can be:
  1. **linear**, or
  2. sometimes a **slowly expanding spiral**.
- [slide image] Slide 4 contrasts a straight line ("How you may think success happens") with "The Possibility Spiral" [T8-s4 img].

#### 8.3 Philosophical laws [T8-doc; T8-s5]
- The world exists as an **eternal repetition of internal and external changes**. When these repetitions **keep the essence and characteristics** of objects and events, a **rule or law** forms.
- Everything in the universe rests on certain rules, including the development of **nature, society and humanity**.
- ⭐ **The main task of science** (social, natural, etc.) is to research every field and **discover and explain laws**.
- ⭐ **A law is "relatively stable, recurring relationships".** It does **not** show the changeable side of things.
- ⭐ **Example:** by the law of the seasons (the doc says "rules"), **spring comes after winter**. Spring cannot come after summer.
- The class materials define "law" only in general and **list no specific named laws**. 🔎 Look up in Track B: whether the course expects specific named laws.

#### 8.4 Categories of philosophy [T8-doc; T8-s6–s10]
- ⭐ **Category**, from the Greek for "**accusation, utterance, attribute**" (the doc's order is "utterance, accusation, attribute"), is **"an extremely general concept expressing the most essential relations of reality."**
- Studying categories means defining the **most fundamental and broad classes of entities**.

**Aristotle** [T8-s6–s8]
- ⭐ **Aristotle was the first to use the term "category."** In ancient Greek it meant "**accusation**", and Aristotle softened it to "**affirmation**" or "**utterance**".
- ⭐ He discussed categories in the ***Organon***, his work on **logic**. It has **six collections**, and ⭐ the **first collection is about the Categories**.
- ⭐ He applied the term to the **10 most common characteristics (attributes) of things**:
  1. **Substance (essence)**
  2. **Quantity**
  3. **Quality**
  4. **Relation**
  5. **Place**
  6. **Time**
  7. **Position**
  8. **State**
  9. **Action**
  10. **Affection**
- ⭐ He **systematized** the rules and categories of philosophy and **raised philosophy to the level of a science for the first time**. Since then, philosophy has been a science with its own laws, principles and categories.
- In the **East**, **Farobiy** (doc: "Forobiy"), **Beruniy** and **Ibn Sina** also studied the question.
- Things can be **concrete** (physical objects) or **abstract** (idea, time).
- [slide images] book covers "Aristotele — *Organon*" and "*The Categories* — Aristotle" [T8-s6, s7 img].
- Link: Theme 2 says Aristotle "compiled all his writings on logic" in the *Organon* [T2-doc]. Theme 1 gives his dates as 384–322 BC [T1-doc].

**Kant and Hegel** [T8-s9]
- The texts say a new era in the analysis of categories began "in the 17th and 19th centuries". ⚠️ Kant's book is dated **1781**, which is the 18th century, so the stated range doesn't fit the lecture's own example.
- ⭐ **Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781):** categories are **part of our own mental structure**. They are prior concepts through which **we interpret the world** around us.
- ⭐ **Kant's four types of categories:**
  - **Quality:** reality, negation, limitation
  - **Quantity:** unity, plurality, totality
  - **Relation:** substance and feature, reason and movement, interactions
  - **Modality:** possibility, existence
  - 🔎 Look up in Track B: Kant's full table of categories. The class list looks shortened; for example, modality has only two items.
- ⭐ **Hegel**, unlike Kant, grouped categories as:
  - **Being:** sensation, measure
  - **Essence:** cause and effect
  - **Notion:** beauty, goodness, truth
  - 🔎 Look up in Track B: Hegel's subdivisions as usually given.

**What categories are: summary** [T8-s10]
- Summing up the different views, categories as logical concepts are:
  1. the **perception of the universe**;
  2. a **method of cognition** that logically generalizes how things and events (realities) are interconnected;
  3. a **logical concept that changes** as things and events develop;
  4. **one of the levels of historical-logical knowledge** derived from the existence of the universe.
- ⭐ **Conclusion ("many scholars"):** categories are **concepts of the universe**, covering the things and events in it that are **eternal and repetitious**.

#### 8.5 Philosophical methods [T8-doc; T8-s11–s14]
- ⭐ **Method**, from Greek ***methodos*** = "**the way**; way of research, teaching, presentation", is:
  - a **set of techniques and operations** of cognition and practical activity;
  - **the way to achieve certain results** in cognition and practice.
- **Which method is used depends on:**
  - the **purpose** of the activity;
  - the **subject** being studied or acted on;
  - the **conditions** of the activity.
- Every sphere of activity has its own method (artistic creativity, information processing, warfare, etc.). ⭐ **Philosophy mainly considers the method of cognition.**
- **Every method:**
  - is **built on a particular theory**;
  - is a **necessary condition for research**;
  - is only as effective as its deep meaning and essence, and the soundness of the theory behind it.

**Dialectic(s)** [T8-s12]
- From Greek *διαλεκτική (dialektikḗ)*. It is **one of the methods of philosophy**.
- ⭐ **Original sense:** a **discourse or formal discussion** between two or more people who hold **different points of view** but want to **establish the truth through reasoned arguments**.
- ⭐ **The dialectical method** studies objects:
  - **in relation to other objects and to the whole system**;
  - **within a dynamic, evolutionary environment**.
- ⭐ It is usually **contrasted with the metaphysical method**, which studies objects in a **separated, isolated and static** environment.
- ⭐ **Socrates (469–399)** was the first to use the word "dialectic" (the doc adds "in his works that deal with philosophy").
  - The word became popular thanks to **Plato's *Dialogues***, where participants hold different opinions but seek the truth by exchanging them.
  - ⚠️ Both texts write "469–399 **AD**". That conflicts with the lecture's own framing of dialectic as coming "from ancient Greek philosophy" and with Plato's dialogues; read it as **BC** and 🔎 confirm in Track B.
- Link: Theme 2 says Heraclitus "founded the cognitive dialectics" [T2-doc]. Don't confuse this with **who first used the word "dialectic"**, which is Socrates (Theme 8).

**Metaphysics** [T8-s13]
- From Greek "**after physics**". Like dialectics, it is a **universal method**.
- ⭐ The word was introduced in the **1st century BC** by **Andronicus of Rhodes**, described as "the student of Aristotle, the interpreter of his poems".
  - ⚠️ Theme 1 dates Aristotle **384–322 BC** [T1-doc], so a 1st-century-BC "student" doesn't fit, and "poems" looks like a mistranslation. 🔎 Look up in Track B: Andronicus's real connection to Aristotle's works.
- ⭐ **Distinctive features of the metaphysical method:**
  1. **no comprehensive system approach** to the universe;
  2. **denial of interconnection and development** between things and realities.
- The text adds that the method is "related to the systematic study of the **stability** of the subjects, changes in them, the definition of sources and mechanisms of development". ⚠️ This seems to contradict feature 2, so memorize the two numbered features.
- Links:
  - Theme 2: Aristotle divided the theoretical sciences into philosophy (metaphysics), mathematics and physics [T2-doc];
  - Theme 1: "metaphysical philosophy" dealt with abstract objects such as existence, logic and causation [T1-doc].

**Sophism** [T8-s14] (the doc's heading is "Sophistic")
- From the Greek for "**the ability to cunningly debate**". It has two meanings:
  1. the **philosophical tradition in ancient Greece created by the Sophists**;
  2. **reasoning** (a conclusion or proof) built on a **deliberate violation of the laws and principles of formal logic**, using **false arguments presented as correct**.
- ⭐ The ancient Greek sophists introduced it as a **method of training** in the Greek "polices" around the **5th century**. ⚠️ "BC" is implied but not written. 🔎 Look up in Track B: what "polices" means here.
- ⭐ **The Sophists were professional teachers.** They taught **noble youth** **oratory and the art of public debate** to prepare them for **political or other careers**.
- **As a philosophical method**, sophism is a voluntary choice of thought based on **controversial ideas that can be used to prove anything**.

## 4. Compare / contrast tables (as taught)

### 4.1 The four medieval traditions (T5)

| | Gnosticism | Apologetics | Patristics | Scholasticism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Greek root** | *gnostikos*, "the knower" | *apologeomai*, "protect" | *pater*, "father" | *scholastikos*, "school, scientist" |

- **Patristics** (2nd–8th c.): the *Church Fathers* lay down the foundations of Christian doctrine.
- **Scholasticism** (the Middle Ages): *school* scholars use rational method and formal logic to work through that doctrine. Its forerunners were Islamic Ilm al-Kalām and Jewish Kalam.

## 6. Likely midterm short-answer / essay questions, with model-answer outlines

Questions 1–7 cover the midterm themes (5–7). Question 8 covers Theme 8, which is likely post-midterm, so keep it for the final. Every point below comes from the lecture texts and slides.

**Q1 (T5). Describe the main features of Western philosophy in the Middle Ages and its main schools and traditions.**
- **Period and features:**
  - 5th–15th c.; religion affected every aspect of life.
  - Christianity became the state religion and ideology.
  - The Inquisition became a priority: a Roman Catholic institution for finding and punishing heretics, set up in the early 13th c. against heresy in France.
- **Christianity as the background:**
  - *Christos*, "the anointed one"; arose in the early 1st c. and was dominant in the Roman Empire by the 4th c.
  - Three branches; main source the Bible, especially the New Testament.
  - It shaped philosophy.
- **Gnosticism:**
  - *gnostikos*, "the knower"; 1st–5th c.; a mix of Christian, Greek and Eastern ideas.
  - Dualism of the Supreme Spirit (light, good) and matter (evil), so God cannot create the material world.
  - Condemned as heresy; the term was coined by Henry More (17th c.); Manichaeism (Mani, 3rd c.) is linked with it.
- **Apologetics:**
  - *apologeomai*, "protect"; 1st–3rd c.
  - Defended Christianity against non-Christians, pagan rulers and scientists, and argued it could become the state religion.
  - Two lines: denial and confirmation. Representative: Saint Justin (*First Apology*, *Second Apology*, *On the Soul*).
- **Patristics:**
  - *pater*, "father"; 2nd–8th c.
  - The Church Fathers (a title used from the end of the 4th c.) shaped dogmatics, church hierarchy and worship, and the canon.
  - They contributed to medieval ethics and aesthetics.
- **Scholasticism:**
  - *scholastikos*, "school, scientist".
  - Theological dogma + a rationalistic method + formal logic.
  - Its name comes from the Court school of Great Charles (742–814); its forerunners were Islamic Ilm al-Kalām and Jewish Kalam.
- **Conclusion:**
  - In this period philosophy developed inside Christian doctrine and served it.
  - The period ends with Aquinas's synthesis and Roger Bacon's critique.
  - The doc also names realism and nominalism.

**Q2 (T5). Compare the views of Thomas Aquinas and Roger Bacon.**
- **Aquinas (1225–1274):**
  - Moderate realism; "the greatest figure in the scholasticism".
  - United Aristotle's philosophy with theology: Aristotle's natural philosophy and logic can reach the truth of God's existence.
  - *Summa Theologiae*: 3,000 articles, 600 issues. Part 1 covers God's existence, Part 2 "Moving Towards God".
  - The world as a hierarchical ladder: the Earth and its 4 elements at the bottom; the soul between God and the material world; the pope at the top of secular society; an angelic world above the Earth.
- **Roger Bacon (1214–1292):**
  - *Doctor Mirabilis*; English philosopher-naturalist; Oxford; worked in mathematics, chemistry and physics.
  - Created no new teaching but was the first to oppose scholasticism, attacking Albert the Great and Aquinas.
  - *Opus majus* (1268): abstract dialectics is useless; study nature by observation and mathematical calculation.
  - Mathematics is the most reliable, easiest and most accessible science.
  - Two types of experience (life experience; evidence of the senses), plus spiritual experience through inner insight, which anticipated heuristic insight and the role of intuition in science.
- **Contrast:**
  - Aquinas builds a system on authority (Aristotle + theology).
  - Bacon criticizes authority and turns to observation, mathematics and experience.
  - Both belong to the 13th c.

**Q3 (T6). Explain the periodization of the Modern period and the main features and legacy of the European Renaissance.**
- **Periodization:**
  - The Modern period lies between the Middle Ages and the Contemporary era.
  - Early modern (early 16th to mid-18th c.): Renaissance, Age of Discovery, Protestant Reformation.
  - Late modern (mid-18th c. to 1945): Enlightenment.
  - Contemporary period from 1945; some count it as a third stage.
- **"Repositioning of the world" [doc]:**
  - World trade and commercialization.
  - Rationalized politics: bureaucrats enforce central edicts; the French monarchy's 17th-c. institutions (language, prisons, army uniforms, logistics, pensions).
  - Culture: printing and newspapers, the Reformation leading to toleration, the scientific revolution.
- **The Renaissance:**
  - A cultural-ideological era in Western and Central Europe.
  - Features: secularism, a humanistic world view, revival of antiquity ("rebirth").
  - A bridge to the Modern era; Italy 14th–16th c., elsewhere 15th–16th c.
- **Legacy:**
  - Anthropocentric thinking built on humanism and on rediscovered Greek philosophy (Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things"), expressed in art, architecture, politics, science and literature.
  - A new self-consciousness: pride, self-assertion, awareness of one's strength and talent.
  - Man is the creator of himself and of his destiny, no longer needs divine grace, and is lord over nature.

**Q4 (T6). How did Copernicus and Giordano Bruno change the scientific picture of the world?**
- **Copernicus (1473–1543):**
  - Polish; *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*, written just before his death in 1543.
  - Replaced Ptolemy's geocentric system (Earth at the center with complex orbits, ~1500 years old) with heliocentrism, based on studying the motion of the planets.
  - A major achievement of natural science; it showed Christian doctrine on the structure of the world to be inconsistent.
- **Bruno (1548–1600):**
  - Born near Naples; accused of heresy and expelled from the church.
  - Exile in Switzerland, France, England and Germany; returned in 1592 and was arrested by the Inquisition.
  - Did not give up his views under torture and was burned in Rome in 1600.
  - Supported heliocentrism; philosophy must rest on scientific experience; opposed scholasticism.
  - The universe is unique, material, infinite and eternal: many worlds, stars are the suns of other planets, the Earth is a small particle.
- **Link:** this shows the clash between science and Church doctrine; the Church Inquisition (defined in Theme 5) is the institution that arrested and imprisoned Bruno.

**Q5 (T6). Compare continental rationalism and British empiricism.**
- **Context:** in the 17th c. the leading European philosophers were also mathematicians.
- **Rationalism:**
  - Descartes and Pascal (France), Leibniz (Germany).
  - The method of reasoning is the best model, so knowledge comes from reason alone.
  - The dominant belief in continental Europe for the next century.
- **Empiricism:**
  - Francis Bacon and John Locke (Britain): knowledge comes from experience, not reason.
  - Typical of British philosophy in the 17th–18th c.
- **Common ground:** both put the human at the center; human reason or experience leads to knowledge.
- **Optional, from slide images:** Descartes's *Discourse on the Method*; Locke's "social contract" against "monarchy & divine right".

**Q6 (T6). Describe Neo-Thomism and hermeneutics as philosophical schools of the Contemporary period.**
- **Context:** several schools formed in the second half of the 19th c. and in the 20th c. [doc].
- **Neo-Thomism (Neo-Scholasticism):**
  - The largest religious-philosophical tradition; it rebuilt Aquinas's 13th-c. teaching.
  - Complete harmony between science and belief, since both are God's truth.
  - Belief applies where there is no knowledge but must be accepted through logical perception, and God's existence must be proved logically, so "philosophy is to serve religion".
  - The world and society are created by God, and progress follows God's will.
- **Hermeneutics:**
  - "Art of interpretation": the theory of interpreting and understanding texts; a 20th-c. tradition.
  - Biblical, philosophical and philological interpreters; sometimes linked with the myth of Hermes.
  - Three uses: biblical texts; restoring the meaning of literary monuments; interpreting any work. In this sense it is philological criticism.
  - Core: understanding, explanation, analysis. Society rests on dialogue, and the meaning of life is sought in dialogue.
  - Analysing a historical source requires understanding its period.
  - First generalizing work: Augustine (354–430).
- **Contrast:** Neo-Thomism subordinates philosophy to religion, while hermeneutics grounds meaning in human dialogue and interpretation.

**Q7 (T7). (a) What is ontology, and how did Parmenides, Heraclitus and the atomists interpret being? (b) How did later thought change the idea of being, and what are the areas of existence?**
- **(a) Ontology and being:**
  - Ontology is the doctrine of existence and being (principles, entities, categories, structure, patterns), studied together with the theory of knowledge and logic. Lorhard first used the term in *Ogdoas Scholastica*, 1606.
  - Being is a key category: existence in general form, from "to be".
    - Narrow sense: the objective world independent of consciousness.
    - Wide sense: everything that exists, including ideas and fantasies.
    - Objective reality is "matter"; non-being is everything that is not.
  - **Parmenides** (Eleatic school, *On Nature*): four theses (only being exists; it is not generated, not destroyed, only present, motionless, homogeneous, perfect, limited). Slogan: "There is a Being and there is no non-existence".
  - **Heraclitus:** perpetual motion; the cosmos is an eternal living fire; motion is the mutual transition of opposites; being and non-being are inseparable.
  - **Atomists** (Leucippus, Democritus; later Epicurus, Lucretius): atoms are indivisible particles of matter; atomism revives in modern times. From the end of the 5th c. BC, idealistic systems arise.
- **(b) Later changes and areas of existence:**
  - **3rd c.:** a transitional period. Polytheistic nature-gods give way to one creator God, "a revolution in the worldview", and Christianity as state religion excludes other views.
  - **Medieval ontology (slide 10):** the scholastic approach, Aristotelianism, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd, Aquinas's quote, heliocentrism.
  - **Marx and Engels:** after long idealistic views of society, in the 1840s–1850s they founded dialectical materialism and the materialist understanding of history. "Social or public being" is the internal foundation of society, not identical with its natural basis.
  - **Late 19th to early 20th c.:** gnoseology dominates, and some call for rejecting matter, spirit and being.
  - **Areas of existence:** nature, society, consciousness. Natural being is eternal nature plus "second nature" (culture: human, spiritual and social existence).

**Q8 (T8, likely post-midterm / final). Define philosophical laws, categories and methods. Compare Aristotle's, Kant's and Hegel's views on categories, and contrast the dialectical and metaphysical methods.**
- **Background:** everything is in motion, change and development, and none of it is random. Motion is a change in the universe. Development runs from simple to complex, either linear or as a slowly expanding spiral.
- **Law:** relatively stable, recurring relationships, formed when repeated changes keep their essence (spring follows winter). Science's main task is to discover laws.
- **Category:** an extremely general concept expressing the most essential relations of reality (Greek "accusation, utterance, attribute").
  - **Aristotle:** first used the term; the *Organon* has 6 collections, the first being the *Categories*; 10 categories; raised philosophy to a science. In the East: Farobiy, Beruniy, Ibn Sina.
  - **Kant** (1781): categories are part of our mental structure; 4 types (quality, quantity, relation, modality).
  - **Hegel:** being, essence, notion.
  - Four characteristics of categories; conclusion: concepts of the eternal and repetitious in the universe.
- **Method:** "the way"; techniques and operations of cognition and practice. It depends on purpose, subject and conditions. Philosophy studies the method of cognition, and every method rests on a theory.
- **Dialectical method:** objects in relation to each other and the whole system, in a dynamic, evolutionary environment. Socrates first used the word; Plato's *Dialogues* made it popular.
- **Metaphysical method:** objects as isolated and static; no system approach; denies interconnection and development. The word comes from Andronicus of Rhodes, 1st c. BC.
- **Sophism:** "cunning debate"; reasoning that deliberately breaks formal logic. The Sophists were professional teachers of oratory, around the 5th c.
