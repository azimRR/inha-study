## 1. Big picture

- ⭐ **Philosophy = "love of wisdom"** (Greek *phileo* + *sophia*). It studies the most general, essential features and fundamental principles of **existence (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), values (ethics), reason (logic), mind and language**, and each of these is a separate branch [T1-doc; T1-s3].
- Philosophy is one of the oldest disciplines and once covered any body of knowledge. Its three classical branches (**natural, moral, metaphysical**) gave rise to the natural, social and formal sciences. In the modern era, psychology, sociology, linguistics and economics split off as separate disciplines [T1-doc; T1-s5].
- ⭐ The basic question of philosophy is **what is primary: spirit or matter?** The answers are **monism** (materialist or idealist), **dualism** (Descartes) and **pluralism**. For how we know, the lecture names **sensualism, rationalism and irrationalism** [T1].
- ⭐ In East and West alike, philosophy **grew out of mythology**, "the first form of social consciousness".
  - Egypt and Babylon mixed belief in gods with secular knowledge.
  - India had the **Vedas, the Upanishads and the astika/nastika schools**.
  - China had **Confucius ("Li")** and **Lao-Tzu (the Dao's law, In and Yan)** [T2].
- ⭐ Greek philosophy runs **Pre-Socratic → Classical → Hellenistic**.
  - The Pre-Socratics looked for the **first principle**: water, apeiron, air, fire, numbers.
  - **Socrates** put ethics first. **Plato** built the theory of ideas and the ideal state. **Aristotle** wrote the Organon and taught the four causes. **Epicurus** taught atomism [T2].
- ⭐ **Islam** frames early medieval Central Asian thought: the Qur'an, Sunnah and Shari'a, the five pillars, the Sunni/Shia/Kharijite branches and the four caliphates. The **Arab conquest** came in two stages: the late 7th c. (south) and the early 8th c. under **Qutayba ibn Muslim** [T3].
- ⭐ During the **Islamic Golden Age** (8th–13th c.; House of Wisdom under Harun al-Rashid):
  - Central Asians created the **three Sufi orders**: Yassawiya, Kubrawiya, Naqshbandiya.
  - **al-Maturidi** of Samarkand shaped kalam.
  - **Farabi ("the Second Teacher"), Beruni and Ibn Sina** led philosophy and science [T3].
- ⭐ The **Timurid era** began when **Amir Temur** became ruler in **1370**. The lecture calls it "the highest peak of the civilization and culture of the peoples of Central Asia". The rulers sponsored science, and in the 14th–15th c. **logic, natural sciences (especially astronomy), philosophy and ethics** flourished [T4].
- ⭐ Timurid scholars fall into three clusters:
  - **logic**: Taftazoni, Jurjoniy;
  - **astronomy and mathematics in Samarkand**: Ulugbek, Ali Qushchi;
  - **literature and ethics**: Jomi, Navoi, Koshifiy. The lecture frames this part with Herat becoming a cultural centre in the 2nd half of the 15th c. [T4].
- One thread runs through all four themes: **Greek thought, especially Aristotle, feeds into Central Asian thought.**
  - Farabi is "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle, and Ibn Sina is an "adherent of Aristotle".
  - Ulugbek was influenced by Aristotle and Ptolemy (as well as Khorezmi, Farghani, Farobi, Ibn Sino, Beruni).
  - In turn, Ulugbek's astronomy was developed from the 16th c. by Copernicus and Galileo [T3; T4].

#### 1.1 The basic objectives and scope of philosophy  [T1-doc; T1-s3, s5]
- ⭐ **Definition.** Philosophy is a discipline that studies "the most common essential characteristics and fundamental principles" of:
  - existence → **ontology**
  - knowledge → **epistemology**
  - values → **ethics**
  - reason → **logic**
  - plus **mind and language** [T1-doc].
  - The slide wording is that its "main aim" is to study "general and fundamental issues" [T1-s3].
- Each of these problems is a **separate branch** of the subject [T1-doc; T1-s3].
- Philosophy is "one of the most ancient disciplines" and once referred to **any body of knowledge**. It is therefore closely related to **mathematics, natural science, religion and politics** [T1-doc; T1-s5].
- ⭐ **Three major branches in the Classical period** [T1-doc; T1-s5]. The slide title is "The Role of Philosophy in the System of Sciences".

| Classical branch | What it studied | What it "birthed" |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural philosophy** | the physical world | the natural sciences: astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, cosmology |
| **Moral (ethics) philosophy** | goodness, justice, right and wrong | the social sciences, especially ethics and political philosophy |
| **Metaphysical philosophy** | abstract objects: existence, logic, causation | the formal sciences: logic, mathematics, philosophy of science |

- ⭐ In the modern era (the doc adds "and contemporary history"), some investigations that had been part of philosophy became **separate academic disciplines: psychology, sociology, linguistics, economics** [T1-doc; T1-s5].

#### 1.2 The definition of the term and its interpretations  [T1-doc; T1-s3–s4]

- ⭐ **Pythagoras (c. 570–495 BC)** was the **first to use the term** "philosophy".
  - His image: "Life ... is like a festival: some come to compete in it, some to trade, and the happiest to watch."
  - So for Pythagoras the meaning of philosophy is **the search for truth** [T1-doc; T1-s4].

- ⭐ **Herodotus (484–424 BC)**, the Greek historian, "appears to have been the first" to use the **verb "to philosophize"**, which meant **"(to) observe"** [T1-doc; T1-s4].
- ⭐ **Plato (428/427–347 BC):** the task of philosophy is to know **the eternal and absolute truths**. Only philosophers, born with the appropriate wise soul, can do this [T1-doc; the slide sentence T1-s4 is cut short].
- ⭐ **Aristotle (384–322 BC):** the task of philosophy is **comprehension of the universal in the world itself**, and its subject is **the first principles and reasons for being** [T1-doc; T1-s4].

| Thinker | How he understood philosophy |
|---|---|
| Pythagoras | the **search for truth**: the "happiest" at life's festival are the ones who **watch** |

#### 1.3 The concepts and the doctrines of philosophy  [T1-doc; T1-s6–s8]
**Questions of philosophy** (a bold heading in the docx) [T1-doc; T1-s6]
- ⭐ The first general question that reveals the nature of philosophical thinking is **"what is primary: the spirit or matter, ideal or material?"** The general understanding of **being** depends on the answer, because the material and the ideal are being's "ultimate characteristics".
- The question of the primordial origins of being is tied to **monism, dualism and pluralism** [T1-doc; T1-s6].

**Monism, dualism, pluralism** [T1-doc; T1-s7]
- ⭐ **Monism** (Greek *monos*, "one, only"): the world has **one beginning**, either a material or a spiritual substance. There are two kinds:
  - **Materialistic monism**: the ideal derives from the material. Its conclusions rest on the data of natural science (this last point is doc-only).
  - **Idealistic monism**: the material is due to the ideal or spiritual. It must prove that spirit (consciousness, idea, God) created the world, which "cannot be positively solved within the framework of modern science".
- ⭐ **Dualism** (Latin *dualis*, "dual"): the **equality of two principles**, matter and consciousness, physical and mental.
  - Example: ⭐ **R. Descartes** held that being rests on two equal substances (the text writes "sub-stations"): **the thinking (spirit)** and **the extended (matter)**. The slide says "the thinking (spirit) and the matter".
- ⭐ **Pluralism** (Latin *pluralis*; the gloss in the materials just repeats "pluralis"): **several or many** initial bases of being.
  - Example: ancient thinkers who named such different foundations as **earth, water, air, fire**, etc.
- (slide image) [T1-s7]: two symbols beside these definitions, a circle with a central dot and a yin-yang sign.

**Method** [T1-doc; T1-s8]
- Each science has its own method. The ⭐ **philosophical method** (Greek *«methodos»*, "the **path of knowledge**") is:
  - a system of the most common methods of theoretical and practical ("actual") development of reality; and
  - a way of constructing and substantiating philosophical knowledge itself.
- Like other sciences' methods, it **originates in people's practical activity**. Its source is a reflection of the logic and laws of development of **objective reality**.
- ⭐ The three doctrines below are called "doctrines of philosophy" in the doc and "**methods of epistemology**" on the slide [T1-doc; T1-s8]:
  - ⭐ **Sensualism** (Latin *«sensus»*, "feeling"): takes **feelings as the basis of knowledge** and tries to infer all knowledge from the **sense organs and sensations**, "absolutizing their role in cognition".
  - ⭐ **Rationalism** (Latin *«ratio»*, "mind"): the **mind** is the basis of people's cognition and action.
  - ⭐ **Irrationalism**: **denies, or at least limits, the role of reason** in cognition and focuses on **irrational** ways of comprehending being.

> **People in Theme 1:** Pythagoras · Herodotus · Plato · Aristotle · R. Descartes.
> **Terms in Theme 1:** philosophy (phileo, sophia) · ontology · epistemology · ethics · logic · natural / moral / metaphysical philosophy · "to philosophize" · monism (materialistic / idealistic) · dualism · pluralism · method (methodos) · sensualism · rationalism · irrationalism.

#### 2.0 From mythology to philosophy  [T2-doc; T2-s3]
- ⭐ In **both East and West**, philosophical thought **originated from mythology**, "the **first form of social consciousness**".
- ⭐ **"Mythology"** comes from **Middle French** and means **"exposition of myths"**. The term appeared in the **15th century**.
- Mythology explains the world and its phenomena through **the action of gods and heroes**.
- Mythology posed philosophical questions for the first time in human history: how did the world come into being and how does it develop? What are existence and death?
- ⭐ (doc-only, italic in the docx) The first question of the early philosophers was **"What is the universe made of?"**. It soon widened into **"What is the nature of whatever it is that exists?"**

#### 2.1 Eastern philosophy in the ancient period

**(a) Egypt and Babylon** [T2-doc; T2-s4–s6]
- Some of the earliest religious-philosophical ideas arose in the historical territories of modern **Egypt and Iraq**.
- **Babylon** was originally a small city from the period of the **Akkadian Empire, c. 2300 BC**. It became an independent city-state in the **19th century** (the slide says "19th century BC").
- ⭐ Two works reflect religio-philosophical thought:
  - **"The Dialogue between Master and his Slave on the Essence of Life"**
  - **"The song of Arfist"**
  - 🔎 Look up in Track B: these two titles (the spelling "Arfist", and which civilization each belongs to).
- ⭐ **Characteristic feature:** on one hand, **belief in gods** and the influence of divine powers on nature and society. On the other hand, religious views led to the formation of **secular knowledge and scientific thought**. The lecture says this double feature is "distinctive for **all** the civilizations in ancient times".
- ⭐ (slide-only) [T2-s6] **Features of the Ancient Period:** **polytheism**, **belief in magic**, **divinity of the ruler**. The slide image is a chart of Egyptian gods (Isis, Osiris, Horus, Amun, Thoth, Ptah, Set, Hathor and others).

**(b) India** [T2-doc; T2-s7–s9]
- Ancient India is "one of the cradles of human civilization". Its original philosophical doctrines are in written sources such as the **Vedas**.
- ⭐ **Vedas:** written **c. 1,500 BC**.
  - The doc calls them "a sacred science and prophetic book"; the slide calls them "sacred texts … a religious science and prophetic book".
  - Hindus regard them as the words spoken by the **Supreme Lord, Brahma**.
  - They contain ancient Hindu **history, economy, religion, philosophy, morality**.
- ⭐ The Vedas survive as **four collections ("samhites"):** **Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda, Adharvaveda**. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard spelling of "samhites".
- ⭐ **Upanishads** ("Upanishadas" in the doc) are **the philosophical part of the Vedas**. The name means **"mysterious knowledge"**.
  - They are neither books nor philosophical booklets: they are texts by various authors composed **at different times**.
  - Their issues: the universe surrounding the person, its place and role in life; the outside world and human nature; the essence of life and mentality; standards of morals.
- ⭐ Hindu philosophers divided the ancient Indian schools into **two groups: astika and nastika** (italic in the docx).
  - ⭐ **Astika:** **Vedanta, yoga, vaysheshika, mimansa**. These schools acknowledge **the holiness of the Vedas** and say the only truth is expressed in them.
  - ⭐ **Nastika:** **Charvaka-Lokayata** (the doc spells it "Chorvaka Lokayata"/"Chorvaka-lokaya"), **Buddhism, Jainism**. They **do not admit the holiness of the Vedas**. Charvaka-Lokayata followers held **materialist** teachings.

**(c) China** [T2-doc; T2-s10–s11]
- In ancient China, science and culture developed "in a specific way".
- ⭐ **Confucianism** is one of the major Chinese philosophical branches. Its founder is ⭐ **Confucius**, a teacher, editor, politician and philosopher.
  - (slide-only) The slide adds the name **"Kong Fuzi"** and the dates **551–479** (no BC/AD given).
  - The universe is **ruled by heaven**, and ⭐ **the will of heaven is destiny**. The world and its order are sent by the ruler of heaven.
  - ⭐ **"Li"** is the concept that defines the essence of the order: it "reflects the essence of the order of the world".
- ⭐ **Lao-Tzu (VI–V centuries)** is "one of the foremost philosophers of ancient China" and the source of **Daoism**.
  - The universe (the slide says "nature"), society and human life must **obey the Dao's law**.
  - (slide image) [T2-s11]: **"道 Dao (Way, Path)"**, and "Life is all about balance and harmony" with a yin-yang sign.
  - ⭐ Under this law, **"Tsi"**, the **five elements (fire, water, air, land and wood or metal)**, form the basis of the world's objects and ensure their occurrence. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the term "Tsi" and the five-element list as given in class.
  - Nothing in the universe can be permanent and immobile. ⚠️ check wording: the doc says "permanent and **unstable**", the slide says "permanent and **stable**".
  - ⭐ Everything in the world **moves from one state to another**. The **struggle between the opposing forces In and Yan** is the source of the forces that move the universe. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard spelling of "In and Yan".
  - Humans must always be **on the side of good** in this struggle and **love the surrounding nature**. Otherwise the Dao's law is broken, which ends in trouble and tragedy.

#### 2.2 Ancient Western (Greek) philosophy  [T2-doc; T2-s12–s18]
- ⭐ **Time span** ⚠️ check: the doc says **8th century BC – 4th century CE**; the slide says **6th century BC – 3rd century AD** [T2-doc; T2-s12].
- ⭐ **Three stages:** **(1) Pre-Socratic, (2) Classical Greek, (3) Hellenistic** [T2-doc; T2-s12].

**(a) Pre-Socratic philosophy**: these philosophers **first posed the question of the first principle of the universe** [T2-doc; T2-s12].
- ⭐ **Thales of Miletus (624–546 BC**; the doc gives no BC/AD) held that the universe is based on **"all the water"**.
- ⭐ **Anaximander** (Thales's **disciple**; no dates given) saw the world as the infinite, gaseous **apeiron** (the doc misspells it "aperius"; the slide image is labelled "Apeiron"). **Heat and cold** drive the transformation of matter from one state to another.
  - He wrote about **"Nature"**, but the work did not survive.
- ⭐ **Anaximenes (588–525 BC**; the doc typo says "AD") held that **air** is the foundation of the universe.
- ⭐ **Heraclitus (520–460 BC)** [T2-s13] was born into a "jewelry family" in **Ephesus**, "on the military shore of Asia Minor" (⚠️ check wording).
  - ⭐ He **founded the cognitive dialectics** and took ⭐ **fire** as the foundation of the universe.
  - Everything in the universe moves and there is no stagnation. Movement belongs to the whole of nature. **"Eternal action is eternal change."** Movement takes place "in striking contrast".
  - Constant change and transition into opposites relate to the **relative quality of objects**: ⭐ seawater is unfit for humans but fit for fish.
  - His views had a great impact on later philosophical thinking.
  - (slide image) [T2-s13] ⭐ quote: **"Everything flows, nothing stands still."** The image credits it to Heraclitus as "quoted by Plato in Craytulus" [sic].
- ⭐ **Pythagoras (580–500 BC)** [T2-s14] lived on the Greek island of **Samos** (⚠️ T1 gives c. 570–495 BC).
  - ⭐ He founded the **religious-ideological philosophical school** in ancient Greece.
  - Learning the world must **begin with knowing the numbers**.
  - ⭐ At the basis of the universe lie not matter or nature but **idealized figures, that is, numbers**. Things and events are the result of these numbers.
  - ⭐ He was the first to propose a **quantitative category** for explaining things and phenomena in nature.
  - He came from the tribe of nobles and **defended aristocracy**: aristocratic order is vital in social life, and **democracy violates** that order.

**(b) Classical Greek philosophy** [T2-doc; T2-s15–s16]
- ⭐ **Socrates** (no dates given) "made a **radical revolution** in philosophy".
  - Natural philosophy is largely indifferent to man, so **not physics but ethics** comes first. The philosophy of man must be the key to the philosophy of nature, not the other way round.
  - Man is a **knowing and intelligent being**, and ⭐ **the essence of man is the soul**. The soul is a person's ability to realize, to show mental activity, and to be conscientious, moral and virtuous.
  - The soul's potential is realized in **knowledge**; the lack of knowledge is **ignorance**.
  - The body does not dominate the soul. On the contrary, ⭐ **the body is an instrument of the soul**.
- ⭐ **Plato** (dates only in T1: 428/427–347 BC):
  - "a great ally in the history of the world philosophy". He was also an **artist, poet and playwright** and expressed his ideas **in the form of dialogues**.
  - ⭐ **Aristotle** was **Plato's closest friend and disciple**.
  - His teaching on **society and the state** is central to his ideology. He was a classic of ontology and **gnoseology** (the slide spells it "gnoceology") and well informed in political theory and pedagogy. 🔎 Look up in Track B: "gnoseology" (not defined in class).
  - ⭐ His socio-political works: **"The Laws", "The Republic"** and **"The Critique"** (the slide says **"The Crito"**) ⚠️ check.
  - In one book he sets out his views on society and his ideal political system, with his ⭐ **central doctrine, the theory of ideas**. The doc calls this book **"The State"**; the slide calls it **"The Republic"**. ⚠️ check. (The slide image is a book cover, "Plato – The Republic".)
  - ⭐ **Four forms of the state:** theocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny. 🔎 Look up in Track B: Plato's list of state forms (verify "theocracy").
  - ⭐ Plato's idealistic dream of the state lies in **the idea of justice**.

**(c) Hellenistic philosophy** [T2-doc; T2-s17–s18]
- ⭐ **Definition:** the period of ancient Greek philosophy that developed in the **Hellenistic civilization following Aristotle**.
- ⚠️ check (placement of Aristotle): the doc puts the Aristotle paragraph **under** the "Hellenistic philosophy" heading. The slides give Aristotle his own slide (s17) **before** the "Hellenistic philosophy" slide (s18), and the definition itself says Hellenistic philosophy came **after** Aristotle.
- ⭐ **Aristotle** [T2-s17]:
  - an "outstanding scientist" who wrote on **logic, psychology, philosophy, morality, rhetoric, natural sciences**; "all his works are devoted to studying the **ontology**".
  - ⭐ He compiled all his writings on logic in the **"Organon"**.
  - ⭐ He divided the sciences into **theoretical** and **practical**:
    - practical sciences give guidance and **do something useful**;
    - theoretical sciences have three parts: **philosophy (metaphysics), mathematics, physics**.
  - ⭐ Things and phenomena have **four reasons (causes)**: the **material** cause, the **form or shape** cause, the **creative** reason and the **purpose** reason.
    - The **creative cause** is the **source of movement**.
    - ⭐ **Motion is eternal, like the universe**, and the universe has its eternal cause, **the motion force**.
  - His teaching on society and the state is in **"The State"** and **"The Politics"** (slide image: book cover "Aristotle – Politics"). 🔎 Look up in Track B: what "The State" refers to.
- ⭐ **Epicurus (341–270 BC)**, an ancient Greek philosopher [T2-s18]:
  - ⭐ advanced the doctrine of **atomism**, first introduced by ⭐ **Democritus**;
  - taught that the universe is **material, eternal and infinite atoms**, made of **body and emptiness**;
  - his philosophical and moral teachings were "instrumental" in that time;
  - ⭐ held that the **state** should rest on the **mutual understanding and social agreement** of people.

#### 3.1 Principles and main branches of Islam  [T3-doc; T3-s4–s6]
- ⭐ **Islam** is an Arabic word meaning **self-submission to God, obedience, and peace**. (slide image [T3-s4]: "ISLAM is the religion of peace and mercy. The true message of god".)
- The basis of Islamic teaching is the **Koran (Qur'an)** and the **hadiths** (doc).
- ⭐ **Qur'an:** the holy book, believed to be the word of God **dictated to Muhammad by the archangel Gabriel** and written down in **Arabic**.
  - It contains Islamic principles (the slide says "traditions"), beliefs, and legal and moral principles.
  - ⭐ It has **114 units (surahs)** and several verses (**ayats**).
- ⭐ **Sunnah** is **a collection of hadiths**. **Hadiths** record the Prophet Muhammad's **words and actions**. ⭐ Hadiths are **the primary source after the Qur'an**.
- ⭐ **Shari'ah** is **Islamic canonical law**. It is based on the Koran, the traditions of the Prophet (Hadith and Sunna) and other important sources, and forms a system of legal and ethical principles and practical requirements.
- ⭐ **The Five Pillars of Islam** (*arkan al-Islam*; also *arkan ad-din*, "pillars of religion") are obligatory for all believers [T3-s5]:
  1. the creed, **shahadah**
  2. daily prayers, **salat**
  3. almsgiving, **zakat**
  4. **fasting during Ramadan**
  5. the pilgrimage to Mecca, **hajj**, **at least once in a lifetime**
- ⭐ **The main branches:** **Sunnis, Shi'ism** (the slide says "Shiahs"), **Khawarijs (Kharijites)** [T3-s6].
  - ⭐ **Kharijites** appeared in the first century during the **First Fitna**, the leadership crisis after Muhammad's death. In the Middle Ages they split into many subdivisions and then disappeared. ⭐ **Only the Ibodis survived** as a remnant.
  - ⭐ **Sunni vs Shia** differ in **authority (Sunni = caliphate; Shi'ism = imamah)**, in religious ceremonies and in traditions.
    - **Sunni:** the caliph (head of state) should be **elected** by Muslims or their representatives.
    - **Shia:** the caliph should be an **Imam chosen by God from the Ahl al-Bayt** ("Family of the House", Muhammad's direct descendants).
    - ⭐ (doc-only) "In simpler terms, the Sunni favour **election** while the Shia favour **bloodline**."
  - **Shia sects:** ⭐ **Karmats, Ishmaelites, Zaidids** and others. Shiites recognize the Koran as divine, accept the **hadiths related to Ali**, and recognize the imam's authority (imamah). ⭐ They are **about 10%** of the world's Muslims.
  - **Sunnism** is world-wide, ⭐ **about 90%** of Muslims. ⭐ It has **four main legal schools: Hanafi, Shafi'ite, Malikite, Hanbalite**.

#### 3.2 Arab invasion of Central Asia  [T3-doc; T3-s7–s8]
- Under the flag of Islam, Arab tribal groups united over a vast territory in a **single centralized Arab caliphate**.
- ⭐ **Four major caliphates** (in the medieval and modern periods):
  1. **Rashidun** Caliphate (**632–661**)
  2. **Ummayad** Caliphate (**661–750**) (class spelling; 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard spelling)
  3. **Abbasid** Caliphate (**750–1258**)
  4. **Ottoman Empire** (**1517–1924**)
- Along with the Arab invasion, **Movarounnahr** also embraced Islam.
- ⭐ **The Arab conquest of Central Asia came in two periods:**
  - **late 7th century:** Arab troops took southern Central Asia: ⭐ **Balkh, Chaghaniyon, Marv**;
  - **early 8th century:** the Arab army under ⭐ **Qutayba ibn Muslim** conquered ⭐ **Kesh, Samarqand, Tashkent, O'sh, Eastern Turkestan**.

**Islamic Golden Age / Islamic philosophy** (slide title [T3-s8]: "Islamic Philosophy")
- ⭐ The **Islamic Golden Age** (bold in the docx) is traditionally dated **8th–13th century**. Economic development and cultural works flourished.
- ⭐ It began in the reign of the Abbasid caliph **Harun al-Rashid (786–809)**, who established the ⭐ **House of Wisdom in Baghdad**. Scholars from many parts of the world and cultural backgrounds were tasked with translating the world's classical works (the slide says "knowledge") **into Arabic**.
- ⭐ **Early Islamic philosophy** ran from the **early 9th to the late 12th century AD**.
- This period's achievements had a crucial influence on **modern philosophy and science** and on the **Renaissance in Europe**.
- Islamic tradition influenced **all spheres of cultural life in Central Asia**.

#### 3.3 Sufism in Central Asia and the formation of ilm al-kalam

**Sufism** [T3-doc; T3-s10]
- ⭐ **Sufism (Tasawwuf)** is often defined as **"Islamic mysticism"**. Its practitioners are **Sufis**, and its orders are **turuq**. (The slide image reads "Sufism: Mysticism of Islam".)
- ⭐ One of its main issues is **dhikr**, the **practice of remembrance of God**.
- ⭐ **Three main Sufi orders originated and developed with Central Asian scholars: Yassawi, Kubrawi, Naqshbandi.**

**Yassawiya order** [T3-doc; T3-s11]
- ⭐ Founder: **Khoja Ahmad Yassawi**, ⭐ **born in Sayram in 1093**, ⭐ **died in 1166 in Turkestan** (city).
- **Basics of Yassawi:**
  - Sufi concepts based on ⭐ **Tawhid (oneness of God)**;
  - absolute dependence on **the Sunnah and Sharia** (doc) / on **the Qur'an and the Sunnah** (slide) ⚠️ check;
  - an order **based on Sharia**;
  - "sedate" (**khilva**; slide: khilwa) and remembrance. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the meaning of khilva/khilwa.
- Special attention went to **acquiring knowledge, generosity, the struggle against the nafs** (the doc says "gust", the slide "ego"), and **halal (permissible) labor**.
- ⭐ **The Yassawi dhikr is vocal** ("based on vocal performance"; the slide spells it "zikr").
- A Sufi idea taught in this section: the true origin of man is the universe, and human beings must be brought back to this sacred universe.
- ⭐ According to most scholars, Ahmad Yassawi wrote **"Yassawi hikmatlari"**.

**Kubrawiya order** [T3-doc; T3-s12]
- ⭐ Founder: **Najmiddin Kubra (1145–1221)**, ⭐ **born in Khiva**.
- His main body of work is the **analysis of visionary experience**. It includes a **Sufi commentary on the Quran** that he could not finish because he died in **618/1221**.
- ⭐ His popular works: **"Sharhus-sunna val-masolih", "Usuli ashara", "Favoihul-jamol"**.
- ⭐ **The essence of Kubrawiya, four ways:**
  1. Facing God by one's own will
  2. Abstaining from all kinds of tastes
  3. Gaining the satisfaction of God
  4. Loving the truth
- The order only became popular **after Kubra's death (13th c.)**. It developed greatly **outside Central Asia**, but its influence lasted only until the **15th/16th c.**, when it was **overshadowed by the Naqshbandiya** "during the Ottoman Empire"; some nominal followers survived.
- ⭐ **Kubra died during the Mongol invasion.**

**Naqshbandiya order** [T3-doc; T3-s13]
- ⭐ Founder: **Bahauddin Naqshband (1318–1389)**, ⭐ born in the village of **Qasr-i-Hinduvan near Bukhara**.
- ⭐ The slogan **"Dil ba yoru dast ba kor"** (italic in the docx) means **"Heart with God, and hands at work"** (the slide says "hands in labor").
- Man is the result of Allah's grace and **should not forget the world**: his heart should always be with Allah and his hand in labor.
- ⭐ Muslims should repeat the dhikr **orally and mentally**. (Contrast: the Yassawi dhikr is vocal.)

**Ilm al-kalam** (⭐ doc-only, not on the slides) [T3-doc]
- Muslim scholars of the **8th–11th centuries** discussed Islam's basic principles and religious-philosophical themes. This led to philosophical arguments about **the nature and attributes of God** and His relation to man and the universe.
- ⭐ The result was a new discipline within **Muslim scholasticism**: ⭐ **'Ilm al-kalam ("knowledge of kalam"), or simply kalam**.
- ⭐ **The Mutazilites began this science.** They took a principled **rational** position on some theological questions.
- ⭐ In this period, three outstanding figures appeared in three parts of the Muslim world:
  - **al-Maturidi** in **Central Asia (Samarkand)**
  - **al-Ashari** in **Iraq**
  - **al-Tahawi** in **Egypt**
  - All three tried to reconcile contradictory ideas within the rational direction of kalam and to resolve the theological crisis of their time in line with the Quran and Sunnah.
- ⭐ **Asharism** is the philosophical and religious school of Islam that developed in the **X–XI centuries**.
  - It aimed to **purify Islam of non-Islamic elements** and to harmonize religious consciousness with Islamic religious-philosophical thought.
  - ⭐ It founded **orthodox Islamic theology ("orthodox kalam")**, as opposed to **the rationalistic kalam of the Mutazilites**.
- **Nine problems** on which the Ash'arites and the Mutazilites disagreed:
  1. the concept of God and the nature of His attributes
  2. the freedom of the human will
  3. the criterion of truth and the criteria of good and evil
  4. the vision (**ruyah**) of God
  5. the uncreatedness ("uncreation") of the Qur'an
  6. "the encumbrance of creations with overwhelming tasks"
  7. the promise of reward and the threat of punishment
  8. whether God's actions have a rational or irrational basis
  9. whether God is obliged to do what is best for His creatures

**al-Maturidi** [T3-doc; T3-s9, where the slide title is spelled "Motrudi"]
- ⭐ **Abu Mansur Muhammad al-Maturidi** was born in the village of **Maturid near Samarkand**, ⭐ **around 853 AD**. This was in the reign of the Abbasid caliph **al-Mutawvakil** (**847–861**; class spelling, 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard spelling), who fought the Mu'tazilite doctrine and supported the traditional faith (doc-only).
- ⭐ **Books:** **"Kitab at-tawhid"** and **"Kitab ta'vilot al-Qur'an"**, which are of great importance among scholars.
- ⭐ In the "Moturidian" teaching, **Allah, man, faith, universe and science** are the dominant positions.
- (doc-only) He **sharply criticized the Mu'tazilite doctrine of divine justice and unity**. In his view it denied God's will and power, excluded God as the creator of human actions, and subjected Him to external coercion, so that God was not free.
- (slide-only) [T3-s9] He tried to justify religious beliefs. Relying on the Koran, he argued for the existence of **Paradise and hell** and for a **limited free will** in man, while holding that ⭐ **man is responsible for his actions before God**. This work went together with Islamic views and the philosophy of **Aristotle and his followers**.
- (slide-only) [T3-s9] General points about Islamic philosophy:
  - Greek tradition often treats political activity and wisdom as contradictory, while Islamic philosophy sees them as a form of human activity rather than politics.
  - ⭐ **Running the state in Islam is an art**, but it must follow **social justice**, and social justice stays stable through **equality**.
  - The future of humanity is shaped by forming a ⭐ **perfect human**, and morality is an indispensable part of human thought.
  - Islamic intellectuals valued **mind and logic**: "any knowledge that is not based on logic cannot be reliable and effective".

**Abu Nasr Farabi (873–950)** [T3-doc; T3-s14]

**Abu Rayhan Beruni (973–1048)** (⭐ doc-only, no slide) [T3-doc]

**Abu Ali ibn Sina (980–1037)** [T3-doc; T3-s15]

- ⭐ (doc-only) **Three forms of motion:**

#### 4.2 The state of the art of logic  [T4-doc; T4-s5–s9]

**Sa'diddin Taftazoni / Taftazani (1322–1390)** [T4-s5–s6]
- ⭐ "One of the great representatives of **logic**". He was born in the village of ⭐ **Taftazon, in the province of Nisa**.
- From childhood he studied **Arabic language, rhetoric and logic**.
- ⭐ As a professor he taught at the madrasahs of **Turkestan, Herat, Jam and Gijduvon**. His fame and works spread to the Near and Middle East.
- ⭐ At **Temur's invitation** he came to **Samarkand** and lived there until the end of his life.
- ⭐ He wrote **more than 40** scientific works.
- He discussed **cause and effect, freedom of thought, knowledge, consciousness, and logic and its functions**.
  - He accepts that **cause and effect exist in nature**.
  - On free will: good deeds derive from the essence of God.
- ⭐ **The three stages of cognition** [T4-s6]:
  1. the interaction between **the sense organs and objects**;
  2. the **acceptance** of these factors **by the human spirit**;
  3. **mental knowledge**.
- Mental (logical) knowledge is "much farther than the material basis" and arises at a **higher level than emotional knowledge**. **Logic creates the basis for new knowledge.**
- ⭐ "**Imagination and approval are forms of logic.**"
- ⭐ **Why he matters:** he developed **"Ilm al-Kalom"** and applied logical laws (the slide says "principles") to it. He also ⭐ **influenced the foundation of Ulughbek's academic school**.
  - The doc glosses Ilm al-Kalom as "Islamic scholastic (method of critical thought) theology"; the slide says "Islamic scholastic theology".

**Mir Sayyid Sharif Jurjoniy** (no dates given) [T4-s7–s9]
- ⭐ "One of the prominent philosophers of that period". He was ⭐ **born near Astrobod** city.
- ⭐ He travelled to **Istanbul, Cairo, Herat and Shiraz** to learn "the secrets of knowledge" from their scholars.
- ⭐ From **1387** he taught **logic, philosophy, cosmology, Islamic jurisprudence and literature**.
- ⭐ He wrote **more than 50 works**, many on logic, fiqh, philosophy and natural sciences. Among them:
  - ⭐ **"Al-Ta'rifāt"**
  - **"Sughrā"**
  - **"Awsat dar mantiq"**
  - **"Risāla-i wujudiyya"**
- He also wrote ⭐ **commentaries on the works of Ibn Sinā, Chaghminiy and Nasriddin Tusi**.
- His topics: existence; matter and its forms; physical and spiritual attitudes; logical thinking; **the relation between language and thinking**; the universe; humanity; intellect and cognition.
- Like Taftazoni, he held that all things are **in proportion to each other**.
- ⭐ **Simple things rest on four elements: water, fire, air, soil.** Metals, plants and wildlife come from combinations of these four [T4-s8].
- ⭐ He analysed logic and philosophy together. **Conclusions are of three types** [T4-s9]:
  - ⭐ **Syllogism:** a "private" (particular) conclusion derived from the general. It is ⭐ **the basic means of creating theoretical knowledge**.
  - ⭐ **Induction:** reasoning from the "private" (particular) to the general. Inductive knowledge is ⭐ **not always true, because human experience is not always perfect**.
  - ⭐ **Analogy:** similarity of objects; cognition **by comparison**.
- He also commented on the logic of science. He **did not separate logic from philosophy**, yet showed that logic is closely tied to **law and language**.
- ⭐ As a result, from the **14th–15th centuries** logic was taught in madrasas **together with law and linguistics**.

#### 4.3 The development of space (astronomy) and natural sciences  [T4-doc; T4-s10–s13]

### 4.1 Eastern vs Western ancient philosophy  [T2]

| Aspect | Ancient East (Egypt & Babylon · India · China) | Ancient West (Greece) |
|---|---|---|

## 6. Likely midterm short-answer / essay questions
The outlines draw only on the lecture texts and slides. Aim to put the starred facts and dates into each answer.

**Q1. Define philosophy. Explain the origin of the term, how it was interpreted in antiquity, and its role in the formation of the sciences.** [T1]
- **Definition:** studies the most common essential characteristics and fundamental principles of existence (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), values (ethics), reason (logic), mind and language. Each is a separate branch.
- **Term:** *phileo* (love) + *sophia* (wisdom) = "love of wisdom".
- **Interpretations:**
  - **Pythagoras** (c. 570–495 BC) used the term first; his festival image makes philosophy the **search for truth**;
  - **Herodotus** (484–424 BC) used the verb "to philosophize" first (= to observe);
  - **Plato** (428/427–347 BC): knowing eternal, absolute truths;
  - **Aristotle** (384–322 BC): the universal; first principles and reasons for being.
- **Role in the sciences:**
  - Philosophy is one of the most ancient disciplines and once covered any body of knowledge, so it is linked to mathematics, natural science, religion and politics.
  - Its three classical branches gave birth to the sciences: **natural** → astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, cosmology; **moral** → ethics, political philosophy; **metaphysical** → logic, mathematics, philosophy of science.
  - In the modern era, psychology, sociology, linguistics and economics became separate disciplines.

**Q2. What is the basic question of philosophy? Compare monism, dualism and pluralism, and the doctrines of sensualism, rationalism and irrationalism.** [T1]
- **Basic question:** what is primary, spirit or matter (ideal or material)? The general understanding of being depends on the answer.
- **Monism** (*monos*): one beginning.
  - *Materialistic*: the ideal comes from the material, based on natural science.
  - *Idealistic*: the material comes from the ideal. It must prove that spirit (consciousness, idea, God) created the world, which modern science cannot positively solve.
- **Dualism** (*dualis*): two equal principles. Descartes: the thinking (spirit) and the extended (matter).
- **Pluralism** (*pluralis*): many bases, e.g. earth, water, air, fire.
- **Method** (*methodos*, the path of knowledge): it originates in practical activity and reflects the laws of objective reality. Three doctrines (the slides call them methods of epistemology):
  - **sensualism** (*sensus*): the senses are the basis of knowledge;
  - **rationalism** (*ratio*): the mind is the basis;
  - **irrationalism**: reason is denied or limited.

**Q3. How did philosophical thought emerge, and what are the main features of ancient Eastern philosophy?** [T2]
- **Mythology:**
  - the first form of social consciousness;
  - Middle French "exposition of myths", a term from the 15th c.;
  - explains the world through gods and heroes;
  - posed the first questions: how the world arose and develops, what existence and death are, and "What is the universe made of?"
- **Egypt and Babylon:**
  - Babylon: c. 2300 BC under the Akkadian Empire; independent city-state in the 19th c. BC;
  - works: "Dialogue between Master and his Slave on the Essence of Life", "The song of Arfist";
  - belief in gods together with secular and scientific knowledge;
  - (slide) polytheism, magic, divinity of the ruler.
- **India:**
  - Vedas: c. 1500 BC; words of Brahma; four samhites: Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda, Adharvaveda;
  - Upanishads: the philosophical part, "mysterious knowledge";
  - schools: **astika** (Vedanta, yoga, vaysheshika, mimansa) accept the Vedas; **nastika** (Charvaka-Lokayata, a materialist school, Buddhism, Jainism) reject them.
- **China:**
  - **Confucius**: heaven rules; will of heaven = destiny; "Li" = the essence of order;
  - **Lao-Tzu** (VI–V c.): Dao's law; "Tsi" five elements; everything moves; the struggle of In and Yan moves the universe; humans must side with good and love nature.

**Q4. Trace the development of ancient Greek philosophy from the Pre-Socratics to the Hellenistic period.** [T2]
- **Span and stages:** 8th c. BC–4th c. CE in the doc, 6th c. BC–3rd c. AD on the slides. Three stages: Pre-Socratic, Classical, Hellenistic.
- **Pre-Socratics:** the first principle of the universe.
  - Thales (624–546 BC): water.
  - Anaximander: apeiron; wrote the lost "Nature".
  - Anaximenes (588–525 BC): air.
  - **Heraclitus** (520–460 BC): fire; founder of cognitive dialectics; everything moves; "eternal action is eternal change"; relative quality (seawater).
  - **Pythagoras** (580–500 BC, Samos): numbers; the first quantitative category; aristocracy.
- **Socrates:** a radical revolution, ethics before physics. The soul is man's essence; knowledge vs ignorance; the body is the soul's instrument.
- **Plato:** dialogues; "The Laws", "The Republic", "The Critique/Crito"; the **theory of ideas**; four forms of state (theocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny); the ideal state rests on **justice**.
- **Aristotle:**
  - Plato's disciple; "Organon" (logic);
  - theoretical sciences (metaphysics, mathematics, physics) vs practical ones;
  - **four causes** (material, form, creative, purpose); eternal motion;
  - "The Politics".
- **Hellenistic** (after Aristotle): **Epicurus** (341–270 BC) advanced Democritus's atomism (atoms + emptiness) and saw the state as social agreement.

**Q5. Describe the principles and main branches of Islam, the Arab conquest of Central Asia, and the significance of the Islamic Golden Age.** [T3]
- **Islam:** submission to God, obedience, peace.
  - **Qur'an**: dictated to Muhammad by Gabriel; 114 surahs.
  - **Sunnah**: the hadiths, the primary source after the Qur'an.
  - **Shari'ah**: canonical law.
  - **Five pillars**: shahadah, salat, zakat, Ramadan fast, hajj.
- **Branches:**
  - **Sunni**: caliphate; elected caliph; ~90%; Hanafi, Shafi'ite, Malikite, Hanbalite.
  - **Shia**: imamah; Imam from the Ahl al-Bayt; ~10%; Karmats, Ishmaelites, Zaidids.
  - **Kharijites**: arose in the First Fitna; only the Ibodis remain.
- **Caliphates:** Rashidun 632–661, Ummayad 661–750, Abbasid 750–1258, Ottoman 1517–1924.
- **Conquest:**
  - late 7th c.: Balkh, Chaghaniyon, Marv;
  - early 8th c.: Qutayba ibn Muslim takes Kesh, Samarqand, Tashkent, O'sh, Eastern Turkestan;
  - Movarounnahr embraced Islam.
- **Golden Age** (8th–13th c.):
  - Harun al-Rashid (786–809) and the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, where classical works were translated into Arabic;
  - early Islamic philosophy, early 9th – late 12th c.;
  - influence on modern philosophy and science, the **European Renaissance**, and all of Central Asian cultural life.

**Q6. Compare the three Sufi orders founded by Central Asian scholars and explain how ilm al-kalam formed (with the role of al-Maturidi).** [T3]
- **Sufism** (Tasawwuf): "Islamic mysticism"; dhikr = remembrance of God; orders = turuq.
- **Yassawiya:**
  - Khoja Ahmad Yassawi (1093–1166; born Sayram, died Turkestan);
  - Tawhid; Sharia; knowledge, generosity, struggle against the nafs, halal labor;
  - **vocal dhikr**; "Yassawi hikmatlari".
- **Kubrawiya:**
  - Najmiddin Kubra (1145–1221; born Khiva; died during the Mongol invasion);
  - visionary experience; works "Sharhus-sunna val-masolih", "Usuli ashara", "Favoihul-jamol";
  - four ways; later spread outside Central Asia.
- **Naqshbandiya:**
  - Bahauddin Naqshband (1318–1389; born near Bukhara);
  - "Dil ba yoru dast ba kor" (heart with God, hands at work);
  - **oral and mental dhikr**.
- **Kalam:**
  - debates of the 8th–11th c. on God's nature and attributes → 'ilm al-kalam within Muslim scholasticism, **begun by the Mutazilites** (a rational position);
  - three figures: **al-Maturidi** (Samarkand), al-Ashari (Iraq), al-Tahawi (Egypt);
  - **Asharism** (X–XI c.): orthodox kalam vs the Mutazilites' rationalistic kalam; nine disputed issues (God's attributes, free will, the vision of God, the uncreatedness of the Qur'an, …).
- **al-Maturidi:**
  - born c. 853 in Maturid near Samarkand, under caliph al-Mutawvakil;
  - "Kitab at-tawhid", "Kitab ta'vilot al-Qur'an";
  - criticized the Mu'tazilite doctrine of divine justice and unity;
  - (slide) limited free will, but man is responsible before God.

**Q7. Discuss the contributions of Farabi, Beruni and Ibn Sina to philosophy and science.** [T3]
- **Farabi** (873–950):
  - "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle; 160+ works; one of the first to classify the sciences;
  - "The Great Book of Music", "The Virtuous City", "On the Introduction of Knowledge", "Classification of Rhythms";
  - **Wujudu wājib** (God, the first reason) and **wujudi mumkin** (all beings); "no result without reason";
  - (slide) invited to Baghdad; philosophy is the only route to true knowledge; a Platonic utopia.
- **Beruni** (973–1048):
  - studied all the sciences; 150+ works, 28 survive; universe created by Allah;
  - cognition starts with the sense organs and leads to mental cognition; truth comes through **observation and experimentation**; knowledge serves people's needs.
- **Ibn Sina** (980–1037):
  - born Afshona near Bukhara, died Hamadon; Avicenna;
  - 280+ works in the doc (200+ on the slide): "The Book of Healing", "The Canon of Medicine" …;
  - followed Farabi (Wujudu wājib / wujudi mumkin); adherent of Aristotle; the soul is distinct from the body;
  - **three forms of motion** (accidental, forced, natural); motion is bound by space and time.

**Q8. Why is the Timurid period called "the highest peak of the civilization and culture of the peoples of Central Asia"? Discuss its achievements in logic, astronomy, and literature and ethics.** [T4]
- **Context:**
  - Amir Temur became ruler in 1370, after the battle with Amir Husayn;
  - Movarounnahr and Khurasan flourished, and the rulers **sponsored science and culture**;
  - in the 14th–15th c., logic, natural sciences (especially astronomy), philosophy and ethics developed.
- **Logic:**
  - **Taftazoni** (1322–1390; came to Samarkand at Temur's invitation; 40+ works): three stages of cognition; developed Ilm al-Kalom; influenced Ulughbek's school.
  - **Jurjoniy** (taught from 1387; 50+ works, incl. "Al-Ta'rifāt"): four elements; syllogism, induction, analogy. As a result, logic was taught with law and linguistics in madrasas.
- **Astronomy:**
  - **Ulugbek** (1394–1449): grandson of Temur; Samarkand observatory 1424–1429; "Zij-i Jadid-i Kuragāniy" (stars: 1018 slide / 1118 doc); methods from observation to deduction; ideas developed by Copernicus and Galileo.
  - **Ali Qushchi** (1403–1474): explained eclipses.
- **Literature and ethics** (Herat as a centre from the 2nd half of the 15th c.):
  - **Jomi** (1414–1492): "Haft Avrang"; Naqshbandi; God as "violent lover"; humanism; Navoi's teacher.
  - **Navoi**: founder of Uzbek literature; Minister of Husayn Boyqaro in 1472; "Hamsa"; "Khazoyinul maoniy" (~45,000 misras); Chagatai over Persian; love and the perfect person.
  - **Koshifiy** (1440–1505): "Ahloq-i Muhsiniy"; justice = equality.
