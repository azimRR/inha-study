Theme 6. Philosophy in Modern and Contemporary Periods
Modern period is the timeframe of world history, immediately following the Middle Ages and preceding the Contemporary era. The period can be broken into two periods:
1) The early modern period which begins in early 16 century. The period covers significant historical events such as the European Renaissance, the Age of Discovery and the Protestant Reformation. 
2) The late modern period that starts from the mid-18th century and lasts until Contemporary era. By-turn, Contemporary period begins in 1945 and covers historic events which relevant to the present time. It should be noted that some scholars include Contemporary period in Modern era as a third period.
Repositioning of the world is fundamental change in world history. West was backward society but it changed a lot. The big changes in West can be 
1) World Trade, new level of commercialization of Europe. The Europeans produce for market. Increasing of trade activities
2) Until 16 century European politics were still described with feudalism. However, by 17 century European states were beginning to develop new ranges of activities and new sense of rationalism. Central government now sent out increasing number of bureaucrats to provinces to make sure that central edicts were enforced by these provinces. New functions were also included cultural aspect. For instance, in the 17 century French monarchy decided set up institution that monitor French language, set up prisons (criminal justice), armies required set of uniforms, military logistics and, military pensions. So, their political activities were rationalized.
3) Major developments in culture; 1) establishment of printing, newspaper 2) protestant reformation led religious toleration 3) scientific revolution
Renaissance (French Renaissance, Italian Rinascimento), is the era of cultural and ideological development which took place in Western and Central Europe. The main distinctive features of the Renaissance are based on secular character, a humanistic world view, an appeal (an attempt to reestablish) to the ancient cultural heritage, a kind of "revival", “rebirth” of it (hence the name).
The culture of the Renaissance was considered the transition period from the Middle Ages to the Modern era, as the bridge. The question of the chronological boundaries of the Renaissance is complex (in Italy - XIV-XVI centuries, in other countries - XV-XVI centuries.).
The philosophical thinking of this period can be characterized as anthropocentric. The intellectual basis of the Renaissance was based on humanism, and the rediscovery of classical Greek philosophy, such as that of Protagoras, who said that "Man is the measure of all things." This new philosophical approach was well expressed in art, architecture, politics, science and literature.
In the Renaissance the individual acquires much greater independence. Hence the new self-consciousness of man and his new social position: pride and self-assertion, the consciousness of his own strength and talent become the distinctive qualities of a person. Man becomes the creator of himself. As a result, man no longer needs divine grace for his salvation. As a person realizes himself as the creator of his own life and destiny, he is also an unlimited lord over nature.
The Polish scientist Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543), one of the greatest thinkers of the Renaissance, contributed most to philosophy and science. He was born in Poland. 
The famous work of Nicolaus Copernicus – “On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres” which was written just before his death in 1543 was a major event in the history of science. 
Copernicus was famous for his philosophy of heliocentrism (representing the Sun as the center of the universe), which made fundamental changes in science. In his time, the geocentric system of the universe was considered to be generally accepted, with the Earth in the center and a complex system of orbits around it, proposed by Ptolemy about 1500 years earlier. After studying the motion of the planets, Copernicus developed the heliocentric theory of the universe (with the Sun at the center). The great discovery of Copernicus was a major achievement in the science of nature. This discovery scientifically proved inconsistency and the weakness of Christian doctrine on the structure of the world.
One of the most influential thinkers is Jordano Bruno (1548-1600). He was born near Naples, Italy. For his advanced ideas, Bruno was blamed for heresy and expelled from the church. He had to run away from Italy. He had been in exile for a long time in Switzerland, France, England and Germany. In 1592 Bruno returned to Italy, but was arrested and imprisoned by the Church Inquisition. In spite of the torture, he did not abandon his doctrine, and in the end he was sentenced to death, burned in 1600 in Rome. Bruno supported Copernicus’s heliocentric theory. 
He took a number of important theoretical conclusions, taking into account the naturalist experience, that he enriched philosophy even more. According to Bruno's teachings, true philosophy must be based on scientific experience. He opposed the scholastic doctrine. He believed that the universe is unique, material, infinite and eternal. Many of the worlds are out of the solar system. Our planet is the smallest part of the universe. Stars are the sun of other planets. The Earth is a small particle of the infinite world.
In the 17th century, many of the most significant philosophers in Europe were also engaged in mathematicians. In France, René Descartes and Blaise Pascal made major contributions to mathematics, as did Gottfried Leibniz in Germany. They believed that the method of reasoning provided the best model for acquiring the knowledge. Therefore, he was considered as the follower of rationalism. He believed that the knowledge comes from reason alone. This became the predominant belief in continental Europe for the next century. 
At the same time, a very different philosophical tradition was emerged in Britain. On the basis of their investigations Francis Bacon, John Locke came to the conclusion that our knowledge of the world comes not from reason, but experience. This view, known as empiricism, characterized British philosophy during the 17th and 18th centuries.
Despite the division between continental rationalism and British empiricism, both had in common the placing of the human at their centers: it is human being whose reason or experience leads to knowledge.
During the last second half of 19 and 20 centuries several Philosophical schools and traditions were formed and developed.
Neo-Thomism (Neo-Scholasticism) is the largest religious and philosophical tradition of Modern and Contemporary period, which rebuilt Thomas Aquinas's teaching in the Middle Ages (XIII century). It refers to Thomas Aquinas's new, modern doctrine. According to this doctrine, there should be complete harmony between science and belief. They fill one another, both of which are God's truth. Neothomists state that beliefs should be applied where there is no knowledge. But this belief should be accepted, not just faith, but also a logical perception. The philosophy of God must be proven by various logical means. Thus philosophy is to serve religion.
According to Neothomists view, the world, society is created by God, and society's progress is based on God's will. 
Hermeneutics (the term was derived from Greek word, "art of interpretation") is the theory of interpretation and understanding of texts, including texts of classical antiquity. As well as, it is the one of the philosophical traditions that emerged in the XX century. The theorist and practitioner in the field of biblical (theological), philosophical or philological hermeneutics is the follower of hermeneutics.
Although the myth of Hermes, most likely, has nothing to do with the notion of "hermeneutics," it is sometimes associated with the idea of hermeneutics.
The term hermeneutics was used most often in relation to biblical texts, then in the meaning of the doctrine of restoring the original meaning of literary monuments, which were in a distorted and partial form, incomprehensible without comment, and also in the meaning of the interpretation of any work (this includes, for example, the authors' explanatory publications) . In this sense, hermeneutics is a discipline of philological criticism.
Hermeneutics, therefore, focuses on the ideas of understanding, explanation, analysis. Society is based on the dialogue, the communication between people. During the dialogue, cultural, historical and scientific values are created. Therefore, the main purpose of philosophy is to seek out the essence and meaning of life in a dialogue between people. 
According to contemporary followers of this tradition, the correct analysis of any historical source depends on the full understanding of characteristics of taken period by researcher. There are many supporters of this doctrine in modern times.
The author of the first generalizing work on hermeneutics was the Christian thinker Aurelius Augustine (354-430). His work was called "Christian Science, or Foundations of Sacred Hermeneutics and the Art of Church Oratory"
