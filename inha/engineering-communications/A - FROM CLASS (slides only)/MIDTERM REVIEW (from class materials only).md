## 2. Rule sheets: one page per skill

### 2.1 Paraphrasing and plagiarism, as taught [1.1, 1.2; W1-3 §3.1]
- **Definition:** a paraphrase is "a restatement of a text, passage, or work giving the meaning in another form" [1.2-p4a]. ⭐ It is "really" about understanding, internalizing, and restating in your own voice [1.2-p5b].

**Steps** [1.2-p17a, p14a, p12a, p16b, p20b]
1. Reread the passage until you understand it.
2. Highlight the ideas you need.
3. Turn them into short bullet notes, as in the Raymond demo [1.2-p17b].
4. ⭐ **Without looking at the original**, write the ideas in **your own words and your own sentence structure**. For longer passages, **also change the order** of the ideas [1.2-p14a].
5. **Credit:** the author's last name + year ("According to Raymond (2005), …"). With no author, use the title; with no year, use **(n.d.)** [1.2-p7a].
6. Put any exact phrase you keep in **quotation marks**, with the page ⭐ "Remember" [1.2-p13b].
7. ⭐ **Recheck:** own words, own structure, accurate meaning [1.2-p16b].

Steps 4–7 are the **Four Rs** (Reword, Rephrase, Restructure, Recheck), plus **Cite your sources** [1.2-p20b].

**Five musts** for a paraphrase and a summary [1.2-p10b, p12a]: accurate · the author's last name · the year · your own words · your own sentence structure.

**⭐ NOT enough** [1.2-p5a]. Doing only these is still plagiarism:
- change a few words
- rearrange the words
- swap in synonyms
- omit a few words
- change the punctuation
- reorder the phrases
- reorder the sentences

**Plagiarism as taught** [1.1-p6–p9]
- Using someone else's **ideas or language** without acknowledging that you did not create them.
- It covers **ideas, words and unusual structures**, from any source (a book, a webpage, an email).
- Acknowledge the source **and** give a citation. It is sometimes seen as **intellectual theft**.
- 7.1 adds: presenting someone else's words or creative work as your own; it becomes **illegal** if it violates copyright, and you can be sued even without copying word for word or making a profit [7.1-p23].

**Class evidence** [1.1-p3–p5]
- **Legitimate:** the Lester paraphrase uses new words, a new structure (3 sentences become 2, one opening with "Since…,"), a new order, and credit.
- **Plagiarized:** the other version keeps the order, the frame and the key phrases (pink) and only swaps words. It is plagiarism **even with "(Lester 46-47)" at the end**.

**Note-taking safety** ⭐ [1.2-p15b, p16a, p20a]
- Always type the exact text, use quotation marks, and record the author, date and page.
- Keep your own ideas in a separate document.
- Haphazard note taking and haphazard cut-and-paste are "DANGEROUS".

**Quote, summarize or paraphrase?** [1.2-p14b] Limit quotes. If a brief summary carries the point, summarize. If you need detail, paraphrase. Heavy quoting suggests you didn't understand the source.

**What may stay unchanged:** the class demo keeps names and numbers ("Archives of Surgery", 34,000, 44%) [1.2-p18]. 🔎 The exact rule is in Track B.

### 2.3 Sentence patterns [2.1; W1-3 §3.3]
```
P1 Simple            IC.                                   Snow fell.
P2 Compound          IC, cc IC.                            Snow fell, but it felt warm.
                     cc = and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet
P3 Compound          IC; IC.                               Snow fell; it felt warm, though.
P4 Compound          IC; independent marker, IC.           Snow fell; therefore, it felt cold.
                     markers = therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, however, also
P5 Complex           Dependent marker + DC, IC.            When snow fell, it felt cold.
                     markers = because, before, since, while, although, if, until,
                               when, then(?), after, as, as if
P6 Compound-Complex  two ICs + one DC                      When snow fell, it felt cold, but I liked it anyway.
P7 Complex           "ANY CLUE?" (not given in class) 🔎
```
- **Identify it in 4 checks:** (1) count the ICs; (2) a dependent marker **first**, with one IC → P5, or with two ICs → P6; (3) two ICs and no DC → look at the joint: ", cc" = P2, ";" alone = P3, "; marker," = P4; (4) one IC only → P1.
- ⚠️ "then" in the P5 list may be a slip. The 7.3 list of subordinating words (after, although/though, because/since, before/until, if, when/whenever, while/as) has **no "then"** [7.3-p18]. 🔎
- **Errors that break the patterns:**
  - **Comma splice:** two ICs joined only by a comma ("He picked up the ball, he ran down the field") [5.2]. **4 fixes** [7.4-p18]: a semicolon (P3) · a comma + cc (P2) · two sentences · subordinate one clause (P5). *My note:* P4 also works.
  - **Run-on:** two ICs with no punctuation [5.2].
  - **Fragment:** a subject or verb is missing, or a dependent clause stands alone ("Although I read my e-mail") [7.3-p18]. Join it to a main clause. Fragments are "inappropriate for most business documents" 🖍.
  - After a semicolon you need a full IC (the class slip in [2.2-p8]).
- **Paraphrase with patterns:** change the pattern (P2 → P5, P1 + P1 → P4). That is "your own sentence structure" in practice [1.2-p12a], as the [1.1-p3] "Since…," shows.
- Write full stops in your answers; the slides drop them ⚠️.

### 2.4 Paragraph development patterns [2.2; W1-3 §3.4]
| Pattern | Its job (class wording, shortened) | Class example | Must include (*my note*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exemplification** | Explain a statement with examples, details, specific instances | the "poorly made" shirt | A claim + concrete, checkable details |
| **Narration** | Tell a story in chronological order with some details | the puddle jump, "Well done, Dave!" | Time order, specific moments, the point at the end |
| **Description** | Vivid words appealing to sight, hearing, taste, smell, touch | the shag rug vs the "(Not vivid)" sentence | Precise adjectives, 2+ senses |
| **Process** | How to do or make something, in steps | the steak ("To begin,…") | Step signals, ordered steps, reasons |
| **Cause and Effect** | "Detective work" on causes or effects | the fatal cliff-edge selfie | The cause → the chain of effects |
| **Comparison and Contrast** | How two things are similar, and how they differ | "Just as driving recklessly…, so does consuming energy drinks…" | Both similarities and differences; "Just as…, so…", "by contrast" |
| **Definition** | An informal definition of a term | "What is death?" + Aristotle | A question → a definition, possibly from an authority ("According to…") |
| **Classification** | Sort many things into categories | data levels: 5 in government, 4 commercial | One basis for sorting, named categories |
| **Argument/Persuasion** | Make a challenging point and defend it with solid points; a **claim/thesis backed with evidence** | definition only (UNC) | A claim + evidence (+ an answer to an objection) |

- **Structure:** the slides don't define topic sentence, support or closing sentence 🔎. The class examples open with the main statement, give specific support, and often end with the point. 7.4 does recommend "paragraphs with topic sentences" [7.4-p11], but doesn't define them.
- **A developed paragraph** (*my recipe from the class examples*): main statement → 3–5 specific supports that do the pattern's job → transitions → a closing point. Vary the sentence patterns (P2–P6).
- ⚠️ The [5.2] limit of 1–2 sentences per paragraph is a **news** rule. Don't apply it to a "developed paragraph" task.

### 2.5 Quotation-mark rules [QM; 1.2; W1-3 §3.5]
| # | Rule | Mini-example |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | A full-sentence quote **starts with a capital** ⭐ "must" | She said, "**W**e start at nine." |
| R2 | A fragment or phrase quote **starts in lowercase** | the phrase "**d**on't win in practice" |
| R3 | **[Brackets]** for words you add to clarify | "He [Jeff] will be a great dad," |
| R4 | A quote inside a quote takes **'single'** marks | I said, "Dad told me, 'Always give it your all.'" |
| R5 | Quotation marks can show **irony or sarcasm** | he "cares" about us |
| R6 | They can **highlight a word as a word** | I wrote "your" when I meant "you're." |
| R7 | **Titles** of songs, essays, poems, short stories, one-act plays, chapters, TV episodes, articles | the article "…" |
| R8 | ⭐ American English: **periods and commas inside** the closing mark, single or double | "…rain." (British: "…rain".) |
| R9 | **A comma before the opening mark**; if the quote comes first, a **comma inside** the closing mark | Tom said, "…" / "…," said Tom. |
| R10 | If a **parenthetical citation follows**, the period goes **after** it | "…humid" (16). |
| R11 | **? and !** go inside **only if they belong to the quote** | …"Don't Stop Believing"? / "Stop!" shouted… |

- **Harvard (APA):**
  - **H1:** a quote needs the exact text, quotation marks, the author's last name, the year and the page.
  - **H2:** ≤39 words → quotation marks; ≥40 → an indented **block** with no marks.
  - **H3:** two models: `According to Strauss (2008), "…" (p. C01).` and `"…" (Strauss, 2008, p. C01).`
  - **H4:** an **ellipsis** marks words left out ("is…asking").
  - **H5:** [brackets] mark added words.
  - **H6:** ⭐ a quoted phrase inside a paraphrase or summary **must** keep its quotation marks.
  - **H7:** no author → the title; no year → (n.d.)
- **End-of-quote checks, in order:** (1) citation follows? → close the quote, add the (citation), then the period; (2) ? or ! → inside only if it belongs to the quote; (3) period or comma → inside; (4) quote first → comma inside + speaker tag; (5) speaker first → comma before the opening mark, and a capital for a full sentence.
- ⭐ **The class Activity's key contrast:** item 2, *…short story "The Tell-Tale Heart"?* puts the **? outside**; item 7, *…asked, "Have you seen the new Sherlock Holmes movie?"* puts it **inside** [QM-p3].
- **Order of the speaker tag.** QM and the SPQ sample use "…," **said Tom** [QM-p2; 1.3]. The news checklist wants **quote → speaker → verb**: "…," **he said** [5.2]. *My note:* "…," **Karimova said.** satisfies both, so use it everywhere.
- ⚠️ Answer-key item 4 leaves out the comma after the inner "said". Follow R9 and put the comma in.

### 2.6 Media law [ML; 7.1-p22–p25; W4-6 §3.1]
*The slides cover **U.S. law** only. 🔎 Uzbekistan's law is not covered.*

**Definitions**
- **Defamation** = "the action of damaging the good reputation of someone; slander or libel" [ML-p3].
- ⭐ **Slander** = a false **spoken** statement damaging a reputation · ⭐ **Libel** = a **published** false statement damaging a reputation, "a written defamation" [ML-p4]. Memory hook: **S**lander = **S**poken.
- 7.1's wording: defamation is the "intentional public communication of false statements" that damage character or reputation; libel = written, slander = spoken [7.1-p25].
- Defamation is a **tort** (a civil wrong, not a crime in most U.S. states). The **plaintiff** (the person defamed) sues the **defendant** for damages [ML-p5].

**⭐ What a plaintiff must show** [ML-p6]: the statement was **Published · False** (objectively; "saying one's opinion does not count") **· Injurious** (fired, lost customers, shunned, harassed online) **· Unprivileged**. Mnemonic: **P-F-I-U**.

**⭐ The four categories of damaging statements** [ML-p8]: accusing someone of a **crime** · damaging someone in their **public office, profession or occupation** · accusing someone of **serious immorality** · accusing someone of a **loathsome (contagious) disease**.

**⭐ Three traditional defenses** [ML-p9–p14]
1. **Truth**, "**the best defense** against libel" (on two slides). On matters of public concern, the **burden of proof is on the plaintiff**.
2. **Privilege.**
   - **Absolute** for officials (legislators, judges, executives) acting in their official capacities, and for participants in a judicial proceeding.
   - **Qualified** (also called neutral reporting or conditional privilege) for **reporters**, *if* the coverage is **full, fair and accurate**. You may quote anything the U.S. president says.
3. **Fair comment and criticism:** **opinions** about public officials and figures, and about the "**public offerings**" of anyone in the public eye. It holds only if you don't **misstate the facts** behind your opinion and don't **imply hidden damaging information**.

**Two other standards** [ML-p15]
- **Actual malice** = "actual knowledge that the statement is false or reckless disregard for the truth".
- **Negligence tests** are only named. 🔎 Who must prove actual malice is not taught.

**⭐ Ken Paulson's keys** [ML-p16]: Have I reported **fully? factually? fairly? in good faith?**

**Invasion of privacy** = "a violation of a person's right to be left alone" [ML-p17]
- **4 risks** [p17–p18]:
  1. physical **intrusion**, "closely related to trespass"
  2. a misleading story or photo that puts someone in a ⭐ **false light**
  3. ⭐ **true but offensive** private facts
  4. using a **name or picture in an ad**, or for trade
- **2 defenses:** **consent** (don't exceed it) · **newsworthiness** (legitimate public interest) [p19].
- **3 exceptions**, where newsworthiness won't protect you: **trespassing · false light · unwanted publicity** [p19].
- **Life magazine case:** staffers posed as patients to enter a "faith healer's" home. They lost, even though they were working with the district attorney [p20]. Enter **only when invited** by the owner or renter, and never by **deception**.
- **False light example:** a true photo of a careful pedestrian hit by a careless driver is reused in a story about "careless pedestrians". That can bring libel, false light, or both [p21].

**Copyright** [ML-p22; 7.1-p23]
- Protected **from the moment a work is fixed in tangible form**, published or not. It lasts for the **life of the author + 70 years**. Works for hire and anonymous works: **95 years from publication or 100 from creation, whichever is shorter** (🔎 check the figure).
- **Fair use** (ML): "small excerpts" may be quoted without permission.
- 7.1 adds: fair use has **no precise limits**, and you can only claim it **after you are sued**. You can't pre-label content "fair use".

**Political correctness** = the belief that speech offensive to groups' sensibilities should be eliminated, by regulations or penalties if necessary [ML-p23]. 🔎 How this applies to word choice in news writing is not taught.

**Answer template** (W4-6 §3.1): (1) name the problem with the class term → (2) give the class definition → (3) apply the elements or categories to the facts → (4) name the defense or exception → (5) say what the writer should do → (6) finish with Paulson's four questions.

### 2.7 News values [5.1; W4-6 §3.2]
- ⭐ **What news is:** three criteria: **Relevance · Usefulness · Interest** [5.1-p3].
- **Elements of a good story** [5.1-p4]: **Impact · Conflict · Novelty · Prominence · Proximity · Timeliness · Engagement · Solutions**. The last three are listed but not explained 🔎.

| Element | Question to ask | Class warning or tip |
|---|---|---|
| Impact | How many people are affected, and how seriously? "The wider and heavier the impact, the better the story." | Dull but important stories need the next three elements (conflict, novelty, prominence) [p5] |
| Conflict | Is there a struggle between people, among nations, or with natural forces? | Don't **overdramatize or oversimplify** [p6] |
| Novelty | Is it unusual or bizarre? | Can be exaggerated [p8] |
| Prominence | "**Names make news.** The bigger the name, the bigger the news." | Prominence and novelty are often exaggerated into "news" that lacks relevance [p8] |
| Proximity | Is it close to home? How does it relate to the reader's community, including communities of interest (a sport, a hobby, an issue)? | It feeds the localized lead [6.1-p22] |

- **How values steer the lead** (*my note, W4-6*): a prominent person → immediate ID · an ordinary person → delayed ID · a local link → localized lead · something unusual → a feature or partial-summary lead · wide impact → a summary lead with the numbers up front.

### 2.8 The inverted-pyramid checklist [5.2; W4-6 §3.3]
```
████████████████████  LEAD: 1 sentence, 30–35 words max, most important facts
  ████████████████    2nd paragraph: develops an idea from the lead (no chronology)
    ████████████      supporting facts + attributed quotes (≤2 sentences each)
      ████████        background, less important details
        ████          least important (e.g. the timeline)
```
- **Lead formula** (*my note from the class examples*): [who or what] + [what happened / the result] + [where] + [when] + (how and why if known) + [attribution at the end: "…, police said Tuesday"].

**Checklist** [5.2]
- [ ] ⭐ **Descending order of importance**.
- [ ] ⭐ **Lead:** one sentence, **30–35 words max**, the most important information, specific facts.
- [ ] ⭐ **Second paragraph:** develops an idea from the lead; does **not** drop into a chronological narrative.
- [ ] ⭐ **Attribution:**
  - all major information attributed, unless it is commonly known or the information itself implies the source;
  - no strings of quotes;
  - each quote **≤ two sentences**;
  - order **quote → speaker → verb**: "John did not go with her," he said.
- [ ] ⭐ **AP style**: "Always". Check numbers, dates, locations, titles. 🔎 The rules themselves aren't taught.
- [ ] **Grammar:**
  - pronoun agreement;
  - it / its / it's;
  - no "**it is… / there is… / there are…**" ("passive and vague");
  - **past tense**;
  - no comma splices or run-ons;
  - no apostrophe-s plurals.
- [ ] ⭐ **Short paragraphs:** three sentences is probably too long; more than three is definitely too long.
- [ ] **Wordiness:** verbiage, redundancy, repetition.
- [ ] ⭐ **Name, title:**
  - title before the name → **no commas** ("Game warden Brad Fisher arrested…");
  - name before the title → **commas** ("Brad Fisher, the game warden, …").
- [ ] ⭐ **Transitions** tie the paragraphs together.
- [ ] **"Don't copy the wording of the information sheet."**
- [ ] **Names** spelled correctly (check twice).

### 2.9 All lead types, one class example each [6.1, 6.2; W4-6 §3.4]

**News leads** [6.1]
| Lead | Class definition (shortened) | Use it when… | One class example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Summary** | The most important info (result or climax); who, what, where, when, usually how and why; a "**thesis**" in the first sentence; no minute details | ⭐ the **whole** matters more than any part | "DOVER – A speeding car passed a minivan… killing four people, the Ohio Highway Patrol said Friday." |
| **Partial summary** | Omits a few details; sets the tone through word choice | ⭐ feature stories and news stories with **a particular angle**; a full lead would be crowded; to create suspense | "Oprah Winfrey is keeping Miami Valley Hospital busy." |
| **Multiple element / roundup** | Summary leads with much information on ⭐ **separate incidents or aspects linked by time, location, type or cause** | ⭐ **two or more actions of equal importance**. Write a main clause for the common point and subordinate phrases for the rest, *or* put the top item in the lead and the others in paragraph 2 | "State police reported at least 10 people died in Ohio traffic accidents over the weekend, including three who died… in Greene County." |
| **Localized** | A summary or partial summary stressing the **local aspect** of a wider event; "Proximity is an important news value" | The story has a link to your readers' community | "More than 40 people, including a woman from Dayton, were killed in an air show crash…" |
| **Follow-up** | The **latest and most important** information about an earlier event, plus **background in a word or phrase**; "Most stories are follow ups" | An earlier story develops | "A 6-year-old boy taken from a train station platform Monday was found unharmed…" |
| **Immediate identification** | The who is **named** | The person is **prominent**, or what happened to them *is* the story | "President Bush and Polish President Aleksander Kwasniewski agreed…" |
| **Delayed identification** | The who is **described** by title, age, residence or past notoriety; the name comes later | The person isn't well known; the **what** matters more | "A Troy accountant went on trial Monday… for failing to repay $1.6 million…" |
| **Feature** | "Leads with **flair**"; partial summaries that stress **novelty**; precise information + theme, tone and genre through language, rhythm and style | Features; a novel person or situation. Formats named: anecdote, description, staccato, zinger, flashback, direct address, representative example (🔎 the undefined ones) | "In preparation for tonight's poetry reading, Charles Bukowski is out in the parking lot, vomiting." |

- **Partial-summary pitfalls** [6.1-p11]: the significance is vague · readers are confused about the subject · too little precise information to hook them.

**Feature leads** [6.2-p6–p9]. These six "work regardless of publication, topic or audience".
| Lead | Class definition | Watch out | One class example (shortened) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anecdote** 🖍 | A little story about **people** in a **specific place at a specific time**; the "chocolate chips of feature writing"; the most used; it **illustrates rather than dictates** | It needs a real person, time and place | "Friday night. Homecoming. An hour before kickoff." |
| **Scenario** 🖍 | **Recreates a place** so readers "see" the backdrop | **Avoid too much description**; use a light hand and precise details; the present tense gives **immediacy** | Meryl Streep, "Oooh, they have oysters", at **4:20** on the mezzanine of Union Station |
| **Shock** 🖍 | A **shock element** grabs attention | Only with **truly surprising** information; can be combined with other types | 47% of British men would give up sex for a plasma TV |
| **Blind** 🖍 | **Omits a key piece of information** (usually the name) to create curiosity | Name the person soon after (here in sentence 3) | "Business majors can party." (Duff McKagan) |
| **Direct / indirect quote** 🖍 | A **noteworthy** quote that **sums up the main idea**; the indirect version gives the essence without the exact words | Only if the quote can carry the story | "The trouble with writing at home, says author Janette Oke…, is the ever-present lure of housework." |
| **Direct address** 🖍 | Speaks to the reader as "**you**", like a conversation, often friendly advice | It can sound **condescending** or **assume** the scenario is familiar, so know your readers | No example in the handout ⚠️ |

- **⭐ McKinney's 4 ways to begin** [6.2-p5–p6]: tell a story · let someone else talk · thrust us into the action · tell us what you're going to tell us.
- **Keep apart:**
  - summary (the whole) vs multiple element (equal parts);
  - immediate (named) vs delayed (described) identification;
  - **delayed ID** (unknown person, the *what* matters) vs **blind** (any person, held back for **curiosity**) (W4-6 §2.5 O).

### 2.10 Where to begin and the four jobs of a lead [6.2; W4-6 §3.5]
- ⭐🖍 **Four jobs** [6.2-p1]: **attract attention · introduce the topic · set the tone · establish the point of view** (third person, or first person with "I").
- 🖍 **Why it matters:** if the opening doesn't grab readers, the content, "regardless of its importance", may stay "forever unread" [p1].
- **Length:** the opening sentence of a newspaper feature may be **12–15 words**; a magazine's may be 2–3 times that, depending on the audience [p1]. A **news** lead: ≤30–35 words [5.2].
- **Three names:** **lead** (leads readers in) · **hook** (hooks attention) · **introduction** (introduces the topic, tone and point of view) [p3].
- 🖍 **The airplane rule:** articles crash at **takeoff and landing**. The ending should be **smooth, not abrupt; fast-paced, not prolonged**. ⭐ **Know your ending before you begin**, as a pilot knows the destination [p2–p3].
- **Bookends:** the lead gives a **direction**; the ending gives a **destination**. Arrange the middle logically [p3].
- **Nutgraf (billboard paragraph):** tells the story "in a nutshell". It goes **immediately after a creative lead**, and many editors expect one in every article [p4–p5].
- **Summary leads in features:** the "old workhorse". The Rolling Stone lead (**29 words**) answers all six questions; critics call such leads "Boring!", but they make up for it in **information** [p3–p5].
- **Choose by** [p5]:
  - **publication:** short pieces → summary lead;
  - **topic:** how-to gets to the point; profiles and complex issues can open more slowly;
  - **audience:** readers who read for entertainment like description; readers who read for information like a straight approach.
- 🖍 **Process:** writers "**labor for hours**" over a lead and mine their notes for anecdotes, provocative quotations and surprising facts. Write the lead first or last, whichever works for you [p3].

### 2.11 Finding ideas and tightening an angle [6.3, 6.4; W4-6 §3.6–§3.7]
- ⭐ **Original-idea test** [6.3]: answer "**yes**" to **at least a majority** of 10 questions. *My note:* that means at least 6.
  - **Fresh?** (1) No books on it yet · (6) intelligent insight, not the obvious · (9) it questions the **conventional wisdom**.
  - **Will readers care?** (2) it fits a magazine's special-interest readers · (3) a fundamental life issue (death, love, sickness, money, careers, health) affecting **millions** · (7) drama or conflict · (8) colourful **anecdotes**, human interest.
  - **Can I write it?** (4) a strong, central unifying theme · (5) the theme in **one sentence with an action verb** · (10) access to participants, keen observers or experts.
- **Where ideas come from:** read continually, including what few others read (professional quarterlies, association newsletters, academic journals, annual reports, almanacs) [6.3]. 🔎 The rest of the list is not in the handout.
- 🖍 **Principle** [6.4]: "A **limited tale told clearly** has more impact than a sweeping story that lacks depth and insight." The more a topic has been covered, the sharper and fresher the angle must be.
- **Sidebar 3.1** (too broad → tighter): Losing Weight → "Teacher Tells How She Lost 100 Pounds" · Quitting Smoking → "Can Hypnotism Help You Quit the Habit?" · Improving Your Home's Security → "An Ex-Burglar Tells How to Burglar-Proof Your Home" · plus budget travel → "Five Ways to Find Cheap Hotels…" and smartphones → "Don't Buy These Extra Features…".
  - *My note:* each tighter angle adds **a person with first-hand experience · one method (often as a question) · a number · a contrarian twist**.
- 🖍 **Angle tree** [6.4]:
  1. Put the broad topic in a **center circle**.
  2. Brainstorm **four angles** around it.
  3. 🖍 Covering all of them means you will "**lack depth**".
  4. 🖍 Choose **one main angle** and **one sidebar**.
  5. Decide **whom to interview** and what background research to do.
  - Class example (Fig. 3.2), "Saving time": outsourcing home cooking · how to do everything faster · cut down on telephone calls · how to go home earlier. Each angle suits a different magazine.
- 🖍 **A focused angle has unity, action and concreteness.** **Unity** = everything hangs together around a central idea. "Action" and "concreteness" are cut off in the handout 🔎; the nearest class hint is Q5's action verb.

### 2.12 Week 7 frameworks [W7a; W7b]

#### 2.12a The communication process [7.1-p2–p14; 7.4-p5–p6]
- **Communication** = transferring information **and meaning** between senders and receivers through media. It succeeds only when understanding is **transferred or negotiated** 🖍 [7.1-p2].
  - Fig. 1.1: **transmitted** meaning (taken at face value) · **negotiated** meaning (talked through) · **re-created** meaning (the receiver builds her own, e.g. from the word "think") [7.1-p3].
- 🖍 **The 8-step conventional model** [7.1-p8–p9]: idea → **encode** → **medium** (oral, written, visual) → **channel** → receive → **decode** → respond → **feedback**. **Medium = the form; channel = the delivery system.**
- 🖍 **The 7.4 two-person model** [7.4-p5–p6]: the sender perceives → interprets → chooses → **encodes** → transmits through a channel → the receiver perceives → **decodes** → interprets → chooses a response → encodes → **feedback**. Feedback can be direct and immediate or indirect and delayed, verbal or nonverbal.
- 🖍 **Barriers** [7.1-p10]: noise and distractions · competing messages · filters · channel breakdowns.
  - **Noise** can strike at **every step**: **physical** (static, a mower, bad handwriting) or **psychological** (disliking the speaker, worry, a mind already made up) [7.4-p6].
  - 🖍 **Channel overload** = the channel can't carry all the messages. **Information overload** = more than the *human* receiver can handle [7.4-p6; 7.1-p15].
- 🖍 **The receiver must:** **sense → select → perceive** the message (selective attention filters out the rest) · then **remember** it, **be able** to act, and **be motivated** to act [7.1-p11–p13].
  - **5 principles for getting noticed:** expectations · user-friendly · familiarity · empathy · compatibility.
  - **Fig. 1.6:** the less experience you share with the audience, the more background you must give [7.1-p12].
- 🖍 **Conventional ("we talk, you listen") vs social model ("let's have a conversation")** [7.1-p13–p14]:
  - The social model is **interactive, conversational, open to all**.
  - Fig. 1.7 pairs: publication vs conversation · lecture vs discussion · intrusion vs permission · control vs influence · information hoarding vs sharing · hierarchical vs egalitarian.
  - **2 disadvantages:** less control · greater complexity.
- 🖍 **Networks** [7.1-p6–p8]: the **formal** network flows downward · upward · horizontal · diagonal. The **informal** network is healthy, but rampant rumours mean the formal one is failing.

#### 2.12b Effective messages, purposes, costs [7.1-p4–p6; 7.3-p3–p10]
- 🖍 **7.1, five characteristics:** **practical · factual · concise · clear · persuasive** [7.1-p4].
- 🖍 **7.3, five criteria:** **clear · complete · correct · saves the reader's time · builds goodwill** [7.3-p9]. "No single set of words will work in all possible situations": it depends on the writer, audience, purposes and situation.
- 🖍 **Three purposes:** **inform · request or persuade · build goodwill**. Most messages have several [7.3-p8].
- 🖍 **School vs business writing** [7.3-p5–p6]

| Dimension | School | Business |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Show what you learned | Meet an **organizational need** |
| Audience | An instructor paid to read | Readers who read only what seems important, relevant, interesting |
| Information | New to you | **New to the reader** |
| Organization | Thesis → evidence → conclusion | **Main point up front**, for the reader's psychological needs |
| Style | Formal; long words rewarded | **Friendly, not formal**; short words |
| Design | Long paragraphs, double spacing | **Skimmable**: headings, lists |
| Visuals | Rarely | Tables and graphs where useful |

- 🖍 **Cost:** a one-page letter takes 54 minutes ≈ **$21.15** at $23.50 an hour. **Bad writing wastes time:** it is slower to read · needs more revision · drags out discussions · delays action [7.3-p8–p9].
- 🖍 **4 myths**, e.g. "I'll just pick up the phone": "**If it isn't in writing, it didn't happen**" [7.3-p5].
- 🖍 **Professionalism** = performing at a high level with **purpose and pride**. Six elements: be the best · dependable · a team player · respectful · positive · ethical [7.1-p4, p6].

#### 2.12c Audiences [7.3-p7; 7.4-p1–p14]
- 🖍 **Internal** (superiors, peers, subordinates) vs **external** (customers, suppliers, unions, stockholders, potential employees, government agencies, the press, the public) [7.3-p7].
- ⭐🖍 **Five audiences** [7.4-p2–p4]:

| Audience | Role (Instant Replay box) |
|---|---|
| **Initial** | Receives it first and routes it on; may have assigned it |
| **Gatekeeper** | **Can stop** it before the primary audience sees it |
| **Primary** | **Decides or acts** on it |
| **Secondary** | **Comments** on it or **implements** it |
| **Watchdog** | Can't stop it, but has **political, social or economic power** and may act later |

  - One person can play two roles; a supervisor is often both the initial audience and the gatekeeper [p3].
  - Fig. 2.1, a marketing plan: initial and gatekeeper = the writer's boss · primary = the client's executive committee · secondary = the client's marketing staff and the artists who implement.
- 🖍 **What to analyze** [7.4-p6–p10]: **knowledge · demographics** (measurable) **· personality** (MBTI) **· values and beliefs** (psychographics) **· past behavior**. In organizations, add the **discourse community** (preferred channels, formats and styles; topics; what counts as evidence) and **organizational culture** (goals, values, how people get ahead, formality). Use common sense + **empathy** (putting yourself in someone else's shoes and feeling with them).
- **4 tactful reminders** [7.4-p7]: "As you know…" · spell out an acronym the first time · a brief in-text definition · put known information in a subordinate clause.
- **MBTI uses** [7.4-p8]: introverts → a written message; extraverts → a call or meeting; sensing types → details in sequence; intuitive types → the big picture first.
- **Adapt** [7.4-p11–p12]:
  - **strategy:** make the action easy; protect the reader's ego;
  - **organization:** the main point first, **except** with a reluctant reader or bad news;
  - **style:** easy words, no red-flag words, conversational;
  - **document design** and **visuals**.
- **Mixed audiences:** meet the **gatekeeper's and the primary audience's** needs first · executive summary up front, extras in appendices · **avoid "you"** · more formal for mixed or **international** readers [7.4-p12].

#### 2.12d The "you" attitude [7.1-p8, p11; 7.4-p14]
- 🖍 **Audience-centered approach = the "you" attitude:** understand and respect the audience, and make the message meaningful **to them**. It is about "you" (the receiver), not "me" (the sender) [7.1-p8].
- Learn their **beliefs, education, status, communication style, personal and professional concerns**. If you can't, imagine yourself in their position [7.1-p8]. Empathy is "the essence of the 'you' attitude" [7.1-p11]. **Emotional intelligence** = reading others' emotions accurately and managing your own [7.1-p8].
- **In any channel** [7.4-p14]: adapt to the audience · show their **benefits** · overcome **objections** · use the you-attitude and positive emphasis · use visuals · **say exactly what the audience should do**. 🔎 Sentence-level rules for the you-attitude and positive emphasis aren't taught (the reading points to Modules 6–7).
- **Class contrast:** "most of you just sat and stared" (accusatory, about "me") vs an upbeat line on the meeting's value for the team [7.8.2 (d)].

#### 2.12e PAIBOC [7.3-p10–p13; 7.4-p4–p5]
| Letter | Ask before writing | Tip from class |
|---|---|---|
| **P** Purposes | What must the message do for the organization and for you? What should readers know, think, feel or do? | List the major **and** minor purposes. The audience shapes *how*, not *what* |
| **A** Audience(s) | Who are they, how do they differ, which traits matter **for this message**? | Spot the subgroups in a group audience |
| **I** Information | Which points must be included? | Check the draft against the list. To de-emphasize a point, put it in the middle and keep it brief |
| **B** Benefits | Which reasons and **reader benefits** support you? | Find **at least 5**; they needn't be money; adapt them to the reader |
| **O** Objections | Which negatives must you **deemphasize or overcome**? | Find an advantage linked to the negative |
| **C** Context | How will the context affect the response? | Your relationship, morale, the economy, **time of year**, news |

- 🖍 Five of the six (A, I, B, O, C) concern the **audience** [7.4-p4].
- 🖍 **5 situation questions** [7.3-p10]: what's at stake, and to whom? · should you send a message? · which channel? · what to say? · how to say it?
- ⭐ A good solution **solves the organizational problem and meets people's psychological needs** [7.3-p12].

#### 2.12f Channel choice [7.4-p13–p14; 7.3-p5, p10; 7.1-p11, p15]
- **Channels vary in 7 ways:** speed · accuracy · cost · number of messages carried · number of people reached · efficiency · ability to promote goodwill [7.4-p13].

| Use **written** to… | Use **oral** to… |
|---|---|
| present many specific details of a law, policy or procedure | answer questions, resolve conflicts, **build consensus** |
| present complex financial data | use emotion to persuade |
| **minimize undesirable emotions** | get **immediate** action |
| create a record, be visible [7.3-p5] | focus attention; **modify a proposal** on the spot; make group decisions [7.3-p10] |

- **Formality:** paper > email; a scheduled meeting > a call or a hallway chat. **Important message → a more formal channel. Paper is usually better for a first-time contact** [7.4-p14].
- Use **multiple channels** for big audiences or very important messages (brief the key players before the meeting) [7.4-p14].
- Respect the audience's and the discourse community's channel expectations [7.1-p11; 7.4-p14]. If a text exchange turns confused or negative, **reconnect** by phone or in person [7.1-p15].

#### 2.12g Ethics and law in messages [7.1-p22–p25] (brief)
- 🖍 **Ethical communication** includes all the information the audience needs and is **not deceptive in any way**.
- **3 unethical forms:** **withholding** (transparency; native advertising; stealth marketing) · **distorting** (selective quotes, averages that hide extremes, trend tricks, altered images, deep fakes) · **plagiarizing**.
- 🖍 **Dilemma** (both options defensible, or both grey) vs **lapse** (clearly wrong, e.g. promising a date you know you can't meet).
- **5 questions:** honorable intention? · all the needed information? · the effect on everyone? · respecting rights? · a better approach? Tie-breaker: could you explain the choice comfortably to someone you respect?
- **7 legal areas:** promotion (bait-and-switch) · contracts · employee communication · intellectual property · financial reporting · **defamation** · transparency (influencer disclosure).
- Fig. 1.9 vs 1.10, the Pegasus memo: spin vs a neutral subject line, the method, the full findings, and recommendations kept separate.

#### 2.12h Teams: Tuckman, conflict, collaboration [7.5-p2–p11]
- 🖍 **Team** = 2+ people with a shared goal who **depend on one another** [7.5-p2]. Types: committee · problem-solving (task force) · project · creative · cross-functional (counters the "silo effect").
- 🖍 **9 advantages**, e.g. information, buy-in, performance.
- 🖍 **4 disadvantages:** **groupthink** (members withhold contrary opinions) · **hidden agenda** · cost · overload [p3].
- ⭐ **8 traits of effective teams:** shared purpose · a clear, challenging goal · belief in the effort · a balanced mix · the right size · **psychological safety** · the team's needs first · **open, honest communication**. "All eight rely on communication" [p4].
- 🖍 **Roles:** **task-oriented** (the work) vs **team-maintenance** (the people); a team needs both [p4].
- 🖍⭐ **Tuckman** [p4–p5]:
  - **Forming:** roles settle and a leader emerges.
  - **Storming:** disagreement.
  - **Norming:** conflicts resolved; norms set and enforced.
  - **Performing:** productive work; new conflict can send the team back to storming.
  - **Adjourning:** a temporary team disbands and records advice.
  - ⚠️ The text says "four or five phases"; the margin says "usually … five".
- **Conflict** [p5–p6]:
  - **constructive** (surfaces issues, involvement, ideas) vs **destructive** (diverts energy, destroys morale, polarizes; win-lose or lose-lose);
  - 🖍 **5 steps:** worth addressing? → examine yourself → find the true origin → **common ground** → choose a strategy: 🖍 **avoid** (can be the most efficient) · **accommodate** · **compromise** · **collaborate (win-win)**;
  - always with **calm, respectful** words and **active listening**.
- 🖍 **Virtual teams** (2+ locations) [p6–p7]:
  - **Challenges:** technology limits · isolation ("out of the loop") · "us vs them" · missed chance meetings.
  - **Tips:** keep it small (≲10) · the best technology · **one purpose per tool** · special care for isolated members · **don't rely only on writing**.
- 🖍 **Collaborative writing** [p7–p8]:
  - **plan, research and outline together, then write separately**;
  - a detailed outline before dividing the work; **one person does the final revision pass**;
  - clarify roles up front; release "emotional ownership";
  - **5 tips:** clear goals · map the work · play to strengths · frequent checkpoints · appreciate different styles.
- 🖍⭐ **Constructive feedback** = about the **process and outcomes, not the person** [p8].
  - **Table 2.2** [p9]: think changes through · discuss **improvements, not flaws** · focus on what the writer can control · **be specific** · keep it **impersonal** · verify understanding · time it well · say what your feedback can't cover.
  - **Destructive feedback** = complaining or bullying with **no guidance** ("a confusing mess").

#### 2.12i Meetings and minutes [7.5-p11–p18, p35]
- 🖍 **First: is the meeting necessary?** Then 4 tasks [p12]:
  1. **Purpose:** picture the best outcome; it must be stateable **in one sentence**.
  2. **Participants:** only those needed; **more than 10–12** becomes unmanageable.
  3. **Venue and time:** seating and comfort; mornings can be more productive.
  4. **Agenda,** sent early. It answers **3 questions**: what must we do to reach our goals? · which issues matter most to everyone? · what information must be available?
  - There is a planning agenda (Fig. 2.5) and a one-slide presentation agenda (Fig. 2.6).
- 🖍 **Room layouts** [p13]:
  - **conference room** (small; everyone talks)
  - **U-shape** (mid-sized; the presenter walks inside the U)
  - **theater** (large; little interaction)
  - **classroom** (theater with desks)
  - **breakout** (small groups)
- **During** [p12–p15]:
  - keep it on track; follow agreed rules (**parliamentary procedure**, *Robert's Rules of Order* 🔎)
  - make room for quiet people and "think-aloud" people; participate, but **don't interrupt**
  - use devices respectfully
  - **close effectively:** summarize conclusions and actions; check that everyone agrees
- 🖍⭐ **Minutes** = a written summary of the **important information presented and the decisions made** [p15]. They contain:
  - who was present, and who was invited but absent
  - start and end times
  - **all major decisions**
  - **all task assignments**
  - **deferred** subjects
  - an **objective** summary naming who made the major points
  - attachments
  - Recording all of these matters more than the format.
- 🖍 **Fig. 2.7 layout** [p16]:
  - **Header:** title · date and time · called by · type of meeting · facilitator · note taker · attendees.
  - **For each agenda item:** presenter → discussion → **action items table** (✓ task | **person responsible** | **deadline**).
- **Afterwards:** distribute the minutes to participants and **affected people who missed the meeting**, and make sure task assignments are clear [p15].
- **Virtual meetings** [p18], key tips: a well-defined task · prestudy material · assigned roles · log in on time · be present (no multitasking) · mute · summarize each item · **check in with each person by name**.
- 🔎 The class videos on meeting notes and minutes were not covered.

#### 2.12j Listening [7.5-p18–p24]
- 🖍⭐ **Why it is hard** [p19–p20]:
  - **3 unique challenges:** **real time** · **invisibility** · **sound-to-language conversion**.
  - **5 listener behaviours:** poor self-management · **idle brain power** (the brain processes language **3–4×** faster than speech) · the wrong style · barriers to physical reception · flawed recall.
- ⭐ **3 steps** [p20–p23]:
  1. **Minimize barriers.** External ones (environment, channel) and internal ones (multitasking, selective attention and perceptual bias, deciding early, 🖍 **defensive listening** that protects the ego, anger).
  2. **Adapt your style** (Table 2.4) 🖍:
     - **content**: understand and retain; don't challenge;
     - **critical**: understand **and evaluate** logic, evidence, implications, omissions. It is **not** "listening to criticize";
     - **empathic**: understand feelings, needs and wants without judging.
  3. 🖍 **Listen actively** = consciously turn off your filters and biases. **5 elements:** an open, positive mind · active engagement · **respect for silence** · nonverbal awareness · thoughtful note-taking.
- **Long-term memory:** associate · categorize · visualize · mnemonics [p23].
- **Checklist:** don't rely on memory; write down important information [p24].

#### 2.12k Nonverbal communication [7.5-p24–p26; 7.3-p4]
- 🖍 **Definition:** sending and receiving information, **intentionally and unintentionally, without using language** [p24].
  - 7.3's examples: pictures, logos, gestures, **who sits where**, **how long a visitor waits** [7.3-p4].
  - Even a written message's **design and formatting** send signals [7.5-p24].
- ⭐ **3 roles:** **strengthen · weaken · replace** the verbal message.
- ⭐ **6 categories** (Fig. 2.10): **facial expressions · vocal characteristics · gesture and posture · personal appearance · touch · time and space**.
- 🖍 The claim that **7% of meaning is in words and 93% is nonverbal** is a **myth**: it comes from old studies of the speaker's feelings only. "Ignore any claim" of a fixed percentage.
- 🖍 **Don't "read someone like a book".** Avoiding eye contact is **not** a reliable sign of lying, and eye-contact norms vary by culture. If something feels off, ask an honest, respectful question.
- **Touch at work:** nothing beyond **handshakes** [p25–p26].

#### 2.12l Etiquette [7.5-p26–p30]
- 🖍⭐ **Etiquette** = the expected norms of polite behaviour in any situation; it rests on **mutual respect**. Poor etiquette drives away customers and investors and limits careers [p26].
- 🖍⭐ **5 zones:** **workplace · social settings · online · telephone · mobile devices** [p27].
- 🖍 **Workplace** [p27]: be punctual · don't interrupt · no profanity · no strong scents or desk meals · keep the noise down · knock first · **no gossip** · don't come in sick · avoid religion and politics.
- 🖍 **Attire** [p28]: **business formal → business professional** → business casual → casual. If unsure, "dress modestly and simply".
- 🖍 **Introductions** [p28–p29]: yourself = **first and last name + your role**. Others = both full names + a shared interest. Introduce **younger to older** and **lower-ranking to higher-ranking** people.
  - Business meals: no politics, religion, complaints or profanity; be careful with humour.
- 🖍 **Online** [p29]: no personal attacks · stay on topic · correct spelling and capitals · control your emotions · no sloppy messages · ⭐ **never assume privacy** · respect people's time and personal accounts.
- **Telephone** (6) [p29–p30]: identify yourself and the reason for the call · ask if it's a good time · double-check key details · leave brief voicemails (name, number, reason, when you can be reached).
- **Mobile** (10 don'ts) [p30]: e.g. not muting in meetings, texting during meals or conversations, using the camera without permission.

#### 2.12m Culture: high vs low context [7.8.1]
- 🖍⭐ **Definitions** [p2–p4]:
  - **Diversity:** all the traits and experiences that define individuals.
  - **Intercultural communication:** messages between people whose cultures could lead them to **interpret signs differently**.
  - **Culture:** a shared system of **symbols, beliefs, attitudes, values, expectations and norms**.
  - **Cultural competency:** appreciating differences + adjusting your style (**attitude, knowledge, skills**).
- 🖍 You **encode** with your culture's assumptions; your audience **decodes** with theirs [p3].
- 🖍 Culture is learned **directly and indirectly**, then becomes **automatic**. It is **coherent** (logical from the inside) and **complete** [p4].
- 🖍⭐ **Avoid ethnocentrism** (judging others by your group's standards), **xenophobia** (fear of strangers) and **stereotyping** (giving an individual a group's traits). Practise **cultural pluralism** (accepting cultures on their own terms) with **3 habits**: avoid assumptions · avoid judgments · acknowledge distinctions [p4].
- 🖍⭐ **8 dimensions of cultural diversity** (Fig. 3.1) [p5]: **context · laws and ethics · social customs · nonverbal signals · age · gender · religion · ability**. ⚠️ This is what "cultural dimensions" means in class (§5).
- 🖍⭐ **High vs low context** [p5–p6]

| | High-context | Low-context |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning is carried by | Nonverbal actions, setting, implicit understanding | **Explicit words**; rules spelled out |
| Communication is for | Building **relationships** as much as information | **Exchanging information** |
| Decisions focus on | The **means** of deciding; relationships | The **results** ("good for the company? my career?") |
| Time | **Flexible**; the relationship beats the deadline | A **limited resource**; one task per slot |
| Contracts (the reading's example, under legal and ethical differences [p5–p6]) | "Many Asian cultures": may treat a contract as fluid and keep renegotiating | "A U.S. manager": a signed contract ends the negotiation |
| Class examples | **China, Japan, many Arab nations** | **U.S., Germany, Switzerland** |

- **Observe 7 nonverbal areas:** greetings · personal space · touching · facial expressions · eye contact · posture · formality. Follow the locals' lead [p7].
- **Generations** (Pew; no official labels) [p7–p8]: Silent 1928–45 · Boomers 1946–64 · Gen X 1965–80 · Millennials 1981–96 (the largest in the workforce) · Gen Z 1997–.
- ⭐ **4 guidelines** [p10]: know **your own biases** · be careful with the **Golden Rule**, and treat people the way **they** want to be treated · be **accommodating and respectful** · be **patient**, with a sense of humour.
- ⭐ **6 steps** [p21]: study other **cultures** · study other **languages** · **respect** style preferences · **write** clearly · **speak and listen** carefully (use interpreters for important meetings) · **help others adapt**.
- 🖍 **Write clearly** (9) [p15–p17]:
  - short, precise words; avoid words with several meanings ("right")
  - **one idea per sentence**; short paragraphs; generous transitions
  - **local number and date formats** (12-05-22 is ambiguous)
  - **no slang, idioms, humour** or pop-culture references
- **Speak** [p19]: slowly · don't rephrase until it's obviously necessary · ask for feedback · don't talk down · double-check agreement at the end. **Listen:** let people finish.
- 🖍 **Fig. 3.4, the letter to Mr. Li** [p14–p17]:
  - "foreign" → "overseas"
  - **no idioms** or inflammatory words ("sweatshops")
  - **"Dear Mr. Li:" with a colon**
  - context first; requests that aren't demanding; a simple "Thank you" close

#### 2.12n Effective vs ineffective communication: the email checklist [7.8.2 = 7.1 Fig. 1.2 🖍]
| | Ineffective | Effective |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Subject | Vague: "Social Media Strategy" | **Informative**: topic + "meeting" + time |
| (b) Greeting | "All," is **cold** | "Hi team," is friendly, not too casual |
| (c) Opening | No background for people who missed the last meeting; "**This guy**" | Background: the consultant's **name** (Walter Johnson) and expertise |
| (d) Tone and request | **Accusatory** ("most of you just sat and stared"); no who-what-when | **Upbeat**; project leaders email their questions **to Pete by end of day Thursday** |
| (e) Logistics | **Day, not date**; "10:00a.m. to whenever" | **Full date and time span**, the room, a lunch deadline |
| (f) Absentees | Assumes they don't want to come; no invitation to ask questions | A dial-in and web option **without guilt**; questions **in advance** |
| (g) Closing | None, so it reads harsh and abrupt | "**Thanks,**" |
| (h) Signature | First name only; no alternative contact | **Full signature**: name, title, office and mobile numbers |

- **Use it as a grader's checklist** for any email task (§7 F1). 7.3's Exercise 1.11 is the same idea: rank four replies to an angry customer. The best reply thanks her, gives facts, admits "we could have done better", and picks up **her** suggestion (W7a T2).

#### 2.12o Conduct-policy structure (Apple sample) [7.2]
- **Anatomy:**
  - a cover with a **version date** → a **contents page** (6 parts) → **navigation tabs** and a dated footer on every page;
  - the parts: (1) The Way We Do Business Worldwide (values, **4 principles**, scope, training, **your responsibilities**, **reporting**, **no retaliation**, rights) · (2) Workplace Behaviors · (3) Protecting Apple · (4) Individual Accountability (conflicts of interest, **gifts**) · (5) Business Integrity · (6) Resources.
- **4 principles** [p3]: **Honesty · Respect · Confidentiality · Compliance**.
- **Writing moves to copy** (*W7b's 10 lessons, condensed*):
  - purpose and values first; a small, memorable core;
  - scope, duties and consequences early ("up to and including termination");
  - a modular, navigable layout;
  - "**you**" for duties, "**we**" for commitments, **imperatives** ("Speak up", "Never…");
  - **defined terms + concrete limits** ($150 gift rule; $10 for public officials; 100K USD donations; rotation every 3 years);
  - **whom to contact**, with several reporting channels (including anonymous);
  - cross-references; duties balanced with **rights** (no retaliation); respectful framing of hard topics.
- 🖍 **Definitions** [p17–p18]:
  - **bribe** = anything of value, to anyone, to get business or an improper advantage;
  - **kickback** = value given in exchange for passing something to a third party;
  - **facilitating payment** = a payment to speed routine government action. All are prohibited; the only exception is an imminent threat to health or safety, which must be reported.
  - 🖍 **Money laundering** = hiding illicit funds or making them look legitimate. **6 red flags:** large cash · a payer who isn't party to the contract · overpayment · an unexpected currency · a non-business account · unusual patterns.
- **Gifts:** never cash or gift cards · never to influence a decision · a zero-gift rule or a **$150** rule. **Conflict of interest:** real **or perceived**, so disclose it [p11–p16].

#### 2.12p Gaslighting: signs and responses [7.10]
- 🖍⭐ **Definition:** a form of **emotional and psychological abuse** in which someone **deliberately** tries to **confuse you, manipulate you, or make you doubt yourself** [p1].
  - 🖍 You come to doubt your **reality and memories**, through denial, twisted facts, or being told you're "overreacting". It leaves you confused, isolated and powerless.
  - As a **repeated pattern** it is **coercive control**.
  - 🖍 The name comes from Patrick Hamilton's 1938 play *Gas Light*: the husband dims the lights and denies it.
- 🖍⭐ **5 tactics** [p2]: **countering** (denies your version) · **diverting** (changes the subject) · **trivialising** (brushes off your feelings) · **withholding** (refuses to discuss) · **stereotyping** (uses identity stereotypes to dismiss you).
- 🖍 **Typical phrases** [p2]: "You're so sensitive." · "You're overreacting." · "You can't trust your memory." · "You're exaggerating." · "You're crazy."
- ⭐ **10 signs** [p2–p3]:
  - 🖍 second-guessing yourself · 🖍 feeling **dependent** · 🖍 **apologizing a lot**
  - trouble deciding · more sadness or anxiety · **making excuses** for them
  - "never good enough" · no independence · **isolation** · low self-esteem
  - It builds **gradually** [p3].
- It happens **at work** with colleagues too, and with neighbours, family, friends, even medical professionals [p3].
- ⭐ **If it's you, 4 first steps** [p4]: **trust your instincts** · talk to a **trusted person** · contact a **support service** · make a **safety plan**. "Not alone"; "not your fault".
- ⭐ **Helping someone, 6 ways** [p4–p5]: a **safe, private space** · ask how they felt · **validate and believe** them · encourage a **secure record** · don't judge their choices · explore support options.
- *My notes (W7b):*
  - The definition says "**deliberately**"; one disagreement is not gaslighting, so look for the pattern.
  - Helping someone is **empathic listening** in practice; gaslighting breaks the constructive-feedback rules.
  - ⚠️ "Stereotyping" here is a **tactic**; in 7.8.1 it is a thinking error.
- 🔎 There are no workplace-specific examples, and the class video was not covered.

### 7.C Part C: developed paragraph (12 pts)

**Model C1: Comparison and Contrast** (based on 7.3's school vs business table [7.3-p5–p6])
> *School writing and business writing share one basic demand, standard edited English, but they serve very different readers.* A student writes to show an instructor what he or she has learned, and that instructor is paid to read every page, however dull. By contrast, an employee writes to meet a real organizational need, and readers inside and outside the company read only what seems important, relevant and interesting to them. The two kinds of writing are also organized differently: a school essay moves from a thesis through paragraphs of evidence to a conclusion, while a business message usually puts the main point first. Their styles differ as well. School papers often reward long words and long, double-spaced paragraphs, whereas business documents use short, friendly words and headings or lists that a busy reader can skim. In short, the grammar stays the same, but the reader, and therefore almost everything else, changes.

- *Why it fits:* one **similarity** (standard English), then the **differences** point by point (purpose and audience · organization · style and design) [2.2-p9]. It uses contrast transitions ("By contrast", "while", "as well", "whereas", "In short"), and the closing sentence returns to the main statement.

**Model C2: Argument/Persuasion** (claim + evidence + an objection answered)
> *Engineering students should treat writing as a core technical skill, not as a side subject.* First, employers say so: our business-communication reading reports that communication ability ranks first among the qualities employers look for in college graduates. Second, writing affects careers directly, because writers in the top fifth earn more than three times as much as those in the bottom fifth. Third, an engineer's ideas are useful only if others understand them; engineers must explain complex designs to executives, customers and colleagues outside their field. Some students reply that they are being hired to design, not to write. However, almost every entry-level professional job requires e-mails, short talks and written documents. Poor writing also costs money: one page of business writing costs about $21.15 to produce, and an unclear page costs more, since it takes longer to read and needs more rounds of revision. For these reasons, the best engineers will also be careful writers.

- *The evidence comes from:* 7.3-p4 (communication ranks first) · 7.3-p5 (top 20% vs bottom 20%; the myth "I'm not a writer") · 7.1-p2 (engineers share complex ideas with people outside their field) · 7.3-p8–p9 ($21.15; bad writing is slower to read and needs more revision).
- *Why it fits:* a **challenging claim** defended with **solid points** [2.2-p12]: three reasons, an objection answered (as in PAIBOC's O) [7.3-p12], and a closing restatement.

**Grader's checklist C**
- [ ] **Pattern (4):** it does exactly what the [2.2] definition says. C1 shows **similarities *and* differences**; C2 makes a **claim + evidence**, and ideally answers an objection. The pattern is named.
- [ ] **Development (4):** the main statement comes first (1) · **3–5 specific supports**, i.e. facts, examples, numbers, not repetition (2) · a closing point (1).
- [ ] **Coherence (2):** one central idea (unity [6.4]) · transitions between the points [5.2; 7.8.1-p17].
- [ ] **Correctness and style (2):** no comma splices or fragments [5.2; 7.3-p18] · varied patterns (P2–P6) · your own words · facts accurate and credited.
- **Typical point-losers:** a list of one-sentence points with no development · a contrast with no similarity (C1) · opinions with no evidence (C2) · 1–2-sentence news-style paragraphs · 180+ words (not concise).

### 7.D Part D: news writing (19 pts)

**D1: model leads** (word counts are mine)
- **(a) Summary (34 words):** *A water main burst under a northern Tashkent street early Wednesday, collapsing part of the road and leaving about 4,500 residents without running water for about nine hours, a city water utility spokesman said.*
  - Answers what (a burst main, a road collapse), who (4,500 residents), where (northern Tashkent), when (early Wednesday) and who said (the spokesman).
  - The "why" (corrosion) is only **preliminary**, so it is left out and goes lower down with attribution [6.1-p5; W4-6 Task 1].
  - The whole event matters more than any one part [6.1-p20].
- **(b) Delayed identification (27 words):** *A 19-year-old engineering student's emergency call sent repair crews to a northern Tashkent street minutes before a water main burst there Wednesday, a water utility spokesman said.*
  - She is **described** by age and role; her name comes in paragraph 2 [6.1-p35].
  - She is not prominent, so no name [6.1-p31].
  - What she did is the emphasis, and it is attributed to the spokesman because **he** said her call put the crews on the road.
- **(c) Follow-up (32 words):** *The Tashkent city council voted Thursday to inspect every water pipe older than 30 years by the end of 2027, a day after a burst main cut water to about 4,500 residents.*
  - The **latest** news comes first; the background takes one phrase ("a day after a burst main…") [6.1-p27].
  - The source is implied by the information itself, a public vote [5.2].
- **Also accepted:**
  - a **partial summary** that stresses one angle ("Nine hours without water…") [6.1-p10];
  - a **localized** lead for a named district's paper [6.1-p22].
  - **Not** an immediate-identification lead: nobody in the story is prominent [6.1-p31].

**Grader's checklist D1** (3 pts per lead)

- [ ] Specific facts (numbers, place, day) · attribution, usually at the end · **past tense** · no "there was…" [5.2]
- [ ] Nothing invented, and nothing preliminary stated as fact (e.g. "a corroded main" in the lead)

### 7.E Part E: media law (9 pts; 3 per case: name the concept · apply the rule to the facts · give the fix)

**E1: Libel.**
- Published (a blog) · probably **false**, or at least unproven (one angry post) · **injurious** (lost customers) · **unprivileged** (not from an official proceeding). That is P-F-I-U [ML-p6].
- It accuses him of dishonesty that sounds like a **crime** and damages him in his **occupation**: categories 1 and 2 [ML-p8].
- "**Truth is the best defense**", but the blogger can prove nothing [ML-p9].
- **Fix:** check receipts, find named sources, ask Sodiqov for his side, and publish only what can be proved. Run Paulson's questions (fully, factually, fairly, in good faith) [ML-p16].
- **The opinion version is safer:** an opinion is not an "objectively false" statement, so element 2 fails [ML-p6]. Keep any facts behind the opinion accurate (compare the fair-comment conditions [ML-p14]).

**E2: False light** (and possibly libel).
- A true photo used in a **misleading context** paints her as a cheater. This is the class's careful-pedestrian example: a true photo reused with a misleading story [ML-p17, p21].
- Newsworthiness **won't protect** false light [ML-p19].
- If readers take it as an accusation of cheating that harms her reputation, it can also be **libel** (the slide says the pedestrian could sue for libel, false light, or both) [ML-p21].
- **Fix:** use a photo with no identifiable student, or a posed photo with consent, and never imply guilt.

**E3: Two problems.**
- **(1) Trespassing by deception.**
  - Physical intrusion into a private area [ML-p17]. Courts won't protect a trespasser "even if it is newsworthy". This is the *Life* magazine "faith healer" case [ML-p20].
  - **Fix:** ask for access, use public vantage points, interviews and official records. Enter only when invited by the owner or renter, never by disguise.
- **(2) Copyright.**
  - The report is protected from the moment it was fixed in tangible form [ML-p22]. Reprinting all 2,000 words goes far beyond "small excerpts" [ML-p22].
  - Fair use has **no precise limits** and is a defense **only after you are sued** [7.1-p23].
  - **Fix:** quote short excerpts with credit, paraphrase the findings with credit, or get permission.

**Grader's checklist E**
- [ ] The class **term** (libel / false light / trespassing / copyright)
- [ ] The **test applied** to the facts (P-F-I-U, a category, the exception, small excerpts)
- [ ] The **defense or exception** named (truth; newsworthiness doesn't cover false light or trespass; fair use)
- [ ] A concrete **fix**, plus Paulson's four questions
- **Point-losers:** "it's slander" (it is published, so libel) · "newsworthy, so fine" · "fair use because it's educational"

**F1 (b): problems in the original** (any four, each tied to a class rule)
1. **Vague subject line** ("visit") [7.8.2 (a)].
2. **"Hi," with no name:** too casual for a senior reader and a first contact. Formal readers, international readers and first contacts need more formality; the class letter uses "Dear Mr. Li:" [7.8.2 (b); 7.4-p12, p14; 7.8.1 Fig. 3.4].
3. **No background:** who is writing, from which university, and what the project is [7.8.2 (c)].
4. **A demanding tone, with no who-what-when** ("pretty much whenever", "next week", "let me know") [7.8.2 (d)–(e)].
5. **Slang and idioms** ("hit you up", "piece of cake", "gonna", "pics", "pls"): they "don't travel well" across languages, and sloppy messages and acronyms hurt your credibility [7.8.1-p17; 7.5-p29].
6. **A comma splice:** "…take pics of everything, it's gonna be…" [5.2; 7.4-p18].
7. **No reader benefit and no answer to his likely objections** (safety, confidentiality, staff time): PAIBOC B and O [7.3-p12]. Asking for the "specs" requests information a company treats as confidential (compare [7.2-p7]).
8. **No closing, no full signature, no contact details** [7.8.2 (g)–(h)].
9. **Channel:** for a first contact, "paper is usually better" [7.4-p14]. If you must email, make it formal and offer a call (more than one channel).

**Why the rewrite works:**
- The subject line carries the key facts.
- A formal salutation with a colon, as in Fig. 3.4.
- Background before the request.
- **One idea per sentence**, no idioms, and dates written as **day + month name** (12-05-22-style dates are ambiguous [7.8.1-p16]).
- The request is specific, with a reply deadline.
- His objections are answered (safety, photos, confidentiality, a supervisor present), and there is a **benefit** for him (the report).
- It offers a second channel, closes politely, and ends with a full signature (the "you" attitude [7.1-p8]).
