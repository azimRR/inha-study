### 1.2 How the pieces connect

- **SPQ is the hub.** The instructor's sample [1.3] is labelled "Class Assignment #3" and is described as "an assignment on paraphrasing, summarizing and quoting". The Week 2 drill [2.3] repeats the same three-part format on a new text, so expect to produce a P, an S and a Q from a passage.
- **P and S use the Week 1 rules:** your own words, your own sentence structure, the author's meaning kept accurately, and credit to the author [1.2-p10b, p12a]. Swapping synonyms or reordering the original is still plagiarism [1.1-p5, 1.2-p5a].
- **"Your own sentence structure" is where Week 2 helps.** If the original sentence uses one pattern, you can rebuild the idea in a different pattern (for example, a P2 "IC, cc IC" sentence becomes a P5 "When…, IC" sentence). *My note: the slides do not state this link; it is a study strategy that combines [1.2] and [2.1].*
- **Q uses the Week 3 rules and Harvard's quoting rules.** Week 3 covers the comma before the opening quotation mark, periods and commas inside the closing mark, the citation exception, and where ? and ! go [QM-p2]. Harvard requires exact text, author, year and page, with quotation marks for 39 words or fewer and a block for 40 or more [1.2-p7b, p13b].
- **Paragraph patterns [2.2] are the containers** for paraphrases and quotes. The Argument slide defines an academic argument as a main idea (a "claim" or "thesis statement") backed with evidence [2.2-p12]. Paraphrases and quotes are that evidence.

### 1.3 The five points the materials stress most ⭐

1. ⭐ It is **NOT enough** to change a few words, use synonyms, or reorder the original [1.2-p5a]. The pink-highlight slide [1.1-p5] shows this in action.
2. ⭐ Whether you quote, summarize or paraphrase, **you must give credit** to the author [1.2-p6b].
3. ⭐ A paraphrase or summary needs **your own words and your own sentence structure**. A quote needs the **exact text, quotation marks, and the page number** [1.2-p13b table].
4. ⭐ Remember the **Four Rs** (Reword, Rephrase, Restructure, Recheck) and **cite your sources** [1.2-p20b].
5. ⭐ In American English, **periods and commas go inside** the closing quotation mark, unless a parenthetical citation follows [QM-p2].

**Why the p3 version is legitimate** (*my analysis, using the rules in [1.2]*)
- **New wording for the same ideas.** "overuse quotations" becomes "quote excessively". "only about 10%… should appear as directly quoted" becomes "keep quoted material down to a desirable level". "limit… exact transcribing" becomes "minimize the material recorded verbatim".
- **New structure.** Three sentences become two, and the second one opens with "Since…," (a Pattern 5 sentence, see [2.1]).
- **New order.** It starts with the problem in the final paper and then traces it back to note taking. The original goes from notes, to the paper, to the 10% figure, to the advice.
- **Same meaning, with credit:** "(Lester 46-47)".
- It still shares basic topic words such as *students*, *research papers*, *quoted material* and *note taking*. What changed is how the ideas are worded and built. 🔎 Look up in Track B: which words (topic terms, names, numbers) may stay unchanged in a paraphrase.

| Original (Lester) | Plagiarized version | What the writer did |
|---|---|---|

These are exactly the moves Harvard lists as "NOT enough" [1.2-p5a]. ⭐ On p4 the plagiarized version even ends with "(Lester 46-47)" (possibly a copy-paste leftover, see the ⚠️ above), yet the slide still labels it **plagiarized**. *My reading: a citation does not rescue copied wording and copied structure.*

**"What is Plagiarism?": the four bullets, one per slide [1.1-p6 to p9] ⭐**
1. [p6] **Definition:** plagiarism is "the use of someone else's ideas or language without acknowledging that we did not create them."
2. [p7] It applies to **ideas, words and unusual structures**, wherever you find them: in a book, on a webpage, in an email.
3. [p8] When you include a person's information or wording, you **must acknowledge the source** and include a **citation** that tells readers where you got it.
4. [p9] Plagiarism is sometimes seen as **intellectual theft**.

*My note:* bullet 2 explains why copying the original's sentence structure (p5) counts as plagiarism: structures are covered, not just words.

⚠️ Small slips on p6–p9: "including person's information" is missing "a". Bullet 3 mixes "we" with "your readers" and has no final period. Bullet 4 ends with a redundant "— plagiarism".

**Citation form used here:** "(Lester 46-47)" gives the author and page numbers but no year. [1.2] uses APA style instead (author, year, p.). 🔎 Look up in Track B: which citation style (APA or MLA) the course expects.

#### [1.2] Harvard tutorial: "Principles of Paraphrasing" (HGSE, 2011; 22 pages with 2 slides each)

**Structure** [p2a]: Module 1, Defining Correct Paraphrasing. Module 2, Rules for Quoting, Summarizing, and Paraphrasing. Module 3, Tips and Strategies for Successful Paraphrasing. Self Check: a worksheet and answer key, which are **not included** in the class PDF and are only listed on p22b.

**Front matter (p1–p3a).** These are the title slide and the "Tutorial Features" slide; it was an online audio tutorial ("Notes = Full Transcript", "Make sure your computer audio is on"). They also show the outline, the APA *Publication Manual* (6th ed.) and the "APA Exposed" tutorial by Wendy K. Mages. These pages contain no rules.

**MODULE 1: Defining correct paraphrasing (p3b–p5b)**
- [p4a] **Definition** (from Merriam-Webster): a paraphrase is "a restatement of a text, passage, or work giving the meaning in another form."
- [p4b] **Why paraphrase?** ⭐ (the bold phrases on the slide):
  - It signals that **you understand the subject matter**.
  - In education and psychology, the **use of long quotes is not preferred**.
  - It lets you **succinctly compare, contrast, and synthesize the ideas of scholars in your field**.
  - It lets you **demonstrate how your own ideas relate to and build on the ideas of other scholars**.
  - Paraphrasing **correctly** avoids inadvertent plagiarism.
- [p5a] ⭐ **"How Much Do I Need to Change?"** It is **NOT enough** (bold and underlined) to:
  1. change one or even a few words
  2. rearrange the words
  3. replace the words with synonyms
  4. omit a few words
  5. change the punctuation
  6. reorder the phrases in a sentence
  7. reorder the sentences in a paragraph
- [p5b] ⭐ Paraphrasing is **really** about **understanding** a passage, **internalizing** the meaning of the text, and restating the important points **in your own voice**.

**MODULE 2: Rules for quoting, summarizing and paraphrasing (p6a–p14b)**
- [p6b] There are **three ways** to represent another author's work: **quote** the author, **summarize** the author's point or findings, or **paraphrase** the author's text. Quoting follows **different rules** from summarizing and paraphrasing. HGSE students are **required** (underlined) to know these rules, and following them helps you avoid **inadvertently committing plagiarism!** ⭐ **In all three cases you must give credit to the author.**
- [p7a] **No author to credit?** ⭐ You must **always** show when you are using ideas that are not your own. Use the **title** in place of the author's name. If no year is listed, use **(n.d.)**. The slide gives a Wikipedia reference as an example, then warns: ⭐ "Use **CAUTION** with wiki sources!"
- [p7b] ⭐ **Quoting in APA style. You must:**
  - use **quotation marks** for quotes of less than 40 words
  - cite the **author's last name**
  - note the **year of publication**
  - give the **page number(s)** where the quotation appears

  A footnote sends you to the APA manual or "APA Exposed" for block quotes and online sources.
- [p8a] **Long quote** (more than 40 words) from Strauss (2008) in *The Washington Post*. It is introduced with "According to Strauss (2008)," and set as an **indented block** with **no quotation marks**, ending with "(p. C01)". Red arrows on the slide point at the indentation.
- [p8b] **Short quote**: two class models.
  - Signal phrase first: `According to Strauss (2008), "…" (p. C01).`
  - Quote first: `"…" (Strauss, 2008, p. C01).`
  - *My note:* in both models the period comes **after** the parenthetical citation, which matches the Week 3 rule [QM-p2].
- [p9a] ⭐ **A block quote is for 40 words or more. Quotation marks are for 39 words or fewer.**
- [p9b] **Ellipses.** Red boxes highlight "decades…." and "is…asking". The dots mark words left out of the quote. *My note: where the cut falls at the end of a sentence, the slide shows four dots, "decades…."* ⚠️ Inconsistency: in the shortened block the citation sits before the final period ("…reading (p. C01)."). The unshortened version on the same slide ends "…reading. (p. C01)", and the long blocks on p8a and p9a end "…issues. (p. C01)", both with the citation after the period. 🔎 Look up in Track B: where the citation goes after a block quote, and how to space an ellipsis.
- [p10a] **Brackets.** In "Of the 25 **[Newbery Medal]** winners…", the red box shows words added in square brackets to make the quote clear.
- [p10b] ⭐ **Summarizing. You must:** **accurately** reflect the author's message, cite the **author's last name**, note the **year of publication**, use **your own words**, and use **your own sentence structure**.
- [p11a] **Example summary**: one sentence. According to Strauss (2008), books that win the Newbery Medal deal with topics so sophisticated and serious that they may actually discourage children from reading.
- [p11b] **Example paraphrase**: two sentences with more detail. The books sell well even though they focus on difficult topics such as death and developmental disabilities, and instead of building children's interest, these topics may discourage them from reading.
- [p12a] ⭐ **Paraphrasing correctly. You must:** follow the same five rules as for summarizing: be accurate, give the last name, give the year, use your own words, and use your own structure.
- [p12b] **Quote within a paraphrase.** The paraphrase keeps two exact phrases in quotation marks, "the absence of one or both parents" and "mental challenges such as autism", followed by (p. C01). ⚠️ The second phrase is not word for word: the original on p8a reads "such mental challenges as autism".
- [p13a] **Compare and contrast:** the same Strauss text shown as a Quote (block), a Summary (one sentence) and a Paraphrase (two sentences). The summary's wording differs slightly from p11a, but that is a variant, not an error.
- [p13b] ⭐ **THE TABLE.** On the slide, a smiley means Necessary, a red circle means Prohibited, and an X means Not required.

| | Author & Year | Exact Text | Quotation Marks | Page Number(s) | Your Own Words | Your Own Structure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Quote** | Necessary | Necessary | Necessary (#) | Necessary | **Prohibited** | **Prohibited** |
| **Summary & Paraphrase** | Necessary | **Prohibited** | Not required (\*) | Not required | Necessary | Necessary |

  - (#) In APA format, quotation marks are used for quotes of **39 or fewer** words. **Block quotes** are used for quotes of **40 words or more**.
  - (\*) ⭐ "**Remember**, you must place quotation marks around a quoted phrase within a paraphrase or summary."
- [p14a] ⭐ **Longer passages:** "you should **also** try to restructure the order in which the ideas are presented." *My note: this does not contradict p5a. Reordering on its own is not enough, but for long passages Harvard recommends reordering in addition to new words and new structure.*
- [p14b] **Is it better to quote, summarize or paraphrase?** ⭐ In the social sciences it is best to **limit your use of quotes**. Some educators think students quote when they **don't fully understand the original text** and **cannot accurately restate its meaning**. Most readers appreciate **concise papers with well-supported arguments**. If a brief summary can convey the important information, **a summary is best**. If you need more detail to make your point, you **may need to paraphrase**.

**MODULE 3: Tips and strategies (p15a–p20b)**
- [p15b] ⭐ **WARNING!** "Haphazard Note Taking Can Be **DANGEROUS!**" (in red capitals). Will you remember what was paraphrased and what was a direct quote, where each came from, and what the original said?
- [p16a] ⭐ **Taking notes when you type or paste text into a Word file:**
  - **Always** type the **exact** text.
  - **Always use quotation marks** in your notes.
  - **Always include the citation information**: author, date and page number(s).
  - **Always** note **your** own interpretations and ideas in a **separate document**.

  The slide's source line is the *MLA Handbook*, 7th ed. (2009). ⚠️ The slide title reads "Research &Taking Notes", with a space missing.
- [p16b] ⭐ **Recheck all paraphrases in the final edit.** **Make sure** you used **your own words** and **your own sentence structure**, and that the paraphrase still **accurately reflects** the meaning of the original.
- [p17a] ⭐ **An approach to paraphrasing.** It starts from a **deep understanding of the material**, then you **Reword, Rephrase, Restructure**:
  1. Reread the passage you intend to paraphrase.
  2. Highlight the ideas that support your argument.
  3. **Without looking at the original passage**, write the important ideas in your own words.
  4. Check that you have reworded, rephrased and restructured the text.
- [p17b] **Demo: "Highlights to Bulleted List".** The source is a *Newsweek* passage (Raymond, 2005, p. 60) about surgical patients who did not get antibiotics on time. Five ideas are highlighted in five colours and turned into five bullet notes:
  1. the Archives of Surgery report
  2. 44% of 34,000 patients were not given antibiotics appropriately
  3. within 1 hour = best practice
  4. antibiotic errors lead to preventable infections and deaths
  5. discuss pre-surgical antibiotics with the doctor

  ⚠️ One bullet reads "Within1 hour", with a space missing.
- [p18a–b] **"Bulleted List to Paraphrase" and "Original to Paraphrase".** The paraphrase is one paragraph that opens "According to Raymond (2005)," and restates each coloured idea in new words. *My note: look at the order.* The paraphrase **opens with the one-hour best practice (yellow)**, which was only the **third** idea in the original. Then come the study (blue), the consequences (orange), the 44% figure (pink) and the advice (purple). This is p14a's "restructure the order" rule in action. Proper names and numbers (Archives of Surgery, 34,000, 44%) stay the same, and the original's joking opening and side remarks are left out. ⚠️ "Raymond (2005) , best" has a stray space before the comma.
- [p19a] **Maintaining tone and voice:** write in **your** voice, use a tone **appropriate for your audience**, and strive for a **smooth seamless text**.
- [p19b] **A "sticky" situation**, meaning a passage that is hard to restate. Online tools are quick and easy. They can be used to verify the **original** source and to **compare** your quoted or paraphrased text with the **original**.
- [p20a] ⭐ **WARNING!** "Remember Haphazard Cut and Paste is **DANGEROUS!**"
- [p20b] ⭐ **Recap.** Paraphrasing is really about understanding, internalizing, and restating in your own voice. The **Four Rs** are 1. **R**eword, 2. **R**ephrase, 3. **R**estructure, 4. **R**echeck. **Cite your sources.**

**What each part of the sample does**

| Part | Length | Content | Technique to notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paraphrase** | 1 sentence | The core event: who (the judge, and Jones as "a TV scammer"), what (12 years), why (he swindled people through TV ads for his "fantastic ideas") and the result (thousands sent him almost $10 million) | It restates the main point of the article's first two paragraphs in new words. The borrowed phrase **"fantastic ideas"** stays **inside quotation marks**, which is Harvard's ⭐ "Remember" rule [1.2-p13b]. |
| **Summary** | 1 longer sentence | The paraphrase **plus** the three schemes: fake earthquake insurance, claiming ownership of airspace, and a product that "purportedly eliminated calories" | It covers the main points of the **whole** story in compressed form. |
| **Quote** | Jones's three sentences plus a speaker tag | His exact words in quotation marks ("For some reason, TV seems to break the ice. … You enter his living room and become a trusted friend,"), then ", said Bruce Jones, who was sentenced…" | Quote first, **comma inside** the closing quotation mark, then the speaker tag and some context [QM-p2]. In the article this quote is split by "Jones said"; the student joined the parts into one quotation and moved the speaker tag to the end. |

*My note on Summary vs Paraphrase in this course:* in the instructor's own SPQ models ([1.3] and [2.3]), the **Paraphrase restates the key idea** (the opening part of the text). The **Summary is that paraphrase plus the remaining main points of the whole text**, so in these models it is the *longer* part. In Harvard's example [1.2-p11] both versions cover the same passage, so there the summary is shorter. The rules are the same in both: your own words, your own structure, the meaning kept accurately, and credit to the source. For the SPQ task, copy the instructor's layout.

**Quotation marks used inside the article** (good Week 3 examples):
- "official government" and "proved" are in quotation marks to show **irony**, because the report was neither official nor proof. Compare the irony rule in [QM-p1].
- "NoCal." has the **period inside** the closing mark, which is the American style [QM-p2].
- "For some reason," Jones said, "TV seems…" shows a quotation **split by a speaker tag**. 🔎 Look up in Track B: how to punctuate split quotations; the QM handout does not cover them.

**Watch-outs if you imitate the sample.** These are checked against the class's own rules; the sample is still the instructor's model.
- ⚠️ The sample says "sentenced Bruce Jones… **for** 12 years", but the article itself says "sentenced Bruce Jones **to** 12 years".
- ⚠️ The sample says "**in** a result of which", but the class's own passage [1.1-p2] uses "**as** a result".
- ⚠️ Accuracy: the paraphrase says he swindled people "in the United States", but the article says "throughout **the state**". Harvard's rule is to "accurately reflect the author's message" [1.2-p10b].
- ⚠️ Cosmetic: the article mixes straight and curly quotation marks.
- None of the three parts includes in-text credit; the source is credited only on the Reference page. Harvard says credit is necessary in all three cases [1.2-p6b, p13b], so opening with "According to Li (n.d.)," is a safe habit. 🔎 Look up in Track B (or ask the instructor): whether the SPQ assignment expects in-text citations.

#### [2.1] Sentence Patterns: "Building blocks of your story" (instructor's slides, 9 slides)

**Key terms, explained from the class's own formulas and examples**
- **Independent clause (IC):** a clause that can stand alone. Pattern One shows that a single IC, "Snow fell", is already a complete sentence.
- **Dependent clause (DC):** a clause that starts with a **dependent marker**, as in "When snow fell". It needs an IC to complete the thought: "When snow fell, it felt cold".
- **cc** is short for **coordinating conjunction**.
- **Independent marker:** a connecting word, such as *therefore*, that sits between two ICs after a semicolon.
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: formal definitions of independent and dependent clauses. The slides give formulas and examples only.

| # | Name on the slide | Formula (as on the slide) | Connectors listed on the slide | Class example | Punctuation built into the formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Simple sentence [p2] | Independent clause | none | **"Snow fell"** | One IC with its end mark |
| 2 | Compound Sentence [p3] | IC **,** cc IC | *and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet* | **"Snow fell, but it felt warm."** | **Comma before** the cc |
| 3 | Compound Sentence [p4] | IC **;** IC | none | **"Snow fell; it felt warm, though"** | A **semicolon alone** joins the two ICs |
| 4 | Compound Sentence [p5] | IC **;** independent marker **,** IC | *therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, however, also* | **"Snow fell; therefore, it felt cold"** | **Semicolon before** the marker and **comma after** it |
| 5 | Complex Sentence [p6] | Dependent marker + DC **,** IC | *because, before, since, while, although, if, until, when, then, after, as, as if* | **"When snow fell, it felt cold"** | **Comma after** the dependent clause when it comes **first** |
| 6 | Compound-Complex Sentence [p7] | Two ICs and one DC | the lists above | *"When snow fell, it felt cold, but I liked anyway"* | The Pattern 5 comma plus the Pattern 2 comma |

#### [2.2] "Paragraph Patterns" (file name "Paragraph Development"; 13 slides, modified by the instructor)

- [p1] The title slide, "Paragraph Patterns", with the line "Inha University in Tashkent, Modified by Instructor Khasan Khankeldiyev".
- [p2] ⭐ **The nine types of paragraph patterns:**
  1. Exemplification
  2. Narration
  3. Description
  4. Process Analysis
  5. Cause and Effect
  6. Comparison and Contrast
  7. Definition
  8. Classification
  9. Argument/Persuasion

**1. Exemplification [p3]:** "To explain statements that we make with examples, details or specific instances."
- **Class example:** "The new shirt I bought is poorly made." This claim is followed by what happened in the wash: the colours faded, one button cracked and another fell off, a shoulder seam opened, and the sleeves shrank almost two inches.
- *My note:* the paragraph states a general claim first, then gives concrete details you can check, including a measurement ("almost two inches").

**2. Narration [p4–p5]:** "We just tell a story in a chronological order with some details."
- **Class example [p5]:** the narrator hurries across a rain-soaked campus and tries to jump a large puddle to look cool in front of two friends. He fails and splashes everyone, and the friends say "Well done, Dave!". The last line explains that his embarrassment was greater because he had tried to look casual.
- *My note:* the events come in time order and are told in the first person. A short line of dialogue appears in quotation marks, with the "!" inside them [QM-p2]. The paragraph ends with the point of the story.

**3. Description [p6]:** "It describes something or someone with vivid words, appealing to a reader's sight, hearing, taste, smell or tough." ⚠️ "tough" should be "**touch**".
- **Class example:** "A rug covers the living-room floor." is marked ⭐ **(Not vivid)**. The vivid version describes a thick, reddish-brown shag rug laid wall to wall, whose long curled fibers "seem to whisper" when you walk on them barefoot. When you squeeze your toes into it, the soft fibers push back with "a spongy resilience".
- *My note:* the vivid version adds precise adjectives and appeals to the senses: sight (colour, thickness), hearing ("whisper") and touch ("bare feet", "spongy").

**4. Process (Process Analysis) [p7]:** "It explains the reader how to do or make certain things in the sequence of steps". ⚠️ This should read "explains **to** the reader", and the sentence has no final period.
- **Class example, "How to cook a perfect steak":** "To begin," pat the steak dry with paper towels. Then season both sides with salt and pepper; the seasoning sticks to the surface and helps create a crust. The slide cuts the text off with "…." to show that it continues.
- *My note:* the paragraph uses a step signal ("To begin,"), gives commands in order, and explains the reason for a step. The second sentence is a **Pattern 3** sentence (IC; IC). ⚠️ The example switches between "steak" and "steaks".

**5. Cause and Effect [p8]:** "A writer literarily does some detective work by examining the causes of something or the effects of something." ⚠️ "literarily" should be "**literally**".
- **Class example:** a man died for no good cause. He was taking selfies for his Instagram followers on the edge of a cliff and slipped into the abyss. Some followers saw his last moments, and Instagram removed the footage.
- *My note:* the cause is the risky selfie, and the effects are his death, followers seeing it, and the footage being removed.
- ⚠️ Slips: "a couple of selfie" should be "selfies". "instagram" should be capitalized, as it is later in the same paragraph. After the semicolon, "only made a couple of selfie…" has no subject; by the class's own Pattern 3 (IC; IC) it should be "**he** only made…".

**6. Comparison and Contrast [p9]:** "We compare two things to show how they are similar and contrast for their difference."
- **Class example, "Energy drinks":** "Just as driving recklessly may lead to a fatal crash, so does consuming energy drinks if ignoring a recommended daily norm."
- *My note:* the frame "Just as…, so does…" shows a similarity. The class example shows only the comparison half, not a contrast.

**7. Definition [p10]:** "It offers informal definitions to explain what a particular term means."
- **Class example:** "What is death? What does Aristotle say about death?" The answer: according to Aristotle, the dead are more blessed and happier than the living, and dying is a return to one's real home.
- *My note:* the paragraph moves from a question to an answer from an authority, introduced with "According to…". This is the same attribution frame Harvard uses in "According to Strauss (2008),".

**8. Classification [p11]:** "This pattern separates many things into categories so that we could manage or understand them better."
- **Class example** (titled "Date Classification" ⚠️, which should be "**Data**" Classification): government organizations usually use five levels of data classification: Top Secret, Secret, Confidential, Sensitive and Unclassified. Commercial organizations most often use four (Restricted, Confidential, Internal, Public), which are simpler and named after how the data should be handled.
- *My note:* the paragraph uses one basis for sorting (the sensitivity level), names each category, and compares two systems.

**9. Argument/Persuasion [p12]:** "It makes a challenging point and defend it with a series of solid points." ⚠️ "defend" should be "**defends**".
- **Academic definition** (from the UNC Writing Center): in academic writing, an argument is usually a main idea, often called a "claim" or "thesis statement", backed up with evidence that supports the idea. The slide gives no example paragraph.
- *My note:* this slide itself shows two quotation-mark rules from Week 3: quotation marks that highlight words ("claim"), and a comma inside the closing mark ("thesis statement,").

- [p13] **References:** Langan & Albright (2019), *Exploring Writing: Paragraphs and Essays* (4th ed.), which is the course textbook; Segal (2026) for the steak recipe; Packetlabs (2022) for data classification; and the UNC Writing Center page "Argument".

## 3. Rule sheets

### 3.1 Paraphrasing and plagiarism

**A. How to paraphrase: the steps as taught** (combined from [1.2-p17a, p14a, p12a, p16b, p20b])
1. **Understand the passage deeply.** Reread it. ⭐ Paraphrasing is "really" about understanding, internalizing, and restating in your own voice [1.2-p5b].
2. **Highlight** the ideas you need, meaning the ones that support your argument.
3. **Turn the highlights into short bullet notes**, as in the class demo [1.2-p17b].
4. ⭐ **Without looking at the original**, write the ideas in **your own words** and **your own sentence structure**. For a longer passage, **also change the order** of the ideas [1.2-p14a].
5. **Give credit** with the author's last name and the year, for example "According to Raymond (2005), …". If there is no author, use the title; if there is no year, use **(n.d.)** [1.2-p7a].
6. **Put any exact phrase you keep inside quotation marks** [1.2-p13b \*], with the page number, as in [1.2-p12b].
7. ⭐ **Recheck** that you used your own words and your own structure and that the meaning is still accurate [1.2-p16b]. This completes the **Four Rs** (Reword, Rephrase, Restructure, Recheck) plus **Cite your sources** [1.2-p20b].
- Throughout, write in **your** voice, use a tone suited to your audience, and aim for **smooth, seamless text** [1.2-p19a].

**B. The five "musts" for a paraphrase, which also apply to a summary** [1.2-p10b, p12a]
1. Accurately reflect the author's message.
2. Give the author's last name.
3. Give the year of publication.
4. Use your own words.
5. Use your own sentence structure.

**C. ⭐ NOT enough. Doing only these things still counts as plagiarism** [1.2-p5a]
1. changing one or a few words
2. rearranging the words
3. swapping in synonyms
4. leaving out a few words
5. changing the punctuation
6. reordering the phrases in a sentence
7. reordering the sentences in a paragraph

**D. Plagiarism as taught** [1.1-p6 to p9]
- **Definition:** using someone else's ideas or language without acknowledging that you did not create them.
- **Scope:** it covers **ideas, words and unusual structures**, wherever you find them (a book, a webpage, an email).
- **Rule:** when you use a person's information or wording, acknowledge the source and give a citation.
- **How it is viewed:** sometimes as intellectual theft.
- **Class evidence** [1.1-p4–5]: a version that keeps the original's order, frame and key phrases and only swaps a few words is **plagiarized**, even with a citation at the end.

**E. Note-taking safety** [1.2-p15b, p16a, p20a]
- ⭐ Always type the exact text, always use quotation marks in your notes, and always record the author, date and page(s).
- Keep **your own** ideas in a **separate document**.
- ⭐ Haphazard note taking and haphazard cut-and-paste are **DANGEROUS**, because you may later forget what was quoted, where it came from, and what the original said.

**F. Quote, summarize or paraphrase?** [1.2-p14b]
- Limit your quotes.
- If a brief summary conveys the important information, use a **summary**.
- If you need more detail, **paraphrase**.
- Heavy quoting can suggest that you did not fully understand the source.

### 3.3 Sentence patterns cheat sheet [2.1]

```
P1  Simple            IC.                                  Snow fell.
P2  Compound          IC, cc IC.                           Snow fell, but it felt warm.
                      cc = and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet
P3  Compound          IC; IC.                              Snow fell; it felt warm, though.
P4  Compound          IC; independent marker, IC.          Snow fell; therefore, it felt cold.
                      markers = therefore, moreover, thus, consequently, however, also
P5  Complex           Dependent marker + DC, IC.           When snow fell, it felt cold.
                      markers = because, before, since, while, although, if, until,
                                when, then(?), after, as, as if
P6  Compound-Complex  two ICs + one DC                     When snow fell, it felt cold, but I liked it anyway.
P7  Complex           "ANY CLUE?" (not given in class)     (see the 🔎 in §2, [2.1])
```
(Periods added. ⚠️ Slide p7 reads "I liked anyway". The "(?)" after "then" marks the possible slip noted in §2.)

**How to identify a pattern** (*my method, using only the class formulas*)
1. Count the independent clauses (ICs).
2. Check whether the sentence **starts** with a dependent marker from the P5 list. If it does and there is one IC, it is **P5**. If it does and there are two ICs, it is **P6**.
3. If there are two ICs and no dependent clause, look at the joint between them:
   - comma + cc means **P2**
   - semicolon alone means **P3**
   - semicolon + marker + comma means **P4**
4. One IC and nothing else means **P1**.

**Using the patterns in a paraphrase** (*my note*): rebuild the original's pattern. For example, turn P2 ("…, and as a result…") into P5 ("Since…, …"), or a P1 + P1 pair into P4. That is what the legitimate paraphrase in [1.1-p3] does with its "Since…," sentence.

### 3.5 Quotation-mark rules

**From the Week 3 handout [QM]**
- **R1.** A complete-sentence quote starts with a **capital** letter: My sister said, "I need to do my homework."
- **R2.** A fragment or phrase quote starts in **lowercase**: The phrase "don't win in practice"…
- **R3.** Add clarifying words in **[brackets]**: "…this brother [Jeff] will…," said Andrew.
- **R4.** A quote within a quote takes **'single'** marks inside "double" marks: I said, "My father used to tell me, 'Always give it your all.'"
- **R5.** Quotation marks can show **irony or sarcasm**: he "cares".
- **R6.** Quotation marks can **highlight a word** as a word: I wrote "your" when I meant to write "you're."
- **R7.** Put **titles** of songs, essays, poems, short stories, one-act plays, chapters, TV episodes and articles in quotation marks.
- **R8.** ⭐ In American English, **periods and commas go inside** the closing marks, whether single or double.
- **R9.** Put a **comma before the opening mark** (Tom said, "…"). If the quote comes first, the **comma goes inside** the closing mark ("…," said Tom.).
- **R10.** If a **parenthetical citation follows**, the period comes after it: "…humid" (16).
- **R11.** **? and !** go inside only if they belong to the quote; otherwise they go outside: …"Don't Stop Believing"? and "Stop!" shouted…

**From Harvard [1.2] (APA quoting)**
- **H1.** A quote needs the **exact text** in **quotation marks**, the **author's last name**, the **year** and the **page** [p7b, p13b].
- **H2.** Use quotation marks for **39 words or fewer**. For **40 or more**, use a **block quote**: indented, with no quotation marks and the page at the end [p8a, p9a, p13b].
- **H3.** There are two short-quote models: `According to Strauss (2008), "…" (p. C01).` and `"…" (Strauss, 2008, p. C01).` [p8b]
- **H4.** Mark words you leave out with an **ellipsis**: "is…asking", "decades…." [p9b]. [2.3] also uses one at the start of a quote.
- **H5.** Put words you add in **[brackets]**: "Of the 25 [Newbery Medal] winners…" [p10a]. The class's original passage in [1.1-p2] also contains "[research]".
- **H6.** ⭐ **Remember:** a quoted phrase inside a paraphrase or summary **must** be in quotation marks [p13b \*, p12b].
- **H7.** If there is no author, use the **title**. If there is no year, use **(n.d.)** [p7a].

**Order of checks for the end of a quotation** (*my sequence of the class rules*)
1. Does a parenthetical citation follow? If so, close the quote, add the (citation), then the period (R10, H3).
2. Is the end mark a ? or a !? If it belongs to the quote, it goes inside; if not, outside (R11).
3. Is it a period or a comma? It goes **inside** (R8).
4. Does the quote come before the speaker? Then put a comma inside the closing mark and add "said X" (R9).
5. Does the speaker come first? Then put a comma before the opening mark, and use a capital letter if the quote is a full sentence (R9, R1).

- D. "Just as a bridge needs a strong foundation, so does an essay need a clear main idea."
- E. "What is a deadline? A deadline is the last moment by which a task must be finished; in a team project, it is also a promise to everyone else."
- F. "Engineering students at our university fall into three groups. Some study every day, some study only before exams, and some study only in groups."
- G. "Last Friday, I missed the bus, ran to campus in the rain, and arrived just as the exam papers were handed out. "You made it!" my friend whispered."
- H. "Universities should ban phones in exam halls. Phones make cheating easy, and they distract other students; moreover, a total ban is simpler to enforce than partial rules."

**Answers:**
- A. Process: steps in sequence.
- B. Exemplification: a claim plus instances.
- C. Cause and Effect.
- D. Comparison: the "Just as…, so…" frame from [2.2-p9].
- E. Definition: a question, then the term explained, as in [2.2-p10].
- F. Classification.
- G. Narration: time order, with the "!" inside the quotation marks.
- H. Argument/Persuasion: a claim plus supporting points.

**(b)** "The lab is noisy." is **not vivid**. Rewrite it so that it appeals to at least two senses, as in the rug example [2.2-p6].

**Model answer:**
> The basement lab hums with a dozen cooling fans, and a 3D printer clicks and whirs in the corner like an impatient clock. The air smells of hot plastic and solder, and the steel benches feel cold under your palms.

It appeals to hearing (hums, clicks, whirs), smell (hot plastic, solder), touch (cold benches) and sight (basement, steel).

### 6.2 What a grader would look for
This checklist is built from the materials' own "must" lists and ⭐ emphasis; no rubric was given in class.

**Paraphrase and Summary** [1.2-p10b, p12a, p13b, p16b]
- [ ] The meaning is accurate, with no facts added or changed.
- [ ] The words are your own, not synonym swaps.
- [ ] The sentence structure is your own, not the original's frame or order.
- [ ] The author's last name and the year are given (or the title and n.d.).
- [ ] Any phrase kept from the original is in quotation marks.
- [ ] In the class SPQ models, P restates the key idea and S covers the main points of the whole text briefly.

**Quote** [1.2-p7b, p9a, p13b; QM]
- [ ] The words are exact. The only changes are [brackets] for added words and … for omissions.
- [ ] It uses quotation marks for 39 words or fewer, or a block for 40 or more.
- [ ] It gives the author, year and page when they are available.
- [ ] There is a comma before the opening mark, or a comma inside the closing mark when the quote comes first.
- [ ] Periods and commas are inside, the period comes after a (citation), and ? or ! goes inside only if it belongs to the quote.
- [ ] A full-sentence quote starts with a capital, and a fragment starts in lowercase.

**SPQ format** [1.3]
- [ ] It has a heading (name, course, assignment, date) and a centred "Title:" line with the title in bold.
- [ ] It uses the bold labels **Paraphrase:**, **Summary:** and **Quote:** in that order.
- [ ] It ends with a reference note.

**Sentence patterns** [2.1]
- [ ] The formula and punctuation are correct, and the connector comes from the right list.
- [ ] The sentences are complete and end with full stops (the slides drop them, but you shouldn't).

**Paragraphs** [2.2]
- [ ] The paragraph does what the named pattern's definition says:
  - Exemplification: specific examples.
  - Narration: time order.
  - Description: vivid, sensory words.
  - Process: steps in order.
  - Cause and Effect: a clear cause and its effects.
  - Comparison and Contrast: two things compared or contrasted.
  - Definition: a term explained.
  - Classification: clear categories.
  - Argument: a claim backed with evidence.

### 6.3 Traps the class examples warn about
1. Swapping synonyms while keeping the original order is plagiarism [1.1-p5; 1.2-p5a].
2. A citation at the end does not excuse copied wording [1.1-p4].
3. Forgetting the quotation marks around a phrase you kept [1.2-p13b \*].
4. Changing words inside a quotation. Harvard's own slip [1.2-p12b ⚠️] shows how easy this is.
5. Using British placement ("rain".) instead of American ("rain.") [QM-p2].
6. Capitalizing a fragment quote, or failing to capitalize a full-sentence quote [QM-p1].
7. Putting the ? inside when the whole sentence, not the quote, is the question [QM-p3, items 2 and 7].
8. Putting the period before a parenthetical citation [QM-p2; 1.2-p8b].
9. Following a semicolon with something that is not a full clause, like the class typo in [2.2-p8]. After a semicolon you need a complete IC (P3, P4).
10. Changing the meaning while paraphrasing, such as "in the United States" instead of "the state" [1.3], or "failed to counterfeit" [2.3].
11. Haphazard note taking and haphazard cut-and-paste [1.2-p15b, p20a].

### 6.4 Exam-day routine for a source task (built from [1.2-p17a] and the [1.3] layout)
1. Read the passage twice. Mark the key idea and two to four main points.
2. Turn them into bullet notes, then **cover the original**.
3. Write P (the key idea in one sentence), then S (P plus the other main points), then Q (the best line, word for word).
4. Add credit: author and year (or n.d.), plus the page for quotes when it is given.
5. Recheck with the Four Rs and the end-of-quote checks in §3.5. Make sure no phrase was copied without quotation marks.
