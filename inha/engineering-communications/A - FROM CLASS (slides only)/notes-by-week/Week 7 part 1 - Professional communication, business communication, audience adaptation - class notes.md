## 1. Big picture

- **Communication means transferring information *and meaning*.** It succeeds only when understanding is transferred or negotiated [7.1-p2]. Much of Week 7 explains why messages fail (barriers, decoding, overload, wrong audience, wrong channel) and how to prevent it.
- **It is the career skill.** Communication ranks first among the qualities employers want in graduates [7.3-p4]. Engineers must connect with people outside their field [7.1-p2]. Good writers earn more [7.3-p5], and "I won't have to write" is a myth [7.3-p5].
- **Organizations run on communication.** It serves Operations, Intelligence and Relationships [7.1-p3]. Management happens through communication (Mintzberg's three jobs) [7.3-p6]. Messages inform, request/persuade and build goodwill [7.3-p8], and bad writing costs real money ($21.15 for a one-page letter, more when it fails) [7.3-p8–p9].
- **There are two sets of quality criteria.** 7.1 says practical, factual, concise, clear and persuasive [7.1-p4]. 7.3 says clear, complete, correct, saves the reader's time and builds goodwill [7.3-p9]. "No single set of words" works in every situation [7.3-p9].
- **The audience is at the centre.** Use the audience-centered approach, i.e. the "you" attitude [7.1-p8]. Five of PAIBOC's six questions are about the audience [7.4-p4]. Know the five audiences: initial, gatekeeper, primary, secondary, watchdog [7.4-p2–p3]. Analyze what they know, demographics, personality, values and past behaviour, plus discourse community and organizational culture [7.4-p6–p10]. Then adapt strategy, organization, style, design, visuals and channel [7.4-p11–p14].
- **There are two process models.** 7.1 gives the 8-step conventional model and contrasts it with the social communication model [7.1-p8–p14]. 7.4 gives a two-person model with feedback, where noise can strike at every step [7.4-p5–p6].
- **Communication is now digital, social and mobile.** The social model gives stakeholders a voice, which means less control and more complexity. Technology brings 5 benefits but needs 5 cautions. Three innovations are reshaping communication: social/workgroup systems, mobile, and AI-based intelligent communication technology [7.1-p13–p21].
- **Ethics and law.** The three forms of unethical communication are withholding, distorting and plagiarizing. Know dilemma vs lapse, the five ethics questions and the seven legal areas [7.1-p22–p25].
- **Writing mechanics.** Sentence fragments [7.3-p18] and comma splices [7.4-p18–p19].
- **For the exam:** every reading ends with a summary and comprehension questions. §6 turns them into likely midterm questions with short answers.

#### LO1: Why communication matters [7.1-p2–p5]
- ⭐ (HL) **Communication** is the process of transferring information and meaning between senders and receivers, using one or more forms of media. To count as successful it must also **transfer or negotiate understanding** [p2].
- **Figure 1.1, "Sharing Information and Understanding"** [p3]. Three exchanges between a software project manager and his boss:
  - **Transmitted meaning:** "The new app is on schedule" is accepted at face value.
  - **Negotiated meaning:** they talk it through ("two designers quit…") and agree it is on schedule but with a higher risk of slipping.
  - **Re-created meaning:** the boss reacts to the word *think* ("I think everything is fine") and builds her own, negative meaning.
- **Why it matters for your career** [p2]:
  - (HL) Great ideas are of little use unless you can express them clearly and persuasively.
  - (HL) In engineering or finance you must share complex ideas with executives, customers and colleagues. Connecting with people outside your field "can be as important as your technical expertise".
  - In the gig economy (Uber, Lyft) independent contractors must "sell yourself" into each new contract.
  - Top executives spend most of the workday communicating.
  - The world is full of good engineers and accountants but "not full of good communicators", which is your chance to stand out.
- ⭐ (HL) **Why it matters for your company: three areas** [p3]
  1. **Operations:** fast, effective communication between managers and staff, within and between departments, and with external partners. Bottlenecks reduce efficiency and cause quality or safety problems.
  2. **Intelligence:** keep a constant "ear to the ground" for opportunities, risks and impending problems, both inside and outside.
  3. **Relationships:** build connections with all **stakeholders**, i.e. any persons or organizations significantly affected by the company's decisions and operations (employees, customers, investors, creditors, suppliers, local communities). Communication also creates the emotional connections behind a healthy workplace.
- ⭐ (HL) **What makes business communication effective: practical, factual, concise, clear, persuasive** [p4]
  1. *Provide practical information* that helps recipients solve problems, pursue opportunities or act.
  2. *Give facts rather than vague impressions:* concrete language, specific detail, accurate and ethical content. Support opinions with evidence.
  3. *Communicate efficiently.* Concise messages respect people's time and get better responses.
  4. *Clarify expectations and responsibilities.* Aim for a specific response and state what you expect, or what you can do for them.
  5. *Offer compelling arguments and recommendations.* Show readers how *they* benefit from responding as you want.
- ⭐ **Figure 1.2, ineffective vs effective email** [p5]. The email is a team notice about a meeting with an outside customer-service consultant (who will advise on social-media tools). The blue annotations (a)–(h) point out what changed. The highlighted words are (HL) "vague", "cold", "necessary", "accusatory", "not the date", "confusion".

| Element | Ineffective version | Effective version |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Subject line | vague ("Social media strategy") | informative: topic + day + time span |
| (b) Greeting | cold ("All,") | friendly, not too casual ("Hi team,") |
| (c) Opening | no background for people who missed the last meeting; consultant called "This guy" | fills in who the consultant is, his track record and how long he will stay |
| (d) Request | negative and accusatory ("most of you just sat and stared"); unclear who does what by when | upbeat, stresses the value of the meeting; says who prepares questions, who to send them to, and the deadline |
| (e) Meeting details | day only; "10:00 a.m. to whenever" | full date and time span, plus a deadline to register a lunch choice |
| (f) People who can't attend | assumes they don't want to come; no invitation to ask questions | offers dial-in and web slides without guilt-tripping; invites questions ahead of time |
| (g) Closing | none, so the email sounds harsh and abrupt | warm "Thanks," |
| (h) Signature | first name only | full signature: name, title, office and mobile numbers |

#### LO2: Communicating as a professional [7.1-p4–p8]
- ⭐ (HL) **Professionalism** is the quality of performing at a high level and conducting oneself with purpose and pride. It means going beyond minimum expectations and committing to meaningful contributions [p4].
- ⭐ **Figure 1.3, six elements of professionalism** [p6]:
  - **Be the best:** strive to excel.
  - **Be dependable:** keep promises, meet commitments, learn from and take responsibility for mistakes.
  - **Be a team player:** contribute to a larger cause and make others better.
  - **Be respectful:** good etiquette.
  - **Be positive:** keep a positive outlook even when things get tough.
  - **Be ethical:** avoid lapses and weigh dilemmas carefully.
  - The key message: every element depends on effective communication [p4].
- ⭐ (HL) **What employers expect: four communication competencies** [p4, p6]
  1. **Acquiring, processing and sharing information**, called **digital information fluency**: recognize information needs, find and evaluate reliable sources, organize information into cohesive messages, and use it ethically. It includes **critical thinking**, the ability to evaluate evidence completely and objectively to form logical conclusions and sound recommendations.
  2. **Fostering positive working relationships:** listening, etiquette, resolving conflicts respectfully, communicating with people from diverse backgrounds.
  3. **Representing your employer in public:** act responsibly on social media and elsewhere; follow standard grammar, spelling and quality writing and speaking.
  4. **Using the employer's tools efficiently.** Almost all business communication is technology-assisted.
- **"Apply Your Skills Now: Practice Your Professionalism" box** [p7]. Three habits to build now:
  1. **Communicating with instructors:** write as if to a high-level executive (no "Yo, prof"). Use a respectful greeting, complete sentences and standard punctuation, and read how each instructor communicates.
  2. **Quality of your work,** in both substance and presentation.
  3. **Scheduling and commitments:** learn to estimate how long things take, and meet deadlines.
- ⭐ (HL) **Formal communication network.** Ideas and information flow along the lines of command in the organization chart (Figure 1.4 [p7]), in **four directions** [p6, p8]:
  - **Downward:** executives → middle managers → frontline. Carries decisions and the information employees need to do their jobs.
  - **Upward:** employees → top. Gives leaders insight into problems, trends, opportunities, grievances and performance.
  - **Horizontal (lateral):** between departments, to share information, coordinate tasks and solve complex problems.
  - **Diagonal:** crosses department lines while moving up or down.
  - Horizontal and diagonal flows keep communication from being "stifled" in the vertical lines.
- ⭐ (HL) **Informal communication network:** all communication outside the formal channels [p8].
  - Some happens naturally on the job and in social settings; some fills gaps where the formal network does not give employees the information they want.
  - The limits of formal networks helped spur business use of social media.
  - It is healthy and important, but a workplace "rife with rumors and gossip" is a sign the formal network is not working.
- ⭐ (HL) **Audience-centered approach, also called the "you" attitude** [p8]. Understand and respect your audience members and get the message across in a way that is meaningful *to them*. "You" means the receiver, in contrast to messages about "me", the sender.
  - Learn their beliefs, education, status, communication style, and personal and professional concerns.
  - If you can't find out, project yourself into their position using common sense and imagination.
  - (HL) **Emotional intelligence** is the ability to read other people's emotions accurately and manage your own productively. Relating to others' needs is a key part of it.
  - Professional etiquette is a vital part of this approach (the book covers it in Ch. 2, which is not part of this reading).

#### LO3: The communication process [7.1-p8–p14]
- ⭐ (HL) **Conventional communication model, 8 steps** (Figure 1.5) [p8–p9]
  1. **The sender has an idea.** The motive matters: wanting to solve a problem produces a better message than wanting to complain.
  2. **The sender encodes the idea as a message.** A **message** is the "container" for an idea; **encoding** means expressing it in words or images.
  3. **The sender produces the message in a transmittable medium.** The **medium** is the form: oral, written or visual.
  4. **The sender transmits the message through a channel.** The **channel** is the delivery system (e.g., Twitter, email). The medium is *what form* the message takes; the channel is *how it travels*.
  5. **The audience receives the message.** Arrival does not guarantee it is noticed or understood.
  6. **The audience decodes the message,** i.e. extracts the idea (**decoding**).
  7. **The audience responds.** That requires them to remember the message, be able to act on it, and be motivated to act.
  8. **The audience gives feedback** to the sender: verbal, nonverbal or both. Feedback must be decoded too (a smile can mean many things).
  - The model covers only **one cycle**. A real conversation can take dozens of cycles [p9–p10].
- ⭐ (HL) **Barriers in the communication environment** [p10]
  - **Noise and distractions.** External ones include uncomfortable rooms and pop-up reminders; internal ones are thoughts and emotions. **Multitasking** is really switching between tasks, and focus suffers at every switch.
  - **Competing messages.** An audience's undivided attention is "a rare luxury".
  - **Filters:** any human or technological intervention between sender and receiver. They can be intentional (auto-filing email) or unintentional (an overly aggressive spam filter). Organizational structure and culture can also filter, and intermediaries may distort a message for their own needs.
  - **Channel breakdowns:** the channel simply fails, e.g., a colleague forgets to forward your message.
  - Minimizing barriers is everyone's job: mute your phone in meetings, don't talk over cubicle walls, keep music down. Handle messages in batches instead of letting them interrupt you all day.
- ⭐ (HL) **How audiences receive messages** [p11]
  - A receiver must **sense** the message, **select** it from the others competing for attention, and **perceive** it as a real message (the busy-street analogy).
  - **Selective attention** means focusing on a subset of incoming stimuli and ignoring the rest. It can block your message (or a warning car horn).
  - **Five principles for getting messages noticed:**
    1. *Consider audience expectations.* Use the media and channels they expect (e.g., don't suddenly move meeting notices from email to a blog).
    2. *Make messages user-friendly:* easy to find, navigate and read.
    3. *Emphasize familiarity:* familiar words, images and designs (company information belongs on an "About Us" page).
    4. *Practice empathy:* address their wants and needs, which is "the essence of the 'you' attitude".
    5. *Design for compatibility:* the message must work on the audience's devices (mobile screens).
- ⭐ (HL) **How audiences decode messages** [p11–p12]
  - Meaning is assigned personally and is shaped by culture, experience, learning and thinking styles, ego, hopes, fears, beliefs and moods.
  - Self-defense mechanisms ignore, deny or distort threatening information ("You only hear what you want to hear"). Example: wanting to reject the one negative review among four of your business plan.
  - Language and usage differ: does "as soon as possible" mean 10 seconds, 10 minutes or 10 days? Clarify expectations and remove ambiguity.
  - Thinking styles differ: objective logic vs emotion or intuition.
  - **Figure 1.6, shared experience** [p12]: little shared experience → dissimilar meanings → misunderstanding; an average amount → similar meanings → average understanding; a large amount → very similar meanings → high understanding. **The less you share with your audience, the more background and context you must give.**
- ⭐ (HL) **How audiences respond: three conditions** [p12–p13]
  1. **They remember it.** Sensory memory → short-term memory (seconds) → long-term memory (stored actively by memorizing, or passively by linking to existing knowledge) → retrieved when needed. Messages that reflect the audience's wants and needs are remembered better.
  2. **They are able to respond.** Know what the audience can actually do.
  3. **They are motivated to respond.** Show the benefits of responding.
- ⭐ (HL) **The social communication model** [p13–p14]
  - The conventional model reflects a **publishing/broadcasting mindset**: scripted messages go out to a mass audience with few ways to respond. Internally it is "**we talk, you listen**", with managers issuing directives downward.
  - The **social model is interactive, conversational and usually open to all who wish to participate.** Audiences are active participants. The sender *starts a conversation* (asks a question or shares valuable information), and participants revise it: they expand, confirm, amplify or refute it.
  - **Figure 1.7** contrasts the two models (the full table is in §4.3).
  - **Two disadvantages for business** [p14]:
    1. **Less control.** Stakeholders have the "megaphone power" of the crowd (e.g., boycotts, pressure on where companies advertise), and messages "in the wild" get amplified, distorted or misused.
    2. **Greater complexity.** There are more channels to monitor, more noise to filter out, more false information, and more information overload.

#### LO4: Using technology [7.1-p14–p21]
- ⭐ (HL) **Five potential benefits of communication technology** [p14]
  1. **Greater effectiveness:** helps craft clearer, more persuasive messages.
  2. **Greater efficiency:** less time and effort to create, send and consume messages.
  3. **Better and easier research.**
  4. **Improved decision-making:** guidance through complex sets of data.
  5. **Fewer barriers:** more people can take part in communication.
- ⭐ (HL) **Five points for using technology well** [p14–p15]
  1. **Keep technology in perspective.** It is an aid to communication, not a replacement for it.
  2. **Guard against information overload,** i.e. receiving more information than you can effectively process. It causes distraction, stress, mistakes and breakdowns. As a receiver, be your own gatekeeper, prune your channels and use filters. As a sender, don't send unnecessary or poorly crafted messages that need rounds of clarification.
  3. **Use your tools wisely.** The **information technology paradox** says tools can waste as much time as they save. Misuse can also expose companies to lawsuits and security breaches.
  4. **Use your tools efficiently.** Know the basic features; managers must train their staff.
  5. **Reconnect with people.** Messaging, email and other text-heavy modes easily cause misunderstandings and "bruised feelings". If you're stuck in confusion or negativity, phone or visit in person.
- ⭐ **Three sets of technology reshaping communication** [p15–p17]
  1. **Social and workgroup communication systems.** (HL) **Social media** are digital platforms that let stakeholders share, revise, respond to or contribute content. Companies use public networks to reach customers, plus private internal networks with shared files, group messaging, real-time collaboration and virtual meetings.
  2. **Mobile communication.**
     - More than half of internet access is now mobile. Mobile is an essential part of the digital workplace: flexibility, productivity, collaboration and engaging experiences.
     - For many employees the phone is the *primary* connection to company networks, with thousands of business apps. Examples: Rockwell Automation's FactoryTalk TeamONE app for diagnosing production-line problems; Figure 1.8, the Zinc app for deskless workers.
     - Implications: reading and writing on small screens is harder and error-prone, so readers misinterpret or stop reading ("phablets" help only a little). Phones are deeply personal, so mobile communication becomes a "continuous stream of conversations".
  3. **Intelligent communication technology (ICT).** (HL) ICT uses artificial intelligence to enhance the communication experience.
     - Why AI took off: research shifted from **general/strong AI** to **narrow/weak AI**; neural networks became far more powerful; and big data, cheap storage and fast processors arrived together [p17].
     - Core techniques: **machine learning and deep learning** (HL: deep learning is machine learning with *multiple layers* of neural networks and drives most practical tools); **natural language processing** (understanding and generating language, including natural language generation); and **computer vision** (identifying the content of images and video).
- **Special feature, "Empowering Communicators with ICT"** [p18–p21]. Tools grouped by the five benefits:

| Benefit | Tools named in the reading |
|---|---|
| More effective | **augmented writing** (suggests better wording, e.g., Textio) · **augmented reality** (on-the-spot guidance for technicians repairing equipment) |
| More efficient | **applicant evaluation systems** (screen résumés, chatbot interviews, video analysis) · **automated writing** (Yahoo! Sports fantasy-league recaps) · **chatbots and taskbots** (Sam's Club Gift Genie; X.ai's "Amy/Andrew" schedule meetings) |

- **"Essential ICT Terms" box** [p21]
  - **AI** applies computing power to replicate aspects of human intelligence in three stages: collect data → analyze it to make decisions → apply the results.
  - **Strong AI** (also called artificial general intelligence) would comprehensively replicate human intelligence, including transferring learning across domains. **Weak (narrow) AI** handles one specific problem. *All AI with meaningful business impact today is weak AI.*
  - **Machine learning:** a system teaches itself to improve. **Deep learning:** layered neural networks.
  - **Augmented intelligence / hybrid intelligence / cognitive automation:** computers and humans assist each other.
  - **NLP**, including speech recognition.
  - **Data and text mining:** extracting insights from large collections of numbers or text.

#### LO5: Ethical and legal communication [7.1-p22–p25]
- ⭐ (HL) **Ethics** are the accepted principles of right and wrong that govern behaviour and decision-making in a society. Communication is the "public face" of a company, so regulators, legislators, investors, and consumer, environmental and labour groups scrutinize it closely [p22].
- ⭐ (HL) **Ethical communication** includes all the information the audience needs to make an informed decision or take an informed stance, and is not deceptive in any way. Intentionally violating that trust is unethical communication [p22].
- ⭐ (HL) **Three forms of unethical communication** [p22–p23] (some are also illegal):
  1. **Withholding information**, e.g., dodging responsibility for mistakes, or presenting an incomplete set of facts in a proposal.
     - **Transparency** is a sense of openness that gives all participants the information they need to process messages accurately.
     - Audiences deserve to know when they are being marketed to, and by whom:
       - **Native advertising** (sponsored content): ads designed to look like news stories, articles or posts. The U.S. FTC requires a "sponsored" label if consumers could be misled.
       - **Stealth marketing:** promoting without making clear that marketing is taking place. Example: "street team" members who don't disclose they are rewarded by the company.
  2. **Distorting information** through words, numbers or images:
     - selectively misquoting someone;
     - using averages to hide extreme values;
     - manipulating trend calculations (boasting of a 40% sales jump in April when it only recovered from a disastrous March);
     - altering photos, or changing chart scales to exaggerate or hide differences;
     - **deep fakes** in sound and video.
  3. **Plagiarizing.** **Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words or creative work as your own. It becomes illegal if it violates **copyright**, the legal protection for creators of writing, design, programming and recordings.
     - To use others' work properly: document sources, label others' words and images as theirs, use only minor portions (brief quotes), and sometimes get written permission.
     - You can be sued even if you didn't copy word for word and didn't profit.
     - **Fair use** gives some flexibility (especially for noncommercial use) but sets no precise limits, and you can only invoke it as a defense **after** you are sued. You can't pre-label someone else's content "fair use" [p23].
- ⭐ (HL) **Ethical dilemma vs ethical lapse** [p23]
  - **Ethical dilemma:** a choice between alternatives that aren't clear-cut. Either both are ethical and valid, or both sit in the gray area. *Example:* employees want higher wages while investors want low costs, and both positions are valid.
  - **Ethical lapse:** a clearly unethical choice. *Example:* telling a customer you can finish by a date you know you can't meet, which "is simply dishonest. There is no ethical dilemma here."
- ⭐ **Figures 1.9 vs 1.10: the SunStar Sports "Pegasus" memo** [p24–p25]. Two product managers write to the executive committee about market research on their pet project.
  - **Unethical version (1.9):**
    - The subject line ("Pegasus market confirmation") is misleading.
    - "As we predicted" shows their minds were made up before the research.
    - The researcher's reputation is used to imply support she never gave, and her own words aren't shown.
    - "Only a third" had little or no interest is spun into "a two-thirds majority in our favor".
    - Their opinions are mixed in with hers.
    - The close assumes the committee already agrees and would be wrong to disagree.
  - **Ethical version (1.10):**
    - The subject line is neutral ("Market research summary…").
    - It explains the method (236 buyers in 18 states) and the researcher's experience, and offers the full report.
    - It quotes the researcher's summary in full under its own heading: 34% little or no interest, 37% moderate to strong, **29% confused** (a warning about the design).
    - "Our recommendations" is a separate section: the $7.6M development cost is too high for this much uncertainty, so put the project on hold and re-test.
    - It closes by inviting questions.
- ⭐ (HL) **Ensuring ethical communication** [p23–p24]
  - Employers should set clear guidelines, often a written **code of ethics** (a document of the company's ethics policies and expectations for all employees), backed by training and channels such as ethics hotlines.
  - Managers must model ethical behaviour and support employees facing dilemmas. Every employee is responsible for communicating ethically.
  - ⭐ (HL) **The five questions** (margin version: honorable? needed information? effects? rights? a better approach?):
    1. Is my intention honorable, and does it show respect for my audience?
    2. Am I giving readers or listeners all the information they need for an informed stance or decision?
    3. Have I considered the effect on the audience and anyone else affected?
    4. Am I respecting the legal and moral rights of anyone whose information or ideas I use?
    5. Could a different approach produce a more positive outcome for everyone involved?
  - **Tie-breaker:** picture explaining your decision to someone whose opinion you value. Could you comfortably live with your choice?
- ⭐ (HL) **Ensuring legal communication: seven areas** [p24–p25]
  1. **Promotional communication.** Truth-in-advertising laws cover false or deceptive ads, packaging claims, celebrity testimonials, and **bait-and-switch** (advertise a cheap product to lure customers in, then push a pricier one).
  2. **Contracts.** A **contract** is a legally binding promise: one party makes a specified offer and the other accepts. Covers employment, purchasing and leasing, project services, nondisclosure agreements.
  3. **Employee communication.** Laws govern communication with potential and current employees, e.g., employers must inform staff about hazards such as toxic chemicals.
  4. **Intellectual property.** **IP** covers copyrighted materials plus *industrial property* such as patents, product designs and logos.
  5. **Financial reporting.** Publicly traded companies must follow stringent reporting laws.
  6. **Defamation:** the intentional public communication of false statements that damage character or reputation. Written defamation is **libel**; spoken defamation is **slander**.
  7. **Transparency requirements.** E.g., the U.S. FTC requires influencers (celebrities, athletes) to disclose company relationships when they promote products on social media.
  - If you are unsure whether a message is legal, ask your legal department.

**Context: why communication matters now** [7.3-p2–p5]
- "Change" sums up the early 21st century: technology, globalization and economic turbulence [p2]. Workers with high-level skills and education weather it best, and "soft skills" such as communication matter more as employers scrutinize applicants [p3].
- **Literacy gap.** (HL) A Pew-funded study found that more than half of 4-year and 75% of 2-year college graduates can't handle complex real-life literacy tasks [p3]. About 40 million U.S. adults have limited literacy, and corporations may spend $3.1 billion fixing writing deficiencies [p5].
- ⭐ (HL) **Work requires communication** [p3]. People communicate to plan products and services; hire, train and motivate workers; coordinate manufacturing and delivery; persuade customers to buy; and bill them. For many organizations the "product" *is* information or a service, created and delivered through communication.
- ⭐ (HL) **Communication takes many forms** [p4]
  - **Verbal** (uses words): face-to-face or phone conversations · meetings · text, e-mail and voice-mail messages · letters and memos · reports.
  - **Nonverbal** (no words): pictures · company logos · gestures and body language · **who sits where at a meeting** · **how long someone keeps a visitor waiting**.
- **What employers say** [p4–p5]:
  - (HL) A National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) survey put teamwork and verbal communication at the top of the skills employers want.
  - (HL) 99% of people surveyed say global success depends on critical thinking and analysis; 88% want schools to teach communication; reading comprehension ranked highest of 14 skill sets.
  - Communication ability ranks **first** among the qualities employers look for in college graduates.
  - (HL) A career counselor's "transferable skills": the ability to "analyze, write, persuade, and manage".
  - Writers in the top 20% earn more than 3× as much as those in the bottom 20%.
- Sidebar [p3]: pilots must speak English clearly enough to avoid confusion with the control tower; the UN issued new recommendations after accidents where poor English was a factor.

⭐ (HL) **LO1-1: "Will I really have to write?" "Yes. A lot."** Four myths [7.3-p5]:

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| 1. "Secretaries will do all my writing." | Automation and restructuring mean managers do their own writing, data entry and calls. |
| 2. "I'll use form letters or templates." | A **form letter** is a prewritten fill-in-the-blank letter for standard situations. That's fine for routine cases, but the higher you rise the more non-routine problems need creative solutions. |
| 3. "I'm being hired as an accountant, not a writer." | Almost every entry-level professional or managerial job requires e-mails, talks to small groups and paper documents. Doing them well gets you promoted. |
| 4. "I'll just pick up the phone." | Important calls need written follow-up. People put things in writing to **make themselves visible, create a record, convey complex data, make things convenient for the reader, save money, and convey their own messages more effectively.** "If it isn't in writing, it didn't happen." |

⭐ (HL) **LO1-2: "Business communication differs from other school writing."** [7.3-p5–p6] Both need standard edited English; everything else differs:

| Dimension | School writing | Business communication |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | show you learned the material and demonstrate intelligence | **meet an organizational need**: "No one will pay you to write something that he or she already knows." |
| **Audience** | the instructor (and classmates), who is paid to read even boring papers | people inside **and** outside the organization, who read only what seems **important, relevant, and interesting** |
| **Information** | new to you, rarely new to the instructor | **usually new to the reader** (if not, work extra hard to make it interesting) |
| **Organization** | traditional essay: thesis, evidence paragraphs, conclusion | built around the **reader's psychological needs**; usually **main point up front** |
| **Style** | often formal; big words and long sentences rewarded | **friendly, not formal**; short words, a mix of sentence and paragraph lengths |
| **Document design** | long paragraphs, double spacing, no visual design | **skimmable**: headings, lists, single-spaced paragraphs with space between them |
| **Visuals** | rarely anything but words (except math, construction, engineering) | choose the most effective form; even a one-page memo may contain a table or graph |

⭐ (HL) **LO1-3: "Management happens through communication."** [7.3-p6–p8]
- **Mintzberg: managers have three basic jobs** [p6]:
  1. collect and convey information;
  2. make decisions;
  3. promote **interpersonal unity**, i.e. make people want to work together to achieve organizational goals.
  - All three happen through communication. Effective managers interpret the grapevine, speak well in small groups and formal presentations, and write well [p6–p7].
- ⭐ (HL) **Internal audiences** are people in the same organization: **superiors, peers, subordinates**. Figure 1.1 shows the Sales Manager–West's audiences: the President and VPs above, the other sales managers beside, district managers and sales reps below [p7].
- ⭐ (HL) **External audiences** are people outside the organization: **customers, suppliers, unions, stockholders, potential employees, government agencies, the press, the general public** [p7]. Figure 1.2 adds investors, lenders, distributors, franchisees, legislators, the courts, competitors, special-interest groups, trade associations, professional services and foreign governments.
- (HL) **Listening, speaking and interpersonal communication** matter as much as formal documents [p8]. Newcomers must listen to learn their duties and the organization's values and culture. Informal chitchat connects you to the **grapevine**, an informal source of company information. You may be asked to speak to small groups, and networking and workgroups are crucial.
- ⭐ (HL) **Three basic purposes of messages** [p8]
  1. **Inform:** explain something or tell readers something.
  2. **Request or persuade:** you want the reader to act. *Request* suggests the action is easy or routine; *persuade* means you must motivate and convince.
  3. **Build goodwill:** create a good image of yourself and your organization, the kind that makes people want to do business with you.
  - **Most messages have several purposes.** Answering a question informs *and* shows you are competent. A claims adjustment, whether yes or no, should show careful, fair, businesslike consideration. A résumé informs *and* persuades through its layout.

⭐ (HL) **LO1-4: "$21.15 a page, even more if it doesn't work."** [7.3-p8–p9]
- **The calculation:** a one-page letter takes about **54 minutes** to plan, compose and revise. At **$23.50/hour** (wages + benefits) that is **$21.15**, since 54/60 × 23.50 = 21.15. At scale, a Minneapolis company sending 3,000 letters a day spends over $66,000, plus $1,000 in stamps.
- **Approval cycles** (writer ↔ superior, repeated several times) raise the cost. An engineering firm spends $500,000 on an average military proposal and $1 million on a large one [p8].
- **Poor correspondence costs even more:** wasted time, wasted effort and lost goodwill [p8]. (HL) **Bad writing wastes time by:**
  1. taking more time to read;
  2. needing more revision and more rounds of revision;
  3. confusing ideas, so discussions and decisions drag on;
  4. delaying action while the reader asks for more information or works out the meaning [p9].
- **Ineffective messages don't get results.** A reader who has to guess may guess wrong; a reader who finds a request unconvincing or insulting won't act, so 2nd, 3rd and 4th requests follow. Every letter, memo or report either enhances or damages the reader's image of the writer [p9].
- **Good communication pays.** Companies with the most effective employee-communication programs had higher shareholder returns, lower turnover and 30% higher market value [p9]. Sidebar: a typo that entered a $16 million trade as $16 *billion* may have helped drop the Dow 700+ points in 15 minutes (2010) [p9].

⭐ (HL) **LO1-5: "Good messages meet five criteria."** [7.3-p9] Good business and administrative writing:
1. **Is clear.** The meaning the reader gets is the meaning the writer intended; the reader doesn't have to guess.
2. **Is complete.** All the reader's questions are answered, with enough information to evaluate the message and act on it.
3. **Is correct.** All information is accurate, with no errors in punctuation, spelling, grammar, word order or sentence structure.
4. **Saves the reader's time.** Style, organization and visual impact help the reader read, understand and act quickly.
5. **Builds goodwill.** It presents a positive image of the writer and organization, treats the reader as **a person, not a number**, and cements the relationship.

- ⭐ Whether a message meets the criteria depends on the interaction of the **writer, the audience, the purposes and the situation**: "No single set of words will work in all possible situations" (Instant Replay [p10]).
- (HL) **Better writing helps you** [p9]:
  - **save time:** less reading time, no rewriting, fewer "What did you mean?" questions;
  - **make your efforts more effective:** more requests granted promptly *on the first request*;
  - **communicate your points more clearly:** fewer misunderstandings, and disagreements surface and get resolved sooner;
  - **build goodwill** for your organization and an image of yourself as knowledgeable and capable.

⭐ (HL) **LO1-6: "How should I analyze business communication situations? Try PAIBOC."** [7.3-p10, p12–p13]
- (HL) **Five questions to ask before you write or speak** [p10]
  1. **What's at stake, and to whom?** Consider your needs plus your boss's and readers' concerns, and the whole context (shareholders, customers, regulators). When stakes are high, consider emotions as well as facts.
  2. **Should you send a message?** Sometimes, especially when you're new, silence is the most tactful response. But watch for chances to learn, influence and make your case; communication builds careers.
  3. **What channel should you use?** Paper documents and presentations are formal and give you more control. E-mail, phone calls and stopping by are less formal. Oral channels are better for group decision making, clear up misunderstandings faster and feel more personal. Sometimes you need more than one message, in more than one channel.
  4. **What should you say?** How much detail, and whether to repeat what they already know, depends on the document, your purposes, the audiences and the corporate culture.
  5. **How should you say it?** The order of your ideas and your word choice shape the response.
- ⭐ **A good solution must both solve the organizational problem and meet the psychological needs of the people involved** (Instant Replay [p12]).
- **Problem-solving strategies** [p10, p12]:
  - **Understand the situation:** What are the facts? What extra information would help, and where can you get it?
  - **Brainstorm solutions:** develop several, then test them against your audience and purposes.
  - (HL) **Get permission before adding or changing information.** You can't change facts (if it's October you can't pretend it's April), but you may change outdated company habits. Check with your supervisor first.
  - (HL) **Use the PAIBOC questions.**
- ⭐ (HL) **PAIBOC (Figure 1.3)** is an acronym for the questions to answer **before** composing [p12–p13]:
  - **P: Purposes.** What must the message do for the organization and for you? What should readers know, think, feel or do? What image of yourself and the organization should it project? List major *and* minor purposes. Example: one policy announcement can aim to announce, explain the requirements, make readers like the policy, show the organization cares, and show you are a competent manager.
  - **A: Audience(s).** Who are they, how do they differ, and which characteristics matter *for this message*? What do they already know, and how will they respond? Writing to a group (e.g., all employees) means spotting the economic, cultural or situational differences between subgroups.
  - **I: Information.** List every point that must be included and check the draft against the list. If you're unsure whether a fact belongs, ask. *Tip:* to include something **without emphasizing it**, put it in the middle of a paragraph or document and keep it brief.
  - **B: Benefits and reasons.** Brainstorm reasons, logic and reader benefits. They needn't be money; making the reader's job easier or more pleasant counts. **Identify at least five benefits** for an informative or persuasive message and use the strongest. Adapt them to the reader: "the company saves money" helps employees only if the savings reach them, which is rare.
  - **O: Objections.** What negatives must you **deemphasize** (some can only be deemphasized) or **overcome**? Look for an advantage associated with the negative, or rephrase/redefine it.
  - **C: Context.** Your relationship with the reader (liked or resented, older or younger), morale, whether the organization is prospering or struggling, the economy, interest rates, industry and regulatory climate, news, weather, **time of year**, any special circumstances. Draw on experience, courses, common sense, the *Wall Street Journal*, annual reports, or a call to a local businessperson.

**"Polishing Your Prose": sentence fragments** [7.3-p18]
- A complete sentence needs a **subject and a verb**. A **fragment** is missing one ("Passed seven rounds of interviews.") or is a **subordinate clause standing alone** ("Although I read my e-mail"). Some sentences have a subject and verb but are still fragmentary in thought ("I need.").
- **Fixes:** join the fragment to other words to make a complete sentence, or add information.
- **Subordinating words** (they turn a clause into one that can't stand alone): after · although, though · because, since · before, until · if · when, whenever · while, as.
- Fragments are acceptable in résumés, ads and some sales or fund-raising letters. But (HL) "fragments are inappropriate for most business documents. Because they are incomplete, they can confuse or mislead readers." Readers also judge people who make errors as unprofessional or unpromotable.

⭐ **Learning objectives** [7.4-p1]
- **LO2-1** Understand expectations from your organization.
- **LO2-2** Define audiences for messages.
- **LO2-3** Apply strategies for audience analysis with PAIBOC.
- **LO2-4** Apply strategies for individual and group audience analyses.
- **LO2-5** Apply strategies for audience needs analysis.
- **LO2-6** Adapt messages for audiences.

**Introduction** [7.4-p1]
- (HL) Understanding your audience is fundamental to any message's success. Adapt the message to fit the audience's **goals, interests, and needs**.
- Adaptation can be **cynical and manipulative** or **sensitive, empathic and ethical**. Audiences sense manipulation, so empathic adaptation is "almost always more successful, as well as being more ethical".
- Refusing to adapt in the name of "honesty" (criticizing bosses as sharply as you would a sibling) backfires. Almost all organizations expect deference to people in authority, and customers can take their business to companies that treat them respectfully.
- Before sending a tweet or e-mail, think about **all the audiences that may read it**.

**LO2-1: Understanding what your organization wants** ("Building a Critical Skill" box) [7.4-p2]
- **Case:** Michelle suspected her boss was sexist because everyone else got promoted. In fact he valued **punctuality**, and her well-written reports were always late.
- Workplaces have rules about what "**counts**", and they differ even within one industry: some bosses are strict about spelling, some want original ideas and others obedience, some love new technology and others are technophobic.
- **To find out what counts:**
  1. **Ask** your boss: "What parts of my job are most important? What's the biggest thing I could do to improve my work?"
  2. **Listen** to the stories colleagues tell about people who succeeded and failed, and check your reading with them ("So his real problem was…?").
  3. **Observe** who is praised and who is promoted.
- It's a two-way street: Zappos listened to employees (nap room, after-hours social events, staff encouraged to tweet about the company).

⭐ (HL) **LO2-2: "Who is my audience?" "More people than you might think!"** A message may have **five separate audiences** [7.4-p2–p4]:
1. **Primary audience:** decides whether to accept your recommendations, or acts on the message. You must reach the decision maker.
2. **Secondary audience:** asked to comment on the message or to implement it once approved. It can include lawyers who use your message years later as evidence of the organization's culture and practices.
3. **Initial audience:** receives the message first and routes it to the others. It sometimes assigns the message.
4. **Gatekeeper:** can stop the message before it reaches the primary audience, e.g., a secretary who controls access to the boss. Often it is the supervisor who assigned the message, but it can be higher up or outside the organization.
5. **Watchdog audience:** can't stop the message and won't act on it directly, but has **political, social or economic power**. It watches the exchange between you and the primary audience and may base future actions on it.

- **One person can play two roles.** A supervisor is often both initial audience and gatekeeper, and the initial audience is sometimes also the primary audience [p3].
- ⭐ **Figure 2.1, audiences for a marketing plan** [p3]:
  - Writer: an account executive at an ad agency.
  - Initial audience: her boss, who asks her to write the plan. Gatekeeper: the same boss, who must approve it before it goes to the client.
  - Primary audience: the client company's executive committee.
  - Secondary audiences: the client's marketing staff (they comment) and the artists, writers and media buyers (they implement).
- ⭐ **Figure 2.2, audiences for a consulting report** [p3]:
  - Writers: two workers at a consulting think tank.
  - Initial audience and gatekeeper: the consortium of manufacturers that hired them to study proposed federal regulations. If the consortium dislikes the report, it won't send it on.
  - Primary audience: the federal regulatory agency (economists, engineers, policymakers).
  - Secondary audiences: the general public, other manufacturers, the think tank's other clients and competitors.
  - Watchdog: the industry reviewers who commented on drafts. Their goodwill mattered for future contracts, so the authors took their comments most seriously.
- **Sidebar:** Nivea for Men's ad urging men to "re-civilize" themselves caused public outcry and an apology. Consider *all* audiences, and rethink messages that appeal to some audiences at others' expense [p3].
- ⭐ The **Instant Replay box "Five Kinds of Audiences"** [p4] gives the one-line versions:
  - Initial: first to receive, may assign the message.
  - Gatekeeper: can stop it.
  - Primary: decides or acts.
  - Secondary: comments or implements.
  - Watchdog: has power and may act later based on its evaluation.

⭐ (HL) **LO2-3: "Why is my audience so important?" "To be successful, messages must meet the audiences' needs."** [7.4-p4–p6]
- Good business communication is **audience-centered**. Audience is central to both PAIBOC and the communication process [p4].
- **PAIBOC seen through the audience:** five of the six questions (A, I, B, O, C) relate to the audience [p4–p5]:
  - **P:** purposes come from you and your organization. The audience determines *how* you achieve them, not *what* they are.
  - **A:** asks about the audience directly.
  - **I:** what information to give depends on the audience. Say more when the topic is new to them. If they may have forgotten it, protect their ego with "As you know…" or a subordinate clause ("Because we had delivery problems last quarter, …").
  - **B:** what counts as a good reason or benefit depends on the audience. Some are swayed by personal experience, others by scientific studies or experts. For growing vegetables, one person values saving money; another values avoiding chemicals, rare varieties or fresh air.
  - **O:** different audiences have different attitudes; one objects to a price increase, another sees it as routine.
  - **C:** people exist in a context. How well they know and like you and your organization, the economy and recent news all shape their response.
- ⭐ (HL) **Figure 2.3, a two-person communication model with feedback** [p5–p6]:
  - **Sender ("Alex"):** **perceives** a stimulus (literal sight, hearing, taste, smell, touch) → **interprets** it (important? unusual?) → **chooses/selects** what to send → **encodes** it. Encoding uses words but also gestures, clothing and pictures (nonverbal symbols); (HL) "the stage of putting ideas into any of these symbols is called encoding". Then he **transmits** it through a **channel** (memos, calls, meetings, billboards, TV ads, e-mail).
  - **Receiver ("Barbara"):** **perceives** it → (HL) **decodes** it, i.e. extracts meaning from the symbols → **interprets** → **chooses a response** → **encodes** it → sends it back as **feedback**.
  - (HL) **Feedback** may be **direct and immediate** or **indirect and delayed**, and **verbal or nonverbal**.
  - ⭐ **Noise** (anything that interferes) can occur at *every* step:
    - **physical noise:** static on a phone line, a roaring lawn mower, hard-to-read handwriting;
    - **psychological noise:** disliking the speaker, worrying about something else, having already made up your mind.
  - (HL) **Channel overload** is when the channel can't handle all the messages being sent (a small business with only two phone lines). **Information overload** is when more messages arrive than the human receiver can handle. People cope by "first come, first served", by picking the most important messages, or by relying on summaries made by others, and "none of these ways is completely satisfactory".
  - **Miscommunication** can happen at every stage: misperceiving, misinterpreting, choosing badly, encoding poorly, or using the wrong channel. Different **frames of reference** also cause it, because we interpret through our experiences, cultures and subcultures, and the moment in history we live in.
  - **The common-ground rule:** choose information the audience *needs and will find interesting*, encode it in symbols they understand, and send it through a channel they will pay attention to.
  - **Sidebar:** a "talking pineapple" fable on a standardized test so confused eighth graders that the question was thrown out. Anticipate how audiences will read unusual ideas [p6].

⭐ (HL) **LO2-4: "What do I need to know about my audience(s)?" "Everything that's relevant to what you're writing or talking about."** [7.4-p6–p10]
- Almost everything about an audience matters for *some* message, but only a few facts matter for *this* one. There is no one-size-fits-all checklist, so use **common sense and empathy**. (HL) **Empathy** is the ability to put yourself in someone else's shoes and feel with that person. It requires not being self-centered, since "the audience is not just like you" [p6].
- **Individuals vs members of groups** [p6]:
  - Analyze known individuals by talking to them, talking to people who know them, and observing them.
  - Otherwise analyze people as members of a group, e.g., "taxpayers who must be notified that they owe more income tax", "customers living in the northeast side of the city", "employees with small children".
- ⭐ (HL) **Five kinds of information that help most** [p6]: (1) **how much the audience knows** about the topic · (2) **demographic factors** · (3) **personality** · (4) **values and beliefs** · (5) **past behavior**.
- **Table: the relevant factors depend on the message** [p7]

| Message | Audience | Relevant factors (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| Memo: company will reimburse tuition for work-related courses | all employees | attitudes to education (fun vs intimidating), time available, interest in promotion or cross-training, commitment to the company |
| Letter offering special financing on a car | college students | income, expected future income and ability to repay, interest in a new car, attitude to the dealer's cars, knowledge of interest rates, other financing options |
| Letter with a meeting agenda saying you'll bring your child | a client | how well the client knows and likes you, importance of the agenda items, feelings about children, room for a child to play |

- (HL) **Knowledge** [p7]
  - Even people in your own organization don't share all your knowledge (not all 22,000 USAA employees know insurance jargon), and readers forget what they were told.
  - ⭐ **Four tactful ways to remind readers:**
    1. Preface the statement with "As you know," "As you may know," or similar.
    2. Spell out acronyms on first use: "Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP)".
    3. Give brief definitions in the text: "the principal—the money you have invested—".
    4. Put information readers should already know in a **subordinate clause**: "Because the renovation is behind schedule, …".
- (HL) **Demographic factors** [p7–p8]
  - **Demographic characteristics** are measurable features that can be counted objectively: age, sex, race, religion, education level, income, and so on.
  - They are sometimes irrelevant (Mick Jagger, though over 60, probably doesn't subscribe to *Modern Maturity*) and sometimes vital (older workers care more about a pension-plan change).
  - Sources: surveys of customers and donors, census data, purchased data.
  - Often it's enough to spot subsets. Example: a school board seeking a tax increase must also appeal to voters with no children in school.
- (HL) **Personality** [p8]
  - Personality matters when the primary audience is **one person**. The **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator** uses four pairs of dichotomies:
    - **Extraversion–Introversion:** *where someone gets energy*. Extraverts are energized by other people; introverts by their inner world.
    - **Sensing–Intuition:** *how someone gets information*. Sensing types trust the senses and what is real and tangible; intuitive types look at the big picture and the connections between facts.
    - **Thinking–Feeling:** *how someone makes decisions*. Thinking types weigh logical consequences; feeling types weigh the impact on people.
    - **Judging–Perceiving:** *how someone orients to the external world*. Judging types like a planned, orderly life and closure; perceiving types prefer flexibility and possibilities.
  - **Uses:**
    - Team building and personal growth.
    - **Channel choice:** an introvert may prefer a *written* message (time to reflect); an extravert a phone call or face-to-face meeting.
    - **Content order:** sensing types want detailed facts in sequence; intuitive types want the overall situation first.
  - **Play to strengths.** Putting the main point up front suits **judging** types (about 75% of U.S. managers); giving logical reasons suits **thinking** types (nearly 80%).
  - People adapt: only about 50% of Americans are extraverts, yet most managers show extraverted traits, so introverts can learn the behaviors.
- (HL) **Values and beliefs** [p8–p9]
  - **Psychographic characteristics** are **qualitative**: values, beliefs, goals and lifestyles.
  - Example: two neighbouring families with the same income and two children each, one saving every penny and the other spending on cars and dinners out, need different reasons and benefits, and will raise different objections.
  - Knowing what matters to the audience lets you organize information in a way that feels natural to them and choose persuasive appeals.
  - **VALS (Values and Lifestyles)** profiles from the SRI research firm sort U.S. buyers into **8 categories** by primary motivation, resources and degree of innovation: **Innovators, Thinkers, Believers, Achievers, Strivers, Experiencers, Makers, Survivors**.
    - *Strivers:* motivated by achievement, low in resources and innovation; conspicuous consumers who try to be in style without much money.
    - *Innovators:* more disposable income, enjoy cooking and fine food, so a good target for upscale grocers.
  - A survey of 250,000 households found that online buying depends on **psychographics** (attitude to technology, from "profoundly suspicious" to "eagerly accepting"), **not** on demographics such as age or Zip code.
- (HL) **Past behavior** [p9] often predicts future behavior. Fingerhut saw that customers who moved bought furniture and decorations, so it made a "mover's catalog" and saved money by not mailing other catalogs to that group right after a move.
- ⭐ **Analyzing people in organizations** [p9–p10]. Reactions are shaped by personal preferences *and* by **discourse communities** and **organizational culture**.
  - ⭐ **Discourse community:** a group of people who share assumptions about which **channels, formats and styles** to use, which **topics** to discuss and **how**, and **what constitutes evidence**. Everyone belongs to several, which may or may not overlap.
    - **Three questions to analyze one:**
      1. Which channels, formats and styles are preferred (paper memo, e-mail, or walking down the hall), and how formal?
      2. What do people talk about, and what is *not* discussed?
      3. What kind of evidence, and how much, convinces people (personal experience? numbers and formal research?)
    - **Examples:** Procter & Gamble requires one-page recommendations, so writers send one-page memos plus attachments. A Silicon Valley company expects one PowerPoint slide with a triangle and three words around it.
  - ⭐ **Organizational (corporate) culture:** an organization's **values, attitudes and philosophies**. It shows **verbally** in the organization's myths, stories and heroes and **nonverbally** in how it allocates **space, money and power**.
    - **Four questions to analyze it:**
      1. What are its **goals**: making money, serving clients, advancing knowledge, contributing to the community?
      2. What does it **value**: diversity or homogeneity, independence or team play, creativity or following orders?
      3. **How do people get ahead**: seniority, education, being liked, technical discoveries, serving customers? Are rewards for a few or for everyone?
      4. **How formal** are behaviour, language and dress?
    - **Evidence it matters:**
      - Companies in the same field can differ: Andersen Consulting (65,000 people) valued compensation, bonuses, prestige and rewards; the 35-person Creative Good valued communication, relationships, creativity and growth.
      - New hires who "fit" the culture stay longer, are more productive and are more satisfied.
      - Organizations have subcultures (e.g., manufacturing vs marketing).
    - **How to learn a culture:** observe; listen to the stories people tell (Nike: co-founder Bowerman pouring rubber into the family waffle iron teaches *innovation*; Prefontaine's push for better equipment teaches *helping athletes*); read the company website's description of its culture (usually in the employment section). Photo captions [p13] contrast Siebel Systems (professional, competitive, suits, bottom 5% fired yearly) with Sun Microsystems (informal, flexible, work from home).

⭐ **LO2-5: "Now that I have my analysis, what do I do with it?" "Use it to plan strategy, organization, style, document design, and visuals."** [7.4-p11–p12] (Only the heading is highlighted, (HL).)
- If you know the audience well and use words well, most adaptation happens unconsciously. **If you don't know the audience, or the message is very important, analyze formally and revise the draft with the analysis in mind.**
- **Strategy:**
  - make the action as easy as possible;
  - **protect the reader's ego**;
  - decide how to balance logic and emotion, which details to use, and whether to **hard-sell or soft-sell**, based on the audience, organizational culture and discourse community;
  - choose appeals and reader benefits that work for *this* audience.
- **Organization:**
  - Usually get to the point right away, "because most managers are intuitive types". **Two exceptions:** (a) when you must persuade a reluctant reader; (b) when you have bad news and want to let the reader down gradually.
  - Make the pattern clear with headings and overviews (in oral presentations, overviews and signposts).
  - *Note:* p8 links "main point up front" to *judging* types, while p11 says *intuitive*. The reading says both.
- **Style:**
  - easy-to-understand words, a mix of sentence lengths, paragraphs with topic sentences;
  - avoid words that sound **defensive or arrogant**;
  - avoid **hot-button "red-flag" words** that trigger an immediate negative reaction (the reading lists: criminal, un-American, crazy, fundamentalist, liberal);
  - use the **language(s) the audience knows best** (Quebec: English and French; U.S. Southwest: English and Spanish);
  - use **conversational, not "academic", language**.
- **Document design:** lists, headings and a mix of paragraph lengths create white space. Format, footnotes and visuals may be dictated by the organizational culture or discourse community.
- **Photos and visuals:**
  - use bias-free photographs and clip art;
  - visuals change formality (cartoons vs photos of "high art");
  - some cultures (e.g., French, Japanese) use evocative photos only loosely related to the text, while most U.S. audiences expect photos clearly tied to the text, often with a caption;
  - for web documents, consider music and download time.
- **Sidebar:** Carly Fiorina's bizarre "demon sheep" attack ad got attention, but "getting attention" isn't the same as "swaying the audience" [p11].

⭐ **LO2-6: "What if my audiences have different needs?" "Focus on gatekeepers and decision makers."** [7.4-p12]
- If you can't meet everyone's needs, **meet the needs of gatekeepers and primary audiences first**.
- **Content and choice of details:** give an **overview or executive summary** for readers who want only the main points; put enough detail in the body for the primary audience *and anyone who could veto the proposal*; move extra detail (statistics, earlier reports) to **appendices**.
- **Organization:** use **headings and a table of contents** so readers can jump to what interests them; organize around the **primary audience's attitude** to the message.
- **Level of formality:**
  - **avoid personal pronouns**, because "you" stops meaning anyone specific when several audiences read the document;
  - internal *and* external readers → slightly more formal than an internal document;
  - **international audiences → more formal**.
- **Technical terms and theory:** in the body, assume the primary audience's level of knowledge; put background and theory under **separate headings** that others can read or skip; if the primary audience knows more than the others, add a **glossary** and say early on that it exists.
- **Sidebar:** role-play with a friend or colleague first, to test ideas and prepare for objections (Kotter) [p12].

⭐ **LO2-7: "How do I reach my audience(s)?" "Important messages may require multiple channels."** [7.4-p13–p14]
- **Channels vary in** [p13]: **speed · accuracy of transmission · cost · number of messages carried · number of people reached · efficiency · ability to promote goodwill.**
- **A written message makes it easier to** [p14]:
  - present many specific details of a law, policy or procedure;
  - present extensive or complex financial data;
  - **minimize undesirable emotions.**
  - Paper is more formal than e-mail. E-mail suits routine messages to people you already know; **paper is usually better for someone you're writing to for the first time**.
- **An oral message makes it easier to** [p14]:
  - answer questions, resolve conflicts and **build consensus**;
  - use emotion to help persuade;
  - get **immediate** action or response;
  - focus the audience's attention on specific points;
  - **modify a proposal** that isn't acceptable in its original form.
  - Scheduled meetings and presentations are more formal than phone calls or stopping someone in the hall.
- **Important messages call for more formal channels,** whether oral or written [p14].
- ⭐ **In both oral and written messages** [p14]:
  1. adapt the message to the specific audience;
  2. show audience members how they **benefit**;
  3. overcome any **objections**;
  4. use **you-attitude and positive emphasis**;
  5. use visuals to clarify or emphasize;
  6. **specify exactly what the audience should do.**
  - 🔎 Look up in Track B: how to write "you-attitude" and "positive emphasis" sentences. The reading points to Modules 6–7, which are not in the class materials; 7.1 gives only the definition of the "you" attitude.
- **Different discourse communities prefer different channels.** When a university updated its benefits manual, computer scientists and librarians wanted it online, faculty wanted paper, and maintenance workers and carpenters wanted answers by voice mail [p14].
- **Use multiple channels** for big audiences (few channels reach everyone) and for very important messages. Example: talk to key players about a document before the meeting where it will be discussed [p14].
- **Sidebar:** a U.S. Army unit's "red line" call left a soldier's family believing he had been killed. He was alive; another soldier had died ("someone else's tragedy", as the father put it). The unit is reconsidering how it notifies families and may open such calls with "Your son or daughter is fine" when that is true. Message order and channel matter [p14].

**"Polishing Your Prose": comma splices** [7.4-p18–p19]
- A **comma splice** joins two sentences with only a comma, e.g., "We shipped the order on Tuesday, it arrived on Wednesday." Comma splices are almost always inappropriate in business communication; poetry, fiction and some ads use them deliberately.
- **Four fixes:**
  1. use a **semicolon** if the ideas are closely related;
  2. add a **coordinating conjunction** (and, or, but) after the comma;
  3. make **two separate sentences**;
  4. **subordinate** one clause ("Because we shipped the order on Tuesday, it arrived on Wednesday.").

## 3. Key terms glossary (A–Z, one line each)

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)**: computing power that replicates aspects of human intelligence in three stages: collect data → analyze it to decide → apply the result [7.1-p21]
- **Audience-centered approach / "you" attitude**: understanding and respecting the audience and making the message meaningful to them; about "you" (the receiver), not "me" (the sender) [7.1-p8, p27]
- **Augmented intelligence (cognitive automation, hybrid intelligence)**: hybrid set-ups where computers help humans decide, or humans help computers [7.1-p20–p21]
- **Bait-and-switch**: advertising a low-priced product to lure customers, then pushing a pricier one; illegal under advertising law [7.1-p24]
- **Build goodwill**: create the image of you and your organization that makes people want to do business with you [7.3-p8]
- **Channel (communication channel)**: the system that delivers a message (e.g., email, Twitter, memo, meeting, phone) [7.1-p9, p27; 7.4-p5]
- **Channel overload**: the channel can't handle all the messages being sent [7.4-p6]
- **Code of ethics**: a written document of a company's ethics policies and the expectations for all employees [7.1-p23, p27]
- **Comma splice**: two sentences joined with only a comma [7.4-p18]
- **Communication**: the process of transferring information, meaning and understanding between senders and receivers through one or more media [7.1-p2, p27]
- **Communication barriers**: noise and distractions, competing messages, filters, channel breakdowns [7.1-p10, p27]
- **Computer vision**: AI that identifies the content of photos, video and live camera images [7.1-p17]
- **Contract**: a legally binding promise in which one party makes a specified offer and the other accepts [7.1-p24, p27]
- **Copyright**: legal protection for the creators of creative content (writing, design, code, recordings) [7.1-p23, p27]
- **Critical thinking**: evaluating evidence completely and objectively to reach logical conclusions and sound recommendations [7.1-p4, p27]
- **Data literacy**: the ability to access, assess, interpret, manipulate, summarize and communicate data [7.1-p26]
- **Decoding**: extracting the idea or meaning from the symbols of a message [7.1-p9; 7.4-p5]
- **Deep fakes**: convincingly faked audio and video [7.1-p23]
- **Deep learning**: machine learning that uses multiple layers of neural networks [7.1-p17, p21]
- **Defamation**: intentional public communication of false statements that damage character or reputation; **libel** = written, **slander** = spoken [7.1-p25]
- **Demographic characteristics**: measurable, objectively countable features (age, sex, race, religion, education, income) [7.4-p7]
- **Digital information fluency**: recognizing information needs, finding and evaluating reliable sources, organizing information into cohesive messages, using it ethically [7.1-p4]
- **Discourse community**: a group sharing assumptions about which channels, formats and styles to use, which topics to discuss and how, and what counts as evidence [7.4-p10]
- **Emotional intelligence**: reading other people's emotions accurately and managing your own productively [7.1-p8]
- **Empathy**: putting yourself in someone else's shoes and feeling with that person [7.4-p6]
- **Encoding**: putting an idea into a message in words, images or nonverbal symbols [7.1-p9; 7.4-p5]
- **Ethical communication**: includes all the information the audience needs for an informed decision or stance and is not deceptive in any way [7.1-p22]
- **Ethical dilemma**: a choice among alternatives that aren't clear-cut (all ethically valid to some degree, or all in a gray area) [7.1-p23]
- **Ethical lapse**: a clearly unethical choice [7.1-p23]
- **Ethics**: the accepted principles of right and wrong that govern behaviour and decision-making in a society [7.1-p22]
- **External audiences**: people outside the organization: customers, suppliers, unions, stockholders, potential employees, government agencies, the press, the public [7.3-p7]
- **Fair use**: limited room to use others' work without violating copyright; no precise limits, and only a defense after you are sued [7.1-p23]
- **Feedback**: the receiver's response sent back to the sender; verbal or nonverbal, immediate or delayed [7.1-p9; 7.4-p6]

- **Form letter**: a prewritten fill-in-the-blank letter for standard, routine situations [7.3-p5]
- **Formal communication network**: communication flowing along the lines of command, in four directions (downward, upward, horizontal, diagonal) [7.1-p6–p8, p27]

- **Grapevine**: an informal source of company information [7.3-p8]
- **Informal communication network**: all communication outside the formal network [7.1-p8, p27]
- **Information overload**: receiving more information than you can effectively process [7.1-p15; 7.4-p6]
- **Information technology paradox**: information tools can waste as much time as they save [7.1-p15]
- **Initial audience**: receives the message first and routes it to the others; may have assigned it [7.4-p3]
- **Intellectual property (IP)**: copyrighted materials plus industrial property such as patents, product designs and logos [7.1-p25, p27]
- **Intelligent communication technology (ICT)**: systems that use AI to enhance the communication experience [7.1-p17, p27]
- **Internal audiences**: people in the same organization: superiors, peers, subordinates [7.3-p7]
- **Interpersonal unity**: getting people to want to work together toward organizational goals (Mintzberg's third managerial job) [7.3-p6]
- **Machine learning**: a system's general ability to teach itself to improve at its tasks [7.1-p17, p21]
- **Medium (communication medium)**: the form a message takes: oral, written or visual [7.1-p9, p27]
- **Message**: the "container" for an idea sent from sender to receiver [7.1-p9, p27]
- **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator**: a personality instrument with four dichotomies: E–I, S–N, T–F, J–P [7.4-p8]
- **Native advertising (sponsored content)**: advertising designed to look like news stories, articles or social posts [7.1-p22]
- **Natural language processing (NLP)**: computer ability to understand, analyze, respond to and generate human language; voice systems also need speech recognition [7.1-p17, p21]
- **Noise**: anything that interferes with communication, either **physical** (static, a lawn mower, bad handwriting) or **psychological** (dislike, preoccupation, a mind already made up) [7.4-p6, p14]
- **Nonverbal communication**: communication without words (pictures, logos, gestures, who sits where, how long a visitor waits) [7.3-p4]
- **Organizational (corporate) culture**: an organization's values, attitudes and philosophies, shown in its myths, stories and heroes and in how it allocates space, money and power [7.4-p10]
- **PAIBOC**: Purposes, Audience, Information, Benefits, Objections, Context, the questions to answer before writing [7.3-p12]
- **Plagiarism**: presenting someone else's words or creative work as your own [7.1-p23, p27]
- **Primary audience**: decides whether to accept your recommendations, or acts on the message [7.4-p2]
- **Professionalism**: performing at a high level and conducting oneself with purpose and pride [7.1-p4, p27]
- **Psychographic characteristics**: qualitative traits: values, beliefs, goals, lifestyles [7.4-p8]
- **Request vs persuade**: *request* means the action is easy or routine; *persuade* means you must motivate and convince [7.3-p8]
- **Robotic process automation (RPA)**: automating the routine, high-volume "paperwork" side of knowledge work [7.1-p20]
- **Secondary audience**: comments on the message, or implements it after approval [7.4-p2]
- **Selective attention**: focusing on some incoming stimuli or messages and ignoring others [7.1-p11, p27]
- **Sentence fragment**: a word group missing a subject or verb, or a subordinate clause standing alone [7.3-p18]
- **Social communication model**: an interactive, conversational approach, open to all, in which audiences take a full part [7.1-p13, p27]
- **Social media**: digital platforms that let stakeholders share, revise, respond to or contribute content [7.1-p15, p27]
- **Stakeholders**: persons or organizations significantly affected by a company's decisions and operations (employees, customers, investors, creditors, suppliers, communities) [7.1-p3, p27]
- **Stealth marketing**: promoting companies or products without making clear that marketing is happening [7.1-p22]
- **Strong AI vs weak (narrow) AI**: strong AI is general, humanlike intelligence (also called AGI); weak AI targets one specific problem, and all AI with real business impact today is weak AI [7.1-p17, p21]
- **Transparency**: openness that gives all participants the information they need to process messages accurately [7.1-p22, p27]
- **VALS**: SRI's Values and Lifestyles profiles, which sort U.S. buyers into 8 categories by motivation, resources and innovation [7.4-p9]
- **Verbal communication**: communication that uses words (conversations, meetings, texts and e-mail, letters and memos, reports) [7.3-p4]
- **Watchdog audience**: has no power to stop the message but holds political, social or economic power, watches the exchange and may act later [7.4-p3]

## 4. Frameworks & checklists to memorize (as given in the readings)

### 4.1 The communication process: two models side by side

| 7.1 conventional model, 8 steps [7.1-p8–p9] | 7.4 two-person model with feedback [7.4-p5–p6] |
|---|---|
| 1. Sender has an idea | Sender **perceives** a stimulus → **interprets** it |
| 2. Sender **encodes** the idea as a message | **chooses/selects** what to send → **encodes** it (including nonverbal symbols: gestures, clothing, pictures) |
| 3. Sender produces it in a **medium** (oral, written, visual) | (no separate step) |
| 4. Sender **transmits** it through a **channel** | **transmits** it through a **channel** |
| 5. Audience **receives** it | Receiver **perceives** it |
| 6. Audience **decodes** it | **decodes** it → **interprets** it |
| 7. Audience **responds** | **chooses a response** → **encodes** it |
| 8. Audience may give **feedback** | sends **feedback** (direct/immediate or indirect/delayed; verbal or nonverbal) |
| **Barriers:** noise and distractions · competing messages · filters · channel breakdowns [7.1-p10] | **Noise at every step**, physical or psychological · **channel overload** · **information overload** · different **frames of reference** [7.4-p6] |

- My own memory hook (not from the readings) for the 8 steps: **I-E-M-C / R-D-R-F** = Idea, Encode, Medium, Channel / Receive, Decode, Respond, Feedback.
- Both readings stress that a model is a **simplification**: one cycle, with noise and barriers at every point [7.1-p9; 7.4-p5].

### 4.2 Inside the audience's mind [7.1-p11–p13]
- **Receiving takes three events:** **Sense → Select → Perceive.** Selective attention filters out the rest.
- **Five principles for getting noticed:**
  1. Consider audience **expectations** (media and channels).
  2. Make it **user-friendly** (findable, navigable, readable).
  3. Emphasize **familiarity**.
  4. Practice **empathy** (the "you" attitude).
  5. Design for **compatibility** (devices).
- **Decoding is shaped by:** culture · experience · learning and thinking styles · ego · hopes · fears · beliefs · moods · self-defense mechanisms · language and usage · **shared experience** (Figure 1.6: more shared experience → more similar meanings → more understanding).
- **The audience responds only if it can:** **Remember** it · is **Able** to act · is **Motivated** to act.

| Intrusion | Permission |

### 4.4 "What makes a message effective?": the two lists

| 7.1: five characteristics [7.1-p4] | 7.3: five criteria [7.3-p9] |
|---|---|
| **Practical:** useful information for solving problems or acting | **Clear:** the reader gets the intended meaning without guessing |
| **Factual:** concrete, specific, accurate; evidence for opinions | **Complete:** all the reader's questions answered; enough to act |
| **Concise:** efficient, respects people's time | **Correct:** accurate facts; no punctuation, spelling or grammar errors |
| **Clear:** spells out expectations and responsibilities | **Saves the reader's time:** style, organization, visual impact |
| **Persuasive:** compelling arguments; shows readers how they benefit | **Builds goodwill:** positive image; treats the reader as a person, not a number |

- **Where they overlap:** *clear* is on both lists; *concise* ≈ *saves the reader's time*; *factual* ≈ *correct + complete*; *persuasive* ≈ reader benefits (the B in PAIBOC).
- **7.3's caveat:** effectiveness depends on the **writer, audience, purposes and situation**, and no single set of words works everywhere [7.3-p9–p10].
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: the course syllabus lists "four C's" (clear, concise, coherent, complete). These readings don't present that list as such.

### 4.5 Audience-analysis toolkit
- **The "you" attitude / audience-centered approach** [7.1-p8]: write about the receiver's needs, not the sender's. Learn their **beliefs, education, status, communication style, personal and professional concerns**; if you can't, imagine yourself in their position.
- **Five audiences** [7.4-p2–p3], listed in the order a message typically travels: **Initial → Gatekeeper → Primary → Secondary**, with the **Watchdog** looking on.
  - My own memory hook (not from the readings): "**I Get Past Some Watchdogs**".
  - Remember that one person can play two roles.
- ⭐ **PAIBOC** [7.3-p12; 7.4-p4–p5], to answer before writing:
  - **P**: What are your **purposes** in writing or speaking?
  - **A**: Who is (are) your **audience(s)**? How do they differ? Which characteristics are relevant to this message?
  - **I**: What **information** must the message include?
  - **B**: What reasons or reader **benefits** support your position? (Identify at least 5; they needn't be money.)
  - **O**: What **objections** will readers have? Which negatives must you deemphasize or overcome?
  - **C**: How will the **context** affect the response? (Your relationship to the reader, morale, the economy, time of year, special circumstances.)
  - Five of the six (A, I, B, O, C) are about the audience; **P** comes from you and your organization.
- **Five kinds of audience information** [7.4-p6]: knowledge · demographics · personality · values and beliefs (psychographics) · past behavior. In organizations, add **discourse community** and **organizational culture** [7.4-p9–p10]. Always use **common sense + empathy**.
- **Four tactful reminders** [7.4-p7]: "As you know…" · spell out the acronym the first time · a brief in-text definition · put known information in a subordinate clause.
- **Myers-Briggs** [7.4-p8]: E–I (*energy*) · S–N (*information*) · T–F (*decisions*) · J–P (*orientation to the world*). Introverts → written messages; extraverts → calls and meetings; sensing types → detailed facts in sequence; intuitive types → the big picture first. About 75% of U.S. managers are judging types and about 80% thinking types.
- **Discourse community, three questions** [7.4-p10]: preferred channels, formats, styles and formality? · what is (and isn't) discussed? · what evidence convinces, and how much?
- **Organizational culture, four questions** [7.4-p10]: goals? · values? · how do people get ahead? · how formal are behaviour, language and dress? Culture shows verbally (myths, stories, heroes) and nonverbally (space, money, power).
- **Finding out what "counts"** [7.4-p2]: **ask** the boss · **listen** to success and failure stories · **observe** who is praised and promoted.

### 4.6 Adaptation checklist (LO2-5 and LO2-6) [7.4-p11–p12]
- **Strategy:** make the action easy · protect the reader's ego · balance logic and emotion; choose details; hard-sell or soft-sell · choose appeals and benefits that fit this audience.
- **Organization:** get to the point at once, **except** (a) a reluctant reader you must persuade, (b) bad news. Make the pattern visible with headings, overviews and signposts.
- **Style:** easy words · mixed sentence lengths · topic sentences · no defensive or arrogant words · no red-flag words · the audience's best language · conversational, not academic.
- **Document design:** lists, headings, mixed paragraph lengths → white space. Follow the culture's conventions.
- **Visuals:** bias-free · they set the formality · expectations differ by culture (evocative vs captioned and directly related) · on the web, watch music and download time.
- **Multiple audiences:** serve the **gatekeeper and primary audience first**.
  - Executive summary up front, detail in the body, extras in appendices.
  - Headings and a table of contents; organize around the primary audience's attitude.
  - No personal pronouns; more formal for mixed internal/external or international readers.
  - Assume the primary audience's level of knowledge; put background under separate headings; add a glossary, flagged early.

### 4.7 Channel-choice checklist [7.3-p10; 7.4-p13–p14; 7.1-p11, p15]
- **Channels vary in seven ways:** speed · accuracy of transmission · cost · number of messages carried · number of people reached · efficiency · ability to promote goodwill.

| Use **written** when you need to… | Use **oral** when you need to… |
|---|---|
| present many specific details of a law, policy or procedure | answer questions, resolve conflicts, build consensus |
| present extensive or complex financial data | use emotion to help persuade |
| minimize undesirable emotions | get immediate action or response |
| create a record, be visible, make things convenient for the reader [7.3-p5] | focus attention on specific points; modify a proposal on the spot; make group decisions [7.3-p10] |

- **Formality ladder:** paper > e-mail; a scheduled meeting or presentation > a phone call or hallway chat. **Important message → more formal channel.** Paper is better for a **first-time** contact; e-mail suits routine messages to people you know [7.4-p14].
- **Multiple channels** for big audiences and very important messages, e.g., brief key players before the meeting [7.4-p14].
- **Channel expectations** of the audience or discourse community come first [7.1-p11; 7.4-p14].
- If a text exchange is stuck in confusion or negativity, **reconnect** by phone or in person [7.1-p15].
- **Whatever the channel, include these six things:** adapt to the audience · show benefits · overcome objections · use the you-attitude and positive emphasis · use visuals · **say exactly what the audience should do** [7.4-p14].

### 4.8 Tone and plain-language guidelines (compiled; each is from the readings)
- Business style is **friendly, not formal**: short words and a mix of sentence and paragraph lengths [7.3-p6]. Use **easy-to-understand words**, topic sentences, and **conversational, not "academic"** language [7.4-p11].
- Use **concrete language and specific detail**, not vague impressions [7.1-p4]. Remove ambiguity: "as soon as possible" means different things to different people [7.1-p12].
- No **accusatory** tone. The greeting should be **friendly but not too casual**, the close **warm**, and include a full **signature** (Figure 1.2) [7.1-p5].
- Avoid **defensive or arrogant** words and **red-flag** words; **protect the reader's ego** [7.4-p11]. Remind readers tactfully ("As you know…") [7.4-p4, p7].
- **Spell out acronyms** and give **brief definitions** [7.4-p7]. Use the language(s) the audience knows best [7.4-p11].
- Treat the reader **as a person, not a number** [7.3-p9]. Use empathy, not manipulation [7.4-p1].
- **Main point first** (except with reluctant readers or bad news) [7.3-p6; 7.4-p11]. Make documents **skimmable** with headings, lists and white space [7.3-p6; 7.4-p12].
- With several audiences, **avoid "you"** and raise the formality for mixed or international readers [7.4-p12].
- Avoid **sentence fragments** and **comma splices** in business documents [7.3-p18; 7.4-p18].
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: any formal "plain language" standard or guideline set, and the sentence-level rules for *you-attitude* and *positive emphasis*. These readings refer to them but don't teach them.

### 4.9 Digital and social-media guidelines (compiled)
- **Five rules for using technology** [7.1-p14–p15]:
  1. keep technology in perspective;
  2. guard against information overload (both as receiver and as sender);
  3. use your tools wisely (the IT paradox);
  4. use your tools efficiently;
  5. reconnect with people.
- **Represent your employer** responsibly on social media, with standard grammar and spelling [7.1-p6].
- **Social model mindset:** start conversations rather than broadcast, and expect your message to be reshaped and amplified. You'll have less control and more complexity [7.1-p13–p14].
- **Mobile:** small screens raise misreading and drop-off, so design for compatibility [7.1-p11, p16].
- **Transparency:** label sponsored content, don't do stealth marketing, and influencers must disclose company relationships [7.1-p22, p25]. Never present others' content as your own; "fair use" is not a label you can attach [7.1-p23].
- **Think about every audience before you post** [7.4-p1]. Sidebar cases: a politician's aide resigned after offensive Facebook remarks became public; a UK employee lost her job after posting "OMG I HATE MY JOB!" when her boss was her Facebook friend [7.4-p8].
- **KLM as a model** [7.1-p1–p2]: fast responses (aiming for 30 minutes), multilingual service, personal data only by **private DM**, 24/7 coverage, people combined with AI.

### 4.11 Ethics and law checklist [7.1-p22–p25]

### 4.13 Writing mechanics [7.3-p18; 7.4-p18]

### T1. Diagnose and rewrite a harsh message [7.1-p28–p29, Message for Analysis 1-16]
**Situation (summarized):**
- A manager's blog post opens "It has come to my attention that many of you are lying on your time cards".
- It calls this "cheating" and announces that from next Monday there will be cameras at all entrances and key-card logging of entry and exit times.
- It warns that anyone late more than three times "will have to answer to me".
- It adds "I don't care if you had to take a nap or if you girls had to shop".
- It ends with "slackers who are cheaters" and "a few bad apples".

**Task:** (a) Is it effective? Explain using this week's concepts. (b) Rewrite it.

**Model answer (a): ineffective.**

| Problem | Concept it violates |
|---|---|
| Accusatory, insulting tone ("lying", "cheating", "slackers") puts everyone on the defensive | the Figure 1.2 lesson on tone [7.1-p5]; builds goodwill [7.3-p9]; the "you" attitude [7.1-p8] |
| "you girls had to shop" is a disrespectful, sexist remark | professionalism: *be respectful* [7.1-p6]; avoid hot-button and arrogant words [7.4-p11] |
| "Many of you" vs "a few bad apples": punishes everyone for a few | clear [7.3-p9]; factual: no data, only a vague "many" [7.1-p4] |
| Unclear rules: what counts as "late"? three times in what period? what does "answer to me" mean? | clarify expectations and responsibilities [7.1-p4]; complete [7.3-p9] |
| No reason or benefit for readers; ignores obvious objections (cameras, fairness) | PAIBOC **B** and **O** [7.3-p12] |
| A disciplinary issue broadcast on a blog that other audiences may read | channel choice: oral is better for resolving conflict, written for policy details and minimizing emotions [7.4-p14]; think of *all* audiences [7.4-p1] |
| No closing, no invitation to ask questions, no name or contact | Figure 1.2 (g)–(h) [7.1-p5] |
| Ignores how the message will affect the audience | ethics question 3 [7.1-p24] |

**Model answer (b): rewrite** (neutral, specific and complete, with a reason and a way to ask questions):
> **Subject: Automatic entry/exit logging starts Monday, [date]**
> Hello everyone,
> Accurate time cards keep payroll fair for all of us. Recently, some time cards haven't matched actual start and lunch-return times, so we're making record-keeping automatic.
> Starting Monday, [date]:
> • Your key card will log your entry and exit times at every entrance.
> • Cameras will be installed at all building entrances.
> • Please continue to record your actual start and return times.
> If you're running late, let your supervisor know before your shift starts. If late arrivals become a pattern (more than three in [period]), I'll meet with you privately to find a solution.
> Questions about the new system? Stop by my office any afternoon this week or reply to this post.
> Thank you,
> [Name], [Title], [phone]

*Why it works:*
- The subject line is informative and includes the date (Fig. 1.2 a/e).
- It gives a reason and a shared benefit (persuasive).
- It states facts instead of insults (factual).
- It says who does what, and by when (clear expectations).
- Individual cases are moved to private conversations (oral channel for conflict).
- It invites questions and ends with a signature (Fig. 1.2 f–h).

### T2. Score four replies to an angry customer [7.3-p15–p16, Exercise 1.11]
**Situation (summarized):** Storms knocked out a utility's power lines, leaving some customers without electricity for over a week (spoiled food, no air conditioning in 90°F+ heat). Harper Henry writes in angrily: she is a long-time customer and local business leader, and her husband is a county commissioner. Her power was out 4 days; nothing seems to have been done to prepare; there is no plan to bury overhead lines; "Your company puts making money ahead of customer service."

**Task:** rate each reply on the five criteria (clear, complete, correct, saves time, builds goodwill), say which is best, and improve it.

**Model answer:**

| Reply | What it does | Main failures or strengths | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1, "Dear Sir or Madam" | two-sentence generic brush-off ("looking into the problem") | *unclear* (looking into what?), *incomplete* (ignores her points), *incorrect* (typo "You opinion"), impersonal, so *no goodwill*; she'll have to write again | weak |
| 2, "Dear Harper" | jokey admission ("dropped the ball"); weather excuse ("only God Himself controls the weather"); tells her to trim her trees; P.S. jokes about her name | too familiar, flippant, blames the customer, mocks her: **destroys goodwill**; no plan, so *incomplete* | worst |
| 3, "Dear Mrs. Henry" | long, defensive lecture on union contracts, regulations and homeowners' trees ("Unions are killing this business"); apology buried; "Think about that the next time you flip a switch" | wastes her time, offers no action (*incomplete*), condescending and blaming (*no goodwill*) | poor |
| 4, "Dear Ms. Henry" | thanks her; gives facts (storm severity, 1,500+ extra workers); admits "clearly we could have done better"; working with municipalities; names burying lines as an option | clear, correct, concise, respectful; picks up **her** suggestion = goodwill | **best** |

**How to improve reply 4:**
- Make it more *complete*: say what specific steps will be taken, by when, and how she will hear about progress; name a contact person.
- Answer her charge that "nothing" was done to prepare.

### T3. Rank five rumor-control posts [7.3-p16–p17, Exercise 1.12]
**Situation (summarized):** Acme (475 employees) is acquiring Best Products (132 employees). Layoff rumors, mostly false, are spreading on public job-site message boards. HR manager Ed Zeplin drafts five possible posts.

**Model answer (best to worst; positions 1 and 2 are arguable):**
1. **#5 "The True Story about Lay-Offs"**: best for *goodwill*.
   - Empathetic opening (rumors are human nature in uncertain times) and a clear reassurance (no plans to lay anyone off).
   - Shows the CEOs and HR of both companies agree.
   - Promises updates, points to official channels (newsletter, intranet), invites direct contact, signed with full name and title.
   - Weakness: no hard numbers.
2. **#3 "Don't Believe the Rumors"**: best on *facts*.
   - Gives the staffing numbers, admits "some redeployments", explains the basis of the plan, and links to the intranet.
   - **Check the math:** 475 + 132 = 607, and a plan for 625 means hiring 18, not the "20" stated, so it fails *correct*.
   - Also padded (everyone told to read the mission statement) and impersonal (an e-mail address as author).
3. **#1 "It Will Be Great!"**: upbeat but vague.
   - Reveals the CEO told HR two months ago, which looks like secrecy.
   - Centres on HR's own pressure ("me", not "you").
   - "You really should wait" sounds patronizing; no facts.
4. **#2 "HR Staffing" by "HR Boss"**: a one-line denial from an anonymous-sounding author; "Just ask anyone in HR" isn't evidence. Fails *complete* and has low credibility.
5. **#4 "Layoff Rumors Do Acme a Disservice"**: worst.
   - Insults readers ("negative, whining lies", "rumor-mongering").
   - Arrogant ("I'm the one who approves the staffing plan, so I should know").
   - Lectures readers on hard work, which **destroys goodwill** even though the news itself (no layoffs) is good.
- **Ideal post:** #5's tone + #3's facts, with correct numbers. **Audience reminder:** a public board reaches employees of both firms, job seekers and maybe the press, so it has multiple audiences, some of them watchdogs [7.4-p2–p3].

### T4. Identify the five audiences [7.4-p15–p16, Exercise 2.8]
**Situation 3 (summarized):**
- The mayor asks Paul to draft a proposal for the city council: to cut costs, employees working more than 40 hours a week would take compensatory time off instead of overtime pay (police and fire excepted). The idea came from a blue-ribbon panel.
- Before voting, the council will hear from citizens, other cities' mayors' offices, union representatives, department heads, and the panel plus good-government lobbying groups.
- Council members face re-election in six months.

**Model answer:**

| Audience | Who | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial** | the mayor | assigns the proposal and receives it first |
| **Gatekeeper** | the mayor | decides whether it goes on to the council (one person in two roles, as the reading notes) |
| **Primary** | the city council | votes on the change |
| **Secondary** | department heads; union representatives; other cities' mayors' offices; citizens who read and comment | they comment on the proposal, or would implement it (department heads must schedule comp time) |
| **Watchdog** | the blue-ribbon panel and good-government lobbying groups; voters (re-election in six months); unions also hold political and economic power | power without a vote on this decision; they watch the outcome and may act later |

*Use it:*
- Write for the council's level of knowledge.
- Put implementation detail for department heads in a separate section or appendix [7.4-p12].
- Anticipate union and employee objections (PAIBOC O).
- Keep the tone neutral, because watchdogs are reading.

*Quick takes on the other two situations (labels can be argued):*
- **Andrea's dinner seminar:** primary = the couples on her mailing list (they decide whether to attend); gatekeepers = the licensing and permit authorities (no permit, no seminar); secondary = the hotel or business centre and the caterer (they implement); prize-donating vendors = secondary or watchdog (they expect recognition, and their goodwill matters for the future).
- **Carmale's franchise:** primary = the franchise corporation that decides on her application; the lender acts like a gatekeeper (no loan, no franchise), or is the primary audience for a separate loan request.

### T5. Plan a persuasive memo with PAIBOC [7.4-p18, Exercise 2.16]
**Situation (summarized):**
- The company has a generous tuition-reimbursement programme, but employees must take classes outside business hours.
- The classes they need are rarely offered on evenings or weekends, so people wait months.
- You will write to upper management proposing **flex-time**: full-time employees in school may reschedule up to 5 hours a week to attend classes, making the hours up in the morning, evening or on weekends, and only when meetings and duties don't conflict.

**Model answer:**
- **P (purposes):** persuade upper management to approve flex-time (perhaps as a trial); inform them of the scheduling problem; build goodwill by showing you've considered the company's needs, not just employees'.
- **A (audiences):** primary = upper management. Gatekeeper = your supervisor and/or HR, who may need to forward or endorse it. Secondary = department heads (they would approve and track schedules), HR (runs the programme), and co-workers (they cover meetings). Relevant traits: cost-consciousness, worries about coverage and fairness, the culture's attitude to "being at your desk".
- **I (information):** the problem (classes rarely offered after hours; long waits); the limits of the proposal (at most 5 hours a week, hours made up, only when there's no conflict); who approves and tracks schedules; the expected cost or efficiency effect; how it serves the purpose of the existing tuition programme.
- **B (benefits; aim for at least 5, per 7.3-p12):**
  - the company gets the value of its tuition spending sooner, because courses get finished;
  - new skills reach the job sooner;
  - no paid hours are lost, because hours are made up;
  - employees see the organization as supportive (goodwill);
  - employees no longer lose months waiting for a seat;
  - supervisors keep control through approval.
- **O (objections and responses):**
  - cost or lost efficiency → hours are made up, and only when there's no conflict;
  - meeting coverage → supervisor approval each term;
  - fairness to non-students → it ties to an existing benefit open to everyone;
  - tracking burden → a simple sign-off sheet;
  - "they could take distance-learning courses" → the course they need may not be offered online;
  - cultural resistance → propose a trial period.
- **C (context):** the company's competitive position (growing or shrinking), the budget climate, the time of year (before the next registration deadline), the culture's openness to flexibility, your relationship with management.
- **Organization and channel:** management may be reluctant, which is one of the two exceptions to "main point first" [7.4-p11]. So lead with the shared problem and benefits, then make the request. Channel: a written memo, because it covers policy details [7.4-p14], plus talking to key players before it is discussed (multiple channels for an important message) [7.4-p14].

### T7. Ethical or unethical? Dilemma or lapse? [7.1-p30, Exercises 1-29 and 1-30]
**Model answers (using the five ethics questions [7.1-p23–p24]):**
- **(a) Keeping quiet about a possible environmental hazard at your plant.** Unethical, because it withholds information people need (Q2) and ignores who could be harmed (Q3). It can also become a legal issue: employers must inform employees about workplace hazards [7.1-p25]. The ethical step is to report it through proper channels (supervisor, ethics hotline). Knowingly staying silent = **lapse**.
- **(b) Hyping workgroup messaging because managers "never seem to understand" technology.** This is distorting information. It is unethical even if the goal seems good, because ethical communication is "not deceptive in any way". Q5 points to a better approach: honest evidence, a demo, a pilot. **Lapse.**
- **(c) Warning a close friend that she must pay more attention to her work or management will fire her.** A **dilemma**: loyalty and honesty toward a friend vs confidentiality owed to the employer (if you heard it in confidence). Honest, respectful advice based on *your own* observations is ethical (Q1); leaking confidential management plans may not be.
- **(d) Recommending equipment you don't need, to use up the budget so it isn't cut next year.** Unethical: it misrepresents need (distortion) and wastes company money. It fails Q1, and Q5 points to an honest alternative (argue openly for next year's real needs). **Lapse.**
- **1-29: a colleague borrowed equipment without permission, damaged it, returned it, and told only you.**
  - The colleague committed **lapses**: unauthorized use, then concealing the damage (withholding). That could hurt others' work, quality or safety (operations) if the uncalibrated equipment is used.
  - You face a **dilemma**: loyalty to the colleague vs duty to the company and co-workers. Ethical path: urge the colleague to report it now; if they won't, report it through proper channels. Knowingly staying silent would turn your dilemma into your own lapse.

- **1-13 How do multiple messages cause misunderstanding?** Through competing messages and selective attention, information overload, and noise and distractions (multitasking) [p10–p11, p15].
- **1-14 Why invest in AI communication tools?** They deliver the five benefits; see the ICT feature examples [p14, p18–p21].
- **1-15 An interview panel choosing the internal "acting" candidate who doesn't meet the advertised essential criteria over a better-qualified outsider.** *Application answer:* it becomes a **lapse** if the advertised criteria are quietly ignored (unfair to candidate 1, misleading to everyone in the process). It is a genuine **dilemma** only if both choices can be defended and the decision is made openly. Test it with the five questions [p23–p24].

**7.3, Questions for Comprehension and Critical Thinking** [7.3-p14]
- **1.1 Three basic purposes of business messages?** Inform · request or persuade · build goodwill.
- **1.2 Five basic criteria for effective messages?** Clear · complete · correct · saves the reader's time · builds goodwill.
- **1.3 What does PAIBOC stand for?** Purposes · Audience · Information · Benefits · Objections · Context.
- **1.4 Why do writing and speaking matter more as you rise?** Non-routine problems need original messages (form letters only cover routine ones). Managers do their own writing. Writing makes you visible. Management happens through communication (Mintzberg's three jobs) [p5–p6].
- **1.5 Why do they matter even in a low-level job?** Even entry-level jobs need high-level communication; employers rank it first; doing it well gets you promoted [p4–p5].
- **1.6 Why do you need purpose, audience and context to judge a piece of wording?** Effectiveness depends on the interaction of writer, audience, purposes and situation; "no single set of words will work in all possible situations" [p9].

**7.4, Questions for Comprehension and Critical Thinking** [7.4-p15]
- **2.1 Five kinds of audiences?** Initial · gatekeeper · primary · secondary · watchdog.
- **2.2 Ways to analyze an audience?** As individuals or as group members. Look at knowledge, demographics, personality (MBTI), values and beliefs (psychographics, VALS) and past behaviour; in organizations, add discourse community and organizational culture. Always with common sense and empathy.
- **2.3 Three ways to adapt a message?** Strategy · organization · style (the reading adds document design and visuals, plus channel choice).
- **2.6 Why can internal audiences (your boss) feel more important than the external primary audience?** The boss is often both the initial audience and the gatekeeper, who can stop the message. Your success depends on what "counts" in your organization [p2–p3].
- **2.7 What if your boss's criteria differ from the primary audience's?** Find out what counts by asking the boss [p2]. When needs conflict, meet the **gatekeeper's and the primary audience's** needs first [p12], which here means satisfying both: meet the boss's requirements while still giving the primary audience what it needs, and discuss the difference with the boss.

### 6.4 Short application questions (quick answers)
1. *"Send me the report as soon as possible."* What is the risk? **Ambiguous language and usage (a decoding problem)**; give a specific date and time [7.1-p12].
2. A client's e-mail lands in the junk folder. **Barrier: filter (unintentional)** [7.1-p10].
3. A colleague forgot to forward your message to the boss. **Channel breakdown** [7.1-p10].
4. An ad styled like a news article, with no label. **Native advertising**: a transparency problem (withholding), and the FTC requires a label [7.1-p22].
5. "Sales jumped 40% in April!" after March sales collapsed. **Distorting information** (trend manipulation) [7.1-p22].
6. Your boss must approve your plan before the client sees it. **Gatekeeper** (and initial audience) [7.4-p3].
7. Industry reviewers can't block your report but shape your future contracts. **Watchdog** [7.4-p3].
8. "Consider morale, the economy, the time of year." Which PAIBOC letter? **C, context** [7.3-p12].
9. 54 minutes per letter at $23.50 an hour costs how much? **$21.15** [7.3-p8].
10. Best channel for complex financial data? **Written.** For building consensus? **Oral** [7.4-p14].
11. Make "the ESOP changes in May" reader-friendly. **"The Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP) changes in May…"** [7.4-p7].
12. Where does the main point go in bad news? **Not first**: let the reader down gradually [7.4-p11].
13. "Although I read my e-mail." **Fragment** (a subordinate clause on its own) [7.3-p18].
14. An AI tool writes personalized game recaps. **Automated writing** (efficiency) [7.1-p18].
15. The boss says "I think it's fine" and you start to worry. **Re-created meaning** (Figure 1.1) [7.1-p3].
