## 1. Big picture

- **What part 2 of Week 7 covers.** It is the *interpersonal* side of professional communication: working in **teams**, **collaborating** on documents, running **meetings**, **listening**, **nonverbal** signals and **etiquette** (7.5). It then covers communicating **across cultures** (7.8.1), one email shown **ineffective vs effective** (7.8.2), a real company's **business-conduct policy** as a sample (7.2), and a manipulative pattern to recognize, **gaslighting** (7.10).
- **Teams [7.5].** A team is 2+ people with a shared goal who **depend on one another**. Teams bring 9 advantages but risk **groupthink**, **hidden agendas**, cost and overload. Effective teams share 8 traits, with **psychological safety** and open communication among them. Members play **task** or **maintenance** roles and move through **Tuckman's 5 stages** (forming → storming → norming → performing → adjourning).
- **Conflict [7.5].** Conflict can be **constructive** or **destructive**. Resolve it in **5 steps**, ending with a choice of **avoid / accommodate / compromise / collaborate** (collaborate = win-win). **Virtual teams** need small size, the best tools, one clear purpose per tool, care for isolated members, and more than written messages.
- **Collaborative writing & feedback [7.5].** Arrangements range from writer–editor to full collaboration. **Plan and outline together, but write separately**, and agree on goals, process, strengths, checkpoints and styles. Give **constructive feedback** (about the work, not the person; Table 2.2), never **destructive** feedback. Collaboration platforms: CMS, wikis, shared workspaces, social networks, workgroup messaging, intranets/extranets.
- **Meetings [7.5].** First ask whether the meeting is **necessary**. Then set the **purpose**, choose **participants** (≤10–12), choose the **venue and time**, and write an **agenda that answers 3 questions**. During the meeting, lead and participate well; afterwards, send **minutes** (decisions, action items, owners, deadlines, deferred items). Also learn the boxed **meeting-productivity checklist**, the **Five-Minute Guide** and the virtual-meeting tips.
- **Listening and nonverbal signals [7.5].** Listening is hard: it happens in **real time**, speech is **invisible**, and sound must be **converted to language**. Five listener habits make it worse. Improve in **3 steps**: minimize barriers, choose the right **style** (content, critical or empathic), and **listen actively** (5 elements). **Nonverbal** signals can **strengthen, weaken or replace** words and come in **6 categories**. The "7% words / 93% nonverbal" claim is **not** a valid rule.
- **Etiquette [7.5].** Etiquette = the **expected norms of polite behaviour**. It applies in **5 zones**: workplace, social settings, online, telephone and mobile devices. Work attire has **4 levels** of formality.
- **Intercultural communication [7.8.1].** **Diversity** = everything that defines individuals. **Culture** = a shared system of symbols, beliefs, attitudes, values, expectations and norms. **Cultural competency** = attitude + knowledge + skills. Avoid **ethnocentrism and stereotyping** and practise **cultural pluralism**. Recognize the **8 dimensions** of cultural diversity, especially **high- vs low-context** cultures. Treat people the way **they** want to be treated, and improve your skills in **6 steps**: plain writing, no idioms or humour, speaking slowly, asking for feedback.
- **One email, fixed point by point [7.8.2].** The effective version has a specific subject line, a warm greeting, background for absentees, a positive tone, a clear "who does what by when", the full date and time, an inclusive dial-in option, a warm close and a full signature.
- **Conduct policy and gaslighting [7.2, 7.10].** **Apple's policy** shows how a code of conduct is **organized** (values → principles → duties and reporting → themed sections → resources) and **written** ("you"/"we", imperatives, in-text definitions, concrete limits, named contacts). **Gaslighting** is emotional and psychological abuse that makes you **doubt your own reality**. Know its **5 tactics**, typical phrases, **10 signs**, **4 first steps** and how to help someone. It can also happen **at work**.

#### A. Teams (LO1) [p2–7]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Collaboration** = working together to meet complex challenges. It is a prime skill in almost every profession. [p2]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Team** = 2+ people working toward a shared goal who, unlike other work groups, **depend on one another** to reach it. [p2]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Participative management** = involving employees in the company's decision-making. Teams are often its core. [p2]
- Teams range from 2 people with an informal mandate to 100+ people with formal management. Some are **appointed-leader** teams and others are **self-managed**. [p2]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Cross-functional team** = people from more than one department. It overcomes the **silo effect** (each function sees its work from inside its own "silo") and acts as a communication network. However, it is more prone to conflict and needs strong leadership. [p2]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Table 2.1, Common types of business teams** [p2]:
  - **Committee**: permanent, for recurring issues (e.g., governance, workplace safety).
  - **Problem-solving** (a "task force"): analyses a problem, recommends a solution and sometimes implements it.
  - **Project**: completes one specific project (e.g., a product launch).
  - **Creative**: like a project team, but in advertising or web design, often on a series of projects.
  - **Cross-functional**: spans departments; best for companywide issues but hard to manage.
- 🖍 **Advantages of teams (9)** [p3]:
  - more information and knowledge
  - learning opportunities
  - boldness (people take calculated risks more readily as a group)
  - accountability
  - trust building
  - a broader range of viewpoints
  - **buy-in** for the solutions the team creates
  - improved performance (better than top individuals on complex problems)
  - a sense of community in good times and bad
- 🖍 **Disadvantages (4)** [p3]:
  - ⭐ **Groupthink**: peer pressure makes members **withhold contrary opinions** and go along with decisions they don't believe in. Results range from bland work to disasters.
  - ⭐ **Hidden agenda**: private, counterproductive motives (to take control, undermine someone, or pursue an incompatible goal).
  - **Cost**: aligning schedules and coordinating takes time and money.
  - **Overload**: too many team assignments for employees.
- **Ethics Detective, "How Did 'We' Turn into 'I'?"** [p3]. You wrote a marketing plan and credited yourself and your boss. The boss then removed your name and changed the document's author. The reading asks whether that is ethical and how to react: query the boss, or raise it with the CEO's secretary? It gives no answer.
- ⭐ **Characteristics of effective teams (8)** [p4; LO1 summary p31]:
  1. a shared purpose and compatible values
  2. a clear, challenging goal
  3. belief in the value of the team's efforts
  4. a well-balanced mix of people
  5. a size that fits the team's responsibilities
  6. **psychological safety** (people can share information, propose unproven ideas and show vulnerability without fear)
  7. willingness to put the team's needs first
  8. open and honest communication

  The reading notes that **all eight rely on communication**.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Group dynamics** = the interactions and processes among team members. 🖍 ⭐ **Norms** = informal, shared standards of conduct that guide behaviour. Both are shaped by the roles members take and by how the team evolves. [p4]
- 🖍 **Team roles** [p4]:
  - ⭐ **Team-maintenance roles** handle the interpersonal and social side: enforcing norms, making sure everyone is heard, encouraging participation, compromising, mediating.
  - ⭐ **Task-oriented roles** handle work quality and productivity: technical expertise, new ideas, integrating ideas, exploring options, keeping to schedule and budget.
  - A team needs a **healthy balance** of both. People often hold several roles, and anyone can step into one temporarily (e.g., mediating).
- 🖍 ⭐ **Tuckman's stages of team development (Fig. 2.1)** [p4–5]:
  - **Forming**: members come together and settle into roles; a formal or informal leader emerges; the goal is set if not yet defined.
  - **Storming**: positions are asserted and disagreement arises. Constructive conflict is fine, but destructive conflict must be resolved. Everyone helps by valuing diverse views, assuming good intent and letting everyone be heard.
  - **Norming**: conflicts are resolved and the team gets a cohesive personality and clear norms (e.g., civility, dependability), which it then enforces.
  - **Performing**: productive work, individually or together. New conflicts can send the team back to storming and re-norming.
  - **Adjourning**: a temporary team disbands, may do a post-project analysis and records advice for future teams.
  - Note: the text says "four or five phases" (adjourning applies only to temporary teams), while the margin says "usually … five". [p4]
- 🖍 **Resolving conflict** [p5–6]:
  - Causes: competition for resources, disagreement over goals or methods, personality differences.
  - ⭐ **Constructive** conflict forces important issues into the open, increases involvement and generates creative ideas ("Virtuoso teams are not about getting polite results"). [p5]
  - **Destructive** conflict diverts energy, destroys morale and polarizes the team. It leads to **win-lose or lose-lose** outcomes. Approaching conflict as **win-win** (both sides partly satisfied) minimizes losses. [p5]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Five steps to resolve team conflict** [p6; LO1 summary p31]:
  1. **Decide whether it's worth addressing.** It costs time and energy; a minor conflict may disappear on its own (e.g., when a temporary team disbands).
  2. **Examine your own beliefs and behaviours.** You may be contributing to the conflict.
  3. **Identify where it truly originates.** An argument about methods may really be about cultural priorities, such as group harmony vs individual career success.
  4. **Establish common ground.** Find what everyone agrees on (e.g., company goals) and build from there.
  5. **Choose a strategy**, one of four 🖍:
     - **Avoid**: e.g., don't assign people who clash to the same task. It doesn't fix the cause but can be the most efficient option.
     - **Accommodate**: one side gives way for the organization or the relationship.
     - **Compromise**: both sides give something up. "Balanced compromise" is a hallmark of successful teams.
     - **Collaborate**: a new solution that satisfies everyone (**win-win**).
  - 🖍 Whatever the strategy: **respectful, calm communication**, carefully chosen words and gestures, and **active listening**. [p6]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Virtual team** = members in **at least two locations** who rely on technology to communicate and collaborate. It covers telecommuting, colleagues in other offices and remote contractors. [p6]
  - **Benefits** [p6–7]: the best people regardless of location; the economic and personal benefits of telecommuting; less travel; sometimes the only option (e.g., outsourced manufacturing). Studies show successful virtual teams can be **more effective, engaged and productive than co-located teams**.
  - 🖍 **Challenges (4)** [p7]: limits of the technology; emotional isolation and feeling "**out of the loop**"; an "**us versus them**" split between locations; missing the random interactions of a shared office (e.g., running into each other over coffee).
  - 🖍 **Tips for success (5)** [p7]:
    1. Keep the team as small as possible (trouble starts above about 10 people).
    2. Use the best collaboration technology available.
    3. **Clarify the purpose of each tool**: one designated channel per kind of message (schedule updates, task assignments, urgent messages).
    4. Take special care with isolated members: if most of the team shares an office, someone local is responsible for keeping remote members in the loop.
    5. **Don't rely solely on written communication**; phone or video keeps a personal connection.

#### B. Collaborating on communication (LO2) [p7–11]
- 🖍 **Arrangements** range from a simple **writer–editor** relationship to **full collaboration**. [p7]
  - **Writer–editor** [p7–8]: one person reviews another's work, sometimes with several reviewers (e.g., an engineer checks technical accuracy and a marketer checks brand messaging). Revising someone's work makes you a **co-creator**. 🖍 **Clarify roles and expectations up front.** In a general review, the reviewer may suggest anything and the writer may accept or reject each suggestion, which change tracking makes easy. If someone revises your draft, explain your objectives, audience and message, then release "**emotional ownership**".
  - 🖍 **Full collaboration** [p8]: working together from planning to final production. **Composing as a group** is often slow and frustrating, because writing is individual and ideas can be phrased in many valid ways. It can still be a good way to mentor. It is usually better to **plan, research and outline together, then assign the writing** to one person or divide it. If you divide it, **outline the whole document in detail first**, and have one person do a **final revision pass** for a consistent style.
  - 🖍 ⭐ **Tips for any collaborative project (5)** [p8; LO2 summary p32]:
    1. Make sure the goals are clear and agreed on.
    2. Map out how the work will be done (who leads each task, which systems and tools).
    3. Structure assignments around each person's strengths.
    4. Set frequent checkpoints.
    5. Appreciate different writing styles: "If something works, support it."
- 🖍 ⭐ **Constructive feedback** (constructive criticism) focuses on the **process and outcomes** of communication, **not the people**. When you receive it, resist the urge to defend your work or deny the feedback. 🖍 Treat it as a chance to learn and improve. [p8]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Table 2.2, Giving constructive feedback (8)** [p9]:
  1. **Think through suggested changes carefully**: isolated, superficial edits can do harm.
  2. **Discuss improvements rather than flaws**: explain how to make it clearer rather than just saying "this is confusing".
  3. **Focus on controllable behaviour**: things the writer can actually change.
  4. **Be specific**: "I don't get this" gives no direction.
  5. **Keep feedback impersonal**: comment on the message, not the person.
  6. **Verify understanding**: check that the recipient understood your feedback.
  7. **Time your feedback carefully**: leave the writer time to make the changes.
  8. **Highlight your feedback's limitations**: say if you edited quickly or aren't an expert in some part.
- ⭐ **Destructive feedback** is "little more than complaining or bullying"; the key-term definition is criticism **with no guidance** for improvement. The reading contrasts calling a proposal "a confusing mess" with a specific suggestion: describe the manufacturing process more clearly and explain better why the positives outweigh the negatives. [p9, p31]
- **Technologies** [p9–11]:
  - The simplest tools are **commenting** (notes without changing the text) and **change tracking** (proposed edits kept separate and reversible). Some systems also allow live co-editing.
  - **Cloud computing** = the software and files live on the internet, so the whole team can reach them.
  - ⭐ **Major collaboration platforms (6)** [p9–10; LO2 summary p32]:
    1. **Content management systems (CMS)**: for high volumes of digital content; offer **version control** (two people can't unknowingly edit at once) and **workflow** (how content is created, edited and published).
    2. ⭐ **Wikis**: websites where anyone with access can add or edit material; less structured than a CMS.
    3. **Shared online workspaces**: "virtual offices" with shared resources and real-time tools such as digital whiteboards (Fig. 2.2).
    4. **Social networks**: often private and internal; host **communities of practice**.
    5. **Workgroup messaging systems**: messaging plus file sharing and meeting hosting.
    6. **Private networks**: **intranets** (employees only) and **extranets** (employees plus invited outsiders); some have become **social intranets** (Fig. 2.3, Jostle).
  - **Mobile collaboration**, and ⭐ **unified communication** = voice and video calling, conferencing, instant messaging and real-time collaboration in **one system**. [p11]
  - **AI-enabled collaboration**: **taskbots** handle scheduling, data collection and document distribution, and some bots guide meetings. Slack treats bots as team members. The box "Hi, I'm an Algorithm, Your New Teammate" splits bots into **taskbots** (chores; robotic process automation) and **chatbots** (conversation, e.g., Siri, Alexa). Professionals spend 10–20% of work hours on routine tasks. [p11]

#### C. Meetings (LO3) [p11–18]
- **Why they matter.** Much of your workplace communication happens in meetings, and your meeting skills affect how your contributions are recognized. Good meetings solve problems, develop ideas, identify opportunities and build teams; badly run ones waste time. [p11]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Preparing for a meeting** [p12]. First make sure the meeting is **truly necessary**: if a blog post or other message would do, don't meet. Then do **4 planning tasks**:
  1. 🖍 **Define the purpose.** Meetings exchange information, reach decisions, or collaborate to solve problems or find opportunities. **Visualize the best possible outcome** and plan the content from it. If you can't state the purpose **in one sentence**, keep refining.
  2. 🖍 **Select participants.** Invite everyone who really needs to be involved and nobody else. Meetings with **more than 10–12 people** become unmanageable if everyone is expected to take part.
  3. 🖍 **Choose the venue and the time.** Online meetings are often best for multiple locations. For in-person meetings, check the seating (Fig. 2.4), temperature, lighting, ventilation, acoustics and refreshments. **Morning** meetings can be more productive.
  4. 🖍 **Set the agenda.** Distribute it early. A productive agenda answers **3 questions**:
     - What do we need to do in this meeting to accomplish our goals?
     - Which issues matter most to all participants?
     - What information must be available to discuss them?

     Besides the detailed **planning agenda** (Fig. 2.5), you can project a simpler **presentation agenda** (Fig. 2.6) to keep time.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 2.4, Meeting room configurations** [p13]:
  - **Conference room**: small meetings and working sessions; everyone can talk to everyone.
  - **U-shape**: mid-sized meetings; the presenter can walk inside the U; supports conversation.
  - **Theater/auditorium**: large audiences; limits interaction, and documents or devices are hard to use without desk surfaces.
  - **Classroom**: like theater style but with work surfaces; less interaction than a U-shape.
  - **Breakout sessions**: small groups for role-play, brainstorming or problem solving; no interaction between groups.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 2.5, Sample full agenda** ("Merger Issues Brainstorming Session") [p14]. A header gives location, date, time, facilitator, 3 bullet objectives and a **prestudy** file. A table lists **time slot | topic (with its key issues) | discussion leader**, including a break and a closing "wrap up", and the agenda ends with a contact for questions. **Fig. 2.6** is the same agenda condensed onto one slide (times, topics, leaders) for the facilitator to use during the meeting.
- 🖍 **Leading and contributing** [p12–15]. **Everyone shares responsibility** for a productive meeting; the leader has extra accountability. Guidelines:
  - **Keep the discussion on track.** Leaders guide, mediate, probe, stimulate, summarize and redirect, and every participant helps.
  - **Follow agreed-on rules.** The bigger the meeting, the more formal it must be. ⭐ **Parliamentary procedure** is a time-tested method for running meetings; its best-known guide is **Robert's Rules of Order**. 🔎 Track B for details.
  - **Encourage and moderate participation.** 🖍 Make room for people who think out loud *and* people who finalize their thoughts first, and for anyone who is quiet or was interrupted.
  - **Participate actively**, but don't talk just to show what you know.
  - **Don't interrupt.** The loudest people don't necessarily have the best ideas.
  - **Use mobile devices respectfully**, and only if that is accepted practice in your company.
  - **Close effectively.** Verify the objectives were met or arrange follow-up, summarize conclusions and actions, and make sure everyone understands and agrees.
- **Putting results to use** [p15]. Follow up on the issues raised, act on action items, inform affected people who missed the meeting, and keep a record so participants can verify their impressions.
  - 🖍 ⭐ **Minutes** = a written summary of the **important information presented and the decisions made**. One person is assigned to take notes and share them afterwards.
  - ⭐ **What minutes typically include** [p15]:
    - who was present, and who was invited but absent
    - the start and end times
    - **all major decisions**
    - **all task assignments**
    - subjects **deferred** to a later meeting
    - an **objective** summary of important discussions, with the names of those who made major points
    - attachments (handouts, slides)

    The format matters less than recording everything key, especially the responsibilities assigned.
  - 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 2.7, Effective meeting minutes** ("Employee Onboarding Update", March 16, 2:00–3:00 p.m.) [p16]. Header: meeting called by, type of meeting (status update), facilitator, note taker, attendees. Each agenda item then has its **presenter**, a short **discussion** summary and an **action items** table: ✓ task | **person responsible** | **deadline**. The last item has discussion only; it ends with an issue to be raised at a later executive council.
  - Fig. 2.8: meeting software (MeetingBooster) captures decisions. Some systems forward action items to each person, record audio and keep all files in one place. [p15, p17]
- ⭐ **Checklist: Improving Meeting Productivity** [p15]. Full list in §4.
- **Virtual meetings** [p17–18]:
  - ⭐ **Virtual meetings** = participants in two or more locations connected by technology (the key-term definition says "online rather than in person"). Online seminars are **webinars**.
  - Tools range from conference calls and group chats to real-time collaboration systems, immersive **telepresence** and even holograms.
  - **Teleconferencing** is cheap and fine for routine meetings. For complex topics, emotional issues or brainstorming, use **video**: it carries more nonverbal signals and helps new team members bond faster. WebRTC brings video to ordinary browsers and apps.
  - **Tips (12)** [p18]:
    1. Give the meeting a well-defined task.
    2. Send prestudy materials with the agenda, and say what is expected of people.
    3. In large meetings, assign roles: facilitator, technical specialist, minutes taker.
    4. Make sure everyone has up-to-date software.
    5. Do introductions if people don't know each other.
    6. Explain how to ask questions if there is no "raise hand" feature.
    7. **Log in on time.**
    8. **Be present** mentally and emotionally; don't multitask.
    9. **Mute** if you need to cough or make noise.
    10. Watch keyboard noise (mute while typing).
    11. **Summarize each agenda item** and ask for questions.
    12. As facilitator, **check in with each person by name** ("Does that work for you?").

#### D. Listening (LO4) [p18–24]
- Communication needs the mindful participation of **both sender and receiver**. Listening is how you gather information, do your job and build trust (the OpenText lesson). [p18–19]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Table 2.3, Factors that complicate listening** [p19–20]:
  - **3 unique challenges**:
    - **Real-time experience**: speech is consumed as it is created; you can't scroll back.
    - **Invisibility**: there is no visual record if you get lost, and asking a speaker such as a top executive to repeat can feel intimidating.
    - **Sound-to-language conversion**: sounds must become words first, which mumbling, accents, ambient noise and hearing difficulty all disrupt.
  - **5 individual choices and behaviours**:
    - **Poor self-management**: unmanaged emotions; this calls for **emotional intelligence**.
    - **Idle brain power**: the brain processes language **3–4 times faster** than people speak, so an unfocused mind wanders.
    - **Ineffective listening style** for the situation.
    - **Barriers to physical reception**: on the speaker's side (mumbling), the receiver's side (music playing) or in the environment (an open-plan office).
    - **Flawed recall**: you must store information while new information keeps arriving, which is worse with a rambling speaker.
- ⭐ **Three steps to becoming a better listener** [p20]: **(1) minimize barriers, (2) adapt your style, (3) listen actively.**
  1. **Minimize barriers** [p20–21]:
     - **External barriers** = anything in the environment or channel that makes it hard to hear or focus. Fix them, or limit their effect (e.g., summarize key information during the call or in writing). Tell the other person if you can't hear, and **don't guess**.
     - **Internal barriers** = listener behaviours, thoughts and emotions. They include not paying attention or multitasking (which is also disrespectful); **selective attention** and perceptual bias (moulding the message to fit your beliefs); deciding before you've heard the whole message; 🖍 ⭐ **defensive listening** (protecting your ego by tuning out anything that doesn't confirm your beliefs or self-image); and anger or annoyance, which make you judge or reject what you hear.
  2. ⭐ **Adapt your listening style (Table 2.4)**. All three styles are forms of active listening. [p21–22]
     - 🖍 **Content listening**: understand and **retain** the information. Don't evaluate it, and ignore the speaker's appearance, vocabulary or rank. Ask clarifying questions but **don't challenge**, or the speaker may "shut down".
     - 🖍 **Critical listening**: understand **and evaluate**: the logic, the strength of the evidence, the validity of the conclusions, the implications, the speaker's intentions and motives, and any **omissions**. Separate opinion from fact and watch for bias and logical fallacies. It does **not** mean listening in order to criticize.
     - 🖍 **Empathic listening**: understand the speaker's **feelings, needs and wants**, whether or not you agree. The speaker can share without being judged. It complements critical listening, since you switch off your critical faculties. Sometimes simply being heard matters more than the information, and this is hard for natural problem-solvers.
  3. 🖍 ⭐ **Listen actively.** **Active listening** = a conscious effort to engage with others and **turn off your internal filters and biases**. ⭐ **Table 2.5, Five elements of active listening** [p22–23]:
     - **Open and positive state of mind**: commit to the person and the time; expect your beliefs to be challenged; accept that you may not have all the answers.
     - **Active engagement**: manage your wandering mind, and "box" unrelated worries for later.
     - **Respect for silence**: don't speak at the first pause or finish someone's sentence, especially not to look smart.
     - **Nonverbal awareness**: signals can explain or amplify the message.
     - **Thoughtful note-taking**: don't rely on memory. Notes aren't always appropriate (e.g., for personal or confidential talk, or when someone asks your advice). They also help you engage, list your questions and evaluate critically.
- **Memorizing when you can't take notes** [p23]. For short-term memory, repeat the information silently or make mental lists. For long-term memory, use **4 techniques**: (1) **associate** it with something related, (2) **categorize** it into logical groups, (3) **visualize** words as pictures, (4) create **mnemonics** (acronyms, rhymes).
- ⭐ **Table 2.6, Effective vs ineffective listeners** (14 pairs) [p23]: condensed list in §4.
- ⭐ **Checklist: Improving Your Listening Skills** (7 items) [p24]: in §4.

#### E. Nonverbal communication (LO5) [p24–26]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Nonverbal communication** = sending and receiving information, **intentionally and unintentionally, without using language**. [p24]
- ⭐ **Three roles** [p24]:
  - **strengthen or repeat** the verbal message (when signals match the words)
  - **weaken or contradict** it (when they don't match)
  - **replace** words entirely
- 🖍 Nonverbal signals aren't limited to your face, gestures and other personal behaviours. Even the design and formatting of a written message send nonverbal signals. [p24]
- 🖍 **A myth to reject** [p24]: the claim that words carry **7%** of meaning and nonverbal elements **93%** (tone of voice 38%, body language 55%). It comes from experiments more than 50 years old that assessed only the **speaker's feelings** and were never confirmed, so "ignore any claim" of a fixed percentage. 🔎 Track B (Mehrabian) if you want background; the reading names the source only in its endnotes.
- 🖍 **Why nonverbal signals are complex** [p24]:
  - When sending, some signals are **controllable** (clothing), some are **habits** (finger tapping), some are **trainable** (gestures in a presentation) and some are **involuntary** (blushing).
  - When receiving, signals are **not always reliable** (avoiding eye contact ≠ hiding something), and people vary in how well they read them.
  - On the phone, voice pitch and rate carry the signals.
- ⭐ **Six categories (Fig. 2.10)** [p24–25]:
  1. **Facial expressions**: the main vehicle for emotion (its type *and* intensity). Expressions are contagious (a smile or frown triggers the same), and the eyes show attention and interest, influence others, regulate interaction and establish dominance.
  2. **Vocal characteristics**: pitch, pace and stress, used intentionally (stress changes the meaning of "What are you doing?") or unintentionally (fear raises pitch and pace).
  3. **Gesture and posture**: some gestures have specific, intentional meanings (a wave); others are unconscious (slouching, leaning forward, fidgeting, walking briskly) and reveal confidence or nervousness, friendliness or hostility.
  4. **Personal appearance**: people judge it, fairly or not. You control grooming, clothing, accessories, piercings, tattoos and hairstyle. Adopt the style of the people you want to impress, and know the dress code.
  5. **Touch**: conveys warmth, comfort, reassurance, and also **control**. It is governed by culture, and a manager's hug can read as dominance or sexual interest. **Rule: no touching at work other than handshakes**; reciprocate only if you are comfortable.
  6. **Time and space**: making people wait signals self-importance, while being on time shows respect. Don't stand too close. Expectations vary by culture.
- **Using nonverbal signals effectively** [p26]:
  - 🖍 Make sure your signals **don't undermine your success**: use an open, forward posture when pitching collaboration, not leaning back with arms crossed.
  - You also send signals when silent (clothes, how you sit and walk). Looking your best builds confidence.
  - When listening, ask whether the cues **amplify, obscure or contradict** the words (a thin, forced smile after "How did your meeting with the boss go?").
  - 🖍 **Don't assume you can "read someone like a book"**, especially someone whose normal behaviour you don't know. Avoiding eye contact and touching your face are **not** reliable signs of lying, and in some cultures sustained eye contact is disrespectful.
  - If something feels off, **ask an honest, respectful question**.
- ⭐ **Checklist: Improving Your Nonverbal Communication Skills** (7 items) [p26]: in §4.

#### F. Business etiquette (LO6) [p26–30]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Etiquette** = the expected norms of polite behaviour in any situation. It underpins teamwork, collaboration, meetings and listening, which all depend on **mutual respect**. Poor etiquette drives away customers and investors, limits your career and drains morale and productivity, and rudeness is **on the rise**. [p26]
- **Rule of thumb** [p27]: be aware of your **effect on others**, treat everyone with **respect**, and remember that the impressions you leave can last, for you and for the company.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 2.11, The five zones of professional etiquette** [p27]: **in the workplace · in social settings** (when representing your firm) **· online · on the telephone · with mobile devices**.
- 🖍 **Workplace etiquette (10 tips)** [p27]:
  1. Respect people's time (be punctual).
  2. Don't interrupt.
  3. Watch your language (no profanity).
  4. Keep clean and avoid strong scents.
  5. Avoid eating at your desk (especially in open-plan offices).
  6. **Keep the noise down**: no meetings in your cubicle, no loud music (even earbuds can disturb others).
  7. Respect personal space: knock first, and don't enter a cubicle uninvited.
  8. Don't gossip.
  9. **Don't come to work sick.**
  10. Avoid religion, politics and other emotional topics unless your company encourages such discussion.
- **Appearance** [p27–28]. This isn't mindless conformity; it shows respect for an organizational culture "**bigger than you**". Match the style where you work: finance is more formal than tech, creative or industrial firms, and sales and executive roles are more formal than engineering or manufacturing.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Four levels of business attire** [p28]:
  1. 🖍 **Business formal**: tailored suits in conservative colours; the most formal occasions, top executives.
  2. 🖍 **Business professional**: suits or coordinated jackets with skirts or trousers; slightly freer and less expensive.
  3. **Business casual**: slacks or skirts with button-up shirts, blouses and sweaters.
  4. **Casual**: the hardest to pin down (from nice jeans to shorts and sandals).

  The U.S. trend is toward casual, but observe and ask. If unsure, "**dress modestly and simply**" and earn a reputation for what you do, not what you wear.
- 🖍 **Social settings** [p28–29]:
  - Etiquette matters most when you **represent your company in public**.
  - Learn local greeting customs: a firm handshake in North America, a bow in Japan. Stand when shaking hands if you can.
  - 🖍 **Introducing yourself**: first and last name plus a brief description of your role.
  - **Introducing others**: both full names plus something they share (e.g., a professional interest). 🖍 Introduce **younger people to older people** and **lower-ranking to higher-ranking** people.
  - **Business meals** are for business: avoid politics, religion and emotional topics, complaining about work, deeply personal questions and profanity, and be careful with humour.
  - **Receptions**: don't push business cards or pitch to everyone, and pause to bring a newcomer into your conversation.
- 🖍 **Online etiquette (10 guidelines)** [p29]. 🖍 Digital media are "a breeding ground for poor etiquette", so whenever you represent the company online, keep a high standard:
  1. Avoid personal attacks.
  2. Stay on the original topic (start a new thread for a new subject).
  3. Follow basic spelling, punctuation and capitalization; acronym-filled messages hurt your credibility.
  4. Keep virus protection up to date.
  5. Watch your language and control your emotions ("a single indiscretion could haunt you forever").
  6. Don't multitask while messaging.
  7. Don't waste people's time with sloppy or incomplete messages.
  8. **Never assume privacy**: anything you type may be stored, forwarded or read by your boss or security staff.
  9. Be careful with commenting systems (a Facebook login can show your job and employer).
  10. Respect boundaries of time and virtual space: no business on colleagues' personal social accounts, and nobody is available around the clock.
- **Telephone etiquette (6 tips)** [p29–30]:
  1. **Be conscious of your voice**: vary pitch and inflection, and slow down for non-native speakers.
  2. **Be courteous when you call**: give your name and organization, say why you're calling, and ask if it's a good time.
  3. **Answer positively and professionally**: promptly, "with a smile", identifying yourself and the company. Before forwarding a call, put the caller on hold and check that the next person is available.
  4. **End calls with courtesy and clarity**: double-check vital details such as meeting times and dates.
  5. **Use your own voicemail well**: a brief, professional greeting, and a temporary one when you're away. If you never check voicemail, disable it or say so in the greeting; letting messages pile up is "extremely thoughtless".
  6. **Leave considerate voicemails**: consider text or email instead. If you do leave one, keep it brief: name, number, reason and when you can be reached.
- **Mobile-device etiquette** [p30]. Avoid **10 disrespectful choices**:
  1. obnoxious ringtones
  2. not muting in meetings
  3. talking loudly in open offices or public places
  4. talking on the phone right next to someone
  5. personal calls at work
  6. using the camera without permission
  7. calls in restrooms and similar places
  8. texting during meals or while someone is talking to you
  9. letting incoming calls interrupt meetings
  10. voice recognition that disturbs others

  Voice assistants raise new etiquette problems. Company norms vary: investor **Ben Horowitz fines employees** who look at a device during an entrepreneur's pitch.

⭐ **Learning objectives** [p1]:
1. Discuss the **opportunities and challenges** of intercultural communication.
2. Define **cultural competency**, and explain how culture influences business communication.
3. Explain why recognizing cultural variations matters, and list **eight key dimensions of cultural diversity**.
4. List **four general guidelines** for adapting to any business culture.
5. Identify **six steps** to improve your intercultural communication skills.

**Opening case: Siemens AG [p1–2].** Siemens has about 385,000 employees in more than 200 countries. Its German staff come from 125+ nations, and its global management from 107+ countries. Siemens treats diversity as a source of innovative capacity. Former personnel manager Janina Kugel (TEDxBerlin 2015, "Using the Power of Being Different") said that to manage a global organization **you need to listen to people**, and that no single culture can exist in a company that operates in 200+ countries. Siemens signed the UN Women's Empowerment Principles in 2016. Its chief diversity officer, Maria Ferraro, says diverse, inclusive teams breed creativity and innovation.

#### A. Opportunities and challenges (LO1) [p2–3]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Diversity** = "all the characteristics and experiences that define each of us as individuals", which is broader than race or gender. Some aspects are **inherent** (race, age); others are **acquired** (work history, language, religion, cultural immersion, education). [p2]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Intercultural communication** = sending and receiving messages between people whose cultural backgrounds could lead them to **interpret verbal and nonverbal signs differently**. Culture influences every message. [p2]
- **The global marketplace.** Technology has turned borders into weak barriers, and local markets now face worldwide competition, so expect to work with people from other cultures. [p2]
- 🖍 **Advantages of a diverse workforce (3)** [p2]:
  1. a broader range of views and ideas
  2. a better understanding of diverse, fragmented markets
  3. a broader pool of talent to recruit from

  Leading companies have moved from seeing diversity as a **legal requirement** to seeing it as a **strategic opportunity**, and studies link diversity with company performance.
- 🖍 **Challenges** [p3]:
  - Supervisors must motivate diverse employees and foster harmony, teams must work closely together, and companies must coexist with partners and the community.
  - Culture and communication are almost **impossible to separate**. 🖍 Culture shapes **language, nonverbal signals, word meaning, time and space, and the rules of human relationships**, so intercultural communication goes beyond words to beliefs, values and emotions.
  - 🖍 **You encode messages using your own culture's assumptions; your audience decodes them using theirs.** The further apart the two cultures, the harder it is to transfer meaning. (The chapter refers to its Figure 1.6, which is in Ch. 1 and not in these readings.)

#### B. Cultural competency (LO2) [p3–4]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Cultural competency** = appreciating the cultural differences that affect communication, **plus** the ability to adjust your style so cross-cultural messages succeed. It combines **attitude, knowledge and skills**. [p3]
- You are already an expert in your own culture, but that makes your communication **automatic**. An important step is to notice the rules you follow without thinking. [p3]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Culture** = a shared system of **symbols, beliefs, attitudes, values, expectations and norms for behaviour**. Everyone belongs to several cultures, including **subcultures** (ethnic, religious, political, professional). [p3]
- 🖍 **How culture works** [p4]:
  - Members share assumptions about how to think, behave and communicate.
  - Cultures differ in their rate of change, complexity and tolerance of outsiders, which affects the trust and openness you can reach.
  - 🖍 Culture is learned **directly** (being told) and **indirectly** (by observing), and it becomes **automatic**.
  - 🖍 Culture is **coherent**: it looks logical from the inside, which helps it work smoothly but can cause disharmony with other cultures.
  - Culture is **complete**: it answers life's big questions, which dulls curiosity about other cultures.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Overcoming ethnocentrism and stereotyping** [p4]:
  - **Ethnocentrism** = judging other groups by your own group's standards, behaviours and customs, and often concluding that yours is superior.
  - **Xenophobia** = fear of strangers and foreigners (a more extreme reaction).
  - **Stereotyping** = assigning generalized attributes to an individual because of their group (e.g., assuming an older colleague is out of touch with the youth market, or a younger colleague can't lead).
  - **Cultural pluralism** = accepting multiple cultures **on their own terms**.
  - 🖍 **Three habits** that help:
    1. **Avoid assumptions**: consciously resist your culture's automatic impulses.
    2. **Avoid judgments**: different is not wrong or inferior, and appearance or accent shouldn't sway you.
    3. **Acknowledge distinctions**: don't ignore real differences.
  - This is hard even for motivated people, because we hold **unconscious biases** that may conflict with the beliefs we think we hold.

#### C. Recognizing variations: the 8 dimensions (LO3) [p4–9]
- 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 3.1, Major dimensions of cultural diversity** [p5]. The figure draws two heads facing each other:
  - **Left head**:
    - **Context**: the pattern of physical cues, environmental stimuli and implicit understanding.
    - **Laws & ethics**: explicit legal restrictions and implicit ethical guidelines.
    - **Social customs**: formal and informal rules of behaviour.
    - **Nonverbal signals**: their use and meaning.
  - **Right head**:
    - **Age**: perceptions of age; expectations about capabilities and behaviour.
    - **Gender**: perceptions of gender roles; concepts of gender and sexual orientation.
    - **Religion**: expectations of religious expression; degree of inclusivity.
    - **Ability**: accommodation for the full spectrum of physical and cognitive abilities.
  - Note: the reading calls these the "dimensions of cultural diversity". It does **not** use any other "cultural dimensions" model. 🔎 Look up in Track B if the professor uses "cultural dimensions" in the Hofstede sense.

1. 🖍 **Contextual differences** [p5]:
   - ⭐ **Cultural context** = the pattern of physical cues, environmental stimuli and implicit understanding that conveys meaning between members of the same culture.
   - 🖍 ⭐ **High-context culture**: people rely **less on verbal** communication and **more on nonverbal actions and the setting**. Communication is as much about **building relationships** as exchanging information. The examples given are **China, Japan and many Arab nations**.
   - 🖍 ⭐ **Low-context culture**: people rely **more on explicit verbal** communication. Rules and expectations are spelled out, and communication emphasizes **exchanging information** over relationships. The examples given are the **United States, Germany and Switzerland**.
   - 🖍 Context affects decision-making, problem solving, negotiating, dealings across hierarchy levels and socializing:
     - Low-context people focus on the **results** of a decision ("Will this be good for our company? For my career?").
     - High-context people focus on the **means** of deciding, and relationships count as much as facts, so negotiators may spend most of their time building relationships.
     - These are generalizations: tactics that work in one type of culture can backfire in the other.
2. 🖍 **Legal and ethical differences** [p5–6]. Cultural context shapes legal and ethical behaviour, which in turn affects communication.
   - A U.S. manager treats a **signed contract** as the end of negotiation, while a counterpart in many Asian cultures may treat it as fluid and keep renegotiating.
   - Paying government officials to win contracts is standard practice in some places, but bribery is generally illegal for U.S. companies under the **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act**. 🔎 Track B for details. This links to Apple's bribery rules [7.2-p17].
3. 🖍 **Social norms and customs** [p6]. Some rules are **formal** (table manners) and others **informal** (how close to stand). Norms vary in **6 areas**:
   - **Attitudes toward work and success**: e.g., the U.S. view that material comfort earned by individual effort is a sign of superiority.
   - 🖍 **Roles and status**: who talks to whom, about what and how; attitudes to social class and to women in business.
   - 🖍 **Use of manners**: "How was your weekend?" is small talk in the U.S. but sounds intrusive where business and private life are kept separate.
   - 🖍 **Concepts of time**: low-context cultures treat time as a limited resource, often one task per time slot; high-context cultures treat it as flexible, so a relationship can matter more than a deadline.
   - **Future orientation**: some cultures plan and invest for the long term; others are oriented to the present.
   - 🖍 **Openness and inclusiveness**: from outright exclusion of outsiders to subtle pressure to conform.

   Example: **L'Oréal** trains all staff in one conflict-management approach that encourages open disagreement. It felt unnatural at first to its Chinese employees, but they came to value it. Most people are glad to explain their customs, and they see your **curiosity as a sign of respect**.
4. 🖍 **Nonverbal communication** [p7]. Signals only help if sender and receiver give them the **same meaning**; a thumbs-up, for example, can be rude elsewhere. Study the culture beforehand, then **observe 7 areas**:
   - **Greetings**: handshake, bow or kiss, and how often.
   - **Personal space**: how close people stand.
   - **Touching**: whether people touch an arm or slap a back, and avoiding touch that could read as domineering or sexual.
   - **Facial expressions**: e.g., smiling at strangers is common in the U.S. but not universal.
   - **Eye contact**: in the U.S. it signals honesty and openness; elsewhere it can seem aggressive or disrespectful.
   - **Posture**: slouching vs sitting and standing straight.
   - **Formality**: more or less formal than your culture.

   **Follow the lead of locals**: it is a good way to learn and to show respect.
5. 🖍 **Age differences** [p7–8]:
   - 3–5 generations may now work side by side, each shaped by different events, social trends and technology. Add resistance to change and "my way is best", plus competition for jobs and resources, and the result can be tension, mistrust and communication breakdowns.
   - Labels and birth years follow the **Pew Research Center**. There are no official labels; the U.S. Census uses only "baby boomers".
     - **Silent Generation** (1928–1945)
     - **Baby Boomers** (1946–1964), moving into retirement but holding many top jobs
     - **Generation X** (1965–1980), the second-largest generation in the workforce, caught between the other two, now taking over leadership
     - **Millennials / Gen Y** (1981–1996), the **largest** generation in the workforce, entrepreneurial, wanting a more open and sociable workplace
     - **Post-Millennials / Gen Z** (1997–), just entering the workforce
   - These are broad generalities (there are 70M+ U.S. millennials), and the boundaries are fuzzy. Generations are often **more alike than different**. Make no assumptions about an individual, and don't assume your own group is superior.
6. 🖍 **Gender differences (4 points)** [p8–9]:
   1. Perceptions of men and women in business **vary by culture**; bias ranges from overt discrimination to unconscious belief.
   2. 🖍 Entry-level ratios are roughly equal, but the **share of men rises up the corporate ladder**, which affects mentoring.
   3. 🖍 Broadly, **men emphasize content and outcomes; women emphasize relationship maintenance**. Men are more likely to negotiate a raise, and research by Linda Babcock (Carnegie Mellon) found that both sexes see assertiveness as positive in men but negative in women.
   4. 🖍 Outdated concepts of gender and sexual orientation cause discrimination. Many companies ensure equal opportunity for LGBTQ applicants and employees and protect **gender expression**. Communication matters here: listening, clear policies, education.
7. **Religious differences** [p9]. Religion is personal and influential, so it can cause conflict at work. U.S. law bans religious discrimination and harassment and requires **reasonable accommodation**, but accommodation can clash with business demands, and there are no simple answers.
8. **Ability differences** [p9]:
   - Some colleagues and customers have impairments that affect communication: hearing, vision, cognition, or the physical ability to use devices.
   - **Neurodiversity** = atypical thought and behaviour patterns (e.g., autism, social anxiety disorders).
   - Success starts with respect. Employers can provide **assistive technologies** (for communication, computer use and mobility; AI and robotics are expanding them) and make information **accessible**, e.g., formatting documents for **screen readers** (Word and Acrobat give guidance).

#### D. Adapting to other business cultures (LO4) [p9–10]
- ⭐ **Four general guidelines for adapting to any business culture** [p10]:
  1. **Become aware of your own biases.** Understand how your own culture shapes your habits; e.g., knowing you value independence helps you work in a culture that values consensus.
  2. **Be careful about applying the "Golden Rule".** "Treat people the way you want to be treated" fails across cultures. Instead, **treat people the way *they* want to be treated**. (The margin says "Ignore the Golden Rule".)
  3. **Be accommodating and respectful, even if you don't understand or agree.** Awareness is only the first step; changing attitudes and behaviour takes sustained effort.
  4. **Be patient and keep a sense of humour.** When you make a mistake, apologize, ask the accepted way if appropriate, and move on.
  - ⚠️ The chapter summary words #2 as "**ignoring** the Golden Rule" and #3 as "exercising **tolerance, flexibility, and respect**". Learn both wordings. [p21]
- **Adapting to U.S. business culture (6 points)** [p10]. These are generalizations, for newcomers to the U.S.:
  1. **Individualism**: success through your own effort; can clash with team orientation.
  2. **Equality**: a core value, even though the record is imperfect.
  3. **Privacy and personal space**: people stand farther apart.
  4. **Time and schedules**: punctuality; meetings start and end on time.
  5. **Religion**: no official religion; people are expected to respect each other's beliefs.
  6. **Communication style**: **direct**, focused on content and transactions rather than relationships or group harmony.

#### E. Improving intercultural skills (LO5) [p11–20]
- ⭐ **Fig. 3.2, Components of successful intercultural communication** [p11]. **Overcome ethnocentrism** and **recognize cultural variations** → **enhance your sensitivity** to culture and diversity → **effective communication across cultures**. Feeding into the same goal is **improving your intercultural skills**, through 6 boxes: study other cultures and languages · respect style preferences · use interpreters, translators and translation software · write and speak clearly · listen carefully · help others adapt to your culture.
- ⭐ **Six steps (LO5 summary)** [p21]:
  1. study other **cultures**
  2. study other **languages**
  3. **respect** preferences for communication style
  4. **write** clearly
  5. **speak and listen** carefully
  6. **help others adapt** to your culture
  - ⚠️ Fig. 3.2 groups these differently (cultures + languages in one box, writing + speaking in one box, interpreters as their own box). For an exam list, use the summary's six. [p11, p21]

1. 🖍 **Study other cultures** [p11–12]:
   - Adapting needs knowledge **and** the ability and motivation to change your habits.
   - Companies appoint specialists for countries or regions and offer training.
   - Even a little research helps, and people respond to honest effort. **Don't be afraid to ask questions**: it shows respect.
   - Use websites, books, newspapers, music and films (good for nonverbal customs), but **don't rely on entertainment alone**. Imagine judging the U.S. only by its exported comedies and action films.
   - 🖍 ⭐ **Table 3.1, Doing business in other cultures**: 6 actions, each with research questions:
     - **Understand social customs**: greetings, thanking a host (gift? flowers? a note?), rude gestures, refusing invitations politely, taboo topics, how men and women and young and old interact.
     - **Learn clothing and food preferences**: special attire, colours for mourning, love and joy, taboo clothing, meals per day, use of hands and utensils, the seat of honour.
     - **Assess political patterns**: stability, its effect on business, whether to talk politics.
     - **Understand religious and social beliefs**: which groups, what is sacred, effects on communication, tolerance of minority religions, holidays, food rules.
     - **Learn about economic and business institutions**: homogeneous or heterogeneous society, languages, resources and products, business size and control (family, government), working hours, attitudes to appointments, socializing before business, whether money or a gift is expected for arranging deals.
     - **Appraise ethics, values and laws**: competition or cooperation, attitudes to work and money, and whether **politeness matters more than factual honesty**.
2. **Study other languages** [p13]:
   - Multilingual communicators are in growing demand.
   - Even if your counterparts speak your language, **learn common phrases in theirs**. It shows commitment, and they probably spent years learning yours.
   - Apps help with vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation and real-time translation (Fig. 3.3).
   - The same language isn't spoken the same way everywhere: Québec French differs from France's, and in the U.S. vs the U.K. *period / full stop*, *elevator / lift*, *gasoline / petrol*.
3. **Respect preferences for communication style** [p14–17]:
   - Directness, formality and preferred media vary widely. Communication in many cultures is **more formal and less direct than in the U.S.**, and **politeness is valued highly**. Search online for, e.g., "French business correspondence".
   - 🖍 ⭐ **Fig. 3.4a–d, one intercultural letter revised step by step.** Henry Gatlin, founder of Updraft RC (a Kentucky radio-control toy company), writes to Mr. Li at a Chinese contract manufacturer.
     - **(a) Ineffective draft** [p14]:
       - too informal ("cool", "having a look at")
       - a **demanding** tone
       - "here in the States" is too informal, and "**foreign**" is potentially insulting

- 🖍 **Kickback** = a type of bribery: someone is offered money or something of value **in exchange for providing something** (information, a discount, a favour) to a third party. Strictly prohibited.
- 🖍 **Facilitating payment** = a type of bribe used to **speed up routine, non-discretionary government action**. Prohibited. The only exception is an **imminent threat to health or safety**, which must be reported to Business Conduct immediately.

- 🖍 **Money laundering** = hiding illicit funds or making them **look legitimate**. **6 red flags**:
  1. attempts to make large cash payments
  2. payment by someone who is **not a party to the contract**
  3. requests to **pay more** than the contract says
  4. payment in a **currency the contract doesn't specify**
  5. payment from an unusual, **non-business** account
  6. **unusual patterns**, such as bulk purchases of products or gift cards, or repeated cash payments

**Communication lessons: how a business-conduct policy is organized and written** (my analysis of the sample; the document doesn't state these lessons)
1. **Purpose before rules.** It opens with a mission and values statement, so the rules that follow have a reason. [p3]
2. **A small, memorable core.** Four one-word principles, each with a dash definition, summarize the whole policy. [p3]
3. **Scope, duties and consequences stated early:** who is covered, training and certification, who can grant waivers, and "disciplinary action, up to and including termination". [p3–4]
4. **A modular, navigable layout:** a contents page with page numbers, 6 themed parts, short headed sections, tabs on every page and a dated footer, which also gives **version control**. [p2–20]
5. **A voice aimed at the reader:** "**you**" for duties, "**we/Apple**" for commitments, **imperatives** ("Speak up", "Never…", "Do not…"), plain language and bullets.
6. **Precise and testable.** Terms are **defined in the text** (confidential information, conflict of interest, gift, public official, bribe, kickback, facilitating payment, money laundering, material information, record), with **concrete limits** ($150, $10, 100K USD, every 3 years) and examples, so employees can check their own behaviour.
7. **Action-oriented.** Almost every section says **whom to contact** (manager, People Business Partner, Legal, Business Conduct, Corporate Communications) and offers **several reporting channels**, including an anonymous one.
8. **Layered documentation.** The main text stays short by **cross-referencing** detailed policies ("For more information, see…").
9. **Duties balanced with rights.** The no-retaliation section, the employee-rights section and the repeated notes protecting union and other "protected concerted" activity build trust. [p4–5, p9, p12, p19]
10. **Respectful framing of hard topics.** For example, "Respect does not mean that we don't disagree or debate…" [p6]

### 2.5 [7.10] What Is Gaslighting? Meaning and Examples (Western Australia Government web page, 5 pp.)

**Context.** The page is from the WA Government, Department of Communities, dated 10 Sep 2026. It is written for people in **relationships and family or domestic-violence** situations, so its emergency numbers are Australian (000). [p1]

- 🖍 ⭐ **Gaslighting** = a form of **emotional and psychological abuse** in which someone **deliberately** tries to **confuse you, manipulate you, or make you doubt yourself**. [p1]
- 🖍 **What it does** [p1]:
  - It makes you doubt yourself, your **sense of reality** and your **memories**.
  - It can involve **denial, twisting facts**, or making you feel you are **overreacting**.
  - It leaves you **confused, isolated and powerless**.
- When gaslighting is used as a **pattern of repeated behaviours**, it is a form of **coercive control**, which counts as **family and domestic violence**. [p1]
- **Statistics** [p1]: data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics show that **23% of women and 14% of men** have experienced **emotional abuse by a partner**.
- 🖍 **Where the name comes from** [p1]: **Patrick Hamilton's 1938 play *Gas Light***. A husband makes his wife lose trust in her own reality by **dimming the gas lights** and then **denying** the change.
- 🖍 ⭐ **Five gaslighting tactics** [p2]:
  1. **Countering**: denying your version of reality.
  2. **Diverting**: changing the subject to avoid responsibility.
  3. **Trivialising**: brushing off your concerns and feelings.
  4. **Withholding**: refusing to talk about your concerns.
  5. **Stereotyping**: using stereotypes about your identity to dismiss your concerns.
- 🖍 **What it can sound like (7 examples)** [p2]: "You're so sensitive." · "You're overreacting about this." · "You know I would never do that to you." · "You need to get some help." · "You can't trust your memory." · "You're exaggerating what actually happened." · "You're crazy."
- ⭐ **Signs that you are being gaslighted (10)** [p2–3]. You might:
  1. 🖍 second-guess your own thoughts and emotions
  2. 🖍 feel **dependent** on the abuser
  3. 🖍 feel you need to **apologise a lot**
  4. struggle to make decisions
  5. feel more sadness or anxiety than usual
  6. find yourself **making excuses** for the other person's poor behaviour toward you
  7. feel that nothing you do is good enough
  8. feel you have no independence
  9. feel **isolated** from friends and family
  10. have **low self-esteem**, because your thoughts, feelings and experiences are repeatedly denied
- **It develops gradually** [p3], so the signs can be hard to see until the effects are severe (e.g., more anxiety and depression).
- **It isn't only in intimate relationships** [p3]. It can happen **at work with colleagues**, with neighbours, family and friends, and even with **medical professionals**.
- **Why it is dangerous (4)** [p3]. It can:
  1. erode your confidence, self-worth and sense of reality
  2. take away your independence and plant doubt
  3. make you **depend on the abuser as your source of truth**
  4. isolate you from friends and family
- The page embeds a **video** (Damian Green, CEO of Stopping Family Violence) that isn't in the PDF. [p3]
- ⭐ **If it's happening to you**, remember you are "not alone" and "it is not your fault". **First steps (4)** [p4]:
  1. **Trust your instincts**: they are real, and they matter.
  2. **Talk to a trusted person** (a friend, family member or counsellor).
  3. **Contact a support service** (available 24/7).
  4. **Make a safety plan** that fits your situation.
- ⭐ **How to help someone else** [p4–5]. The behaviour can be subtle and easily excused as "just caring" or "just how they are". Offer support and **listen without judgement**. Ways to start the conversation (6):
  1. **Create a safe, private space**, where nobody else can overhear.
  2. **Ask how they felt** when the person said or did that thing.
  3. **Validate their feelings and believe them.**
  4. Encourage them to **keep a record somewhere secure**.
  5. **Avoid judging their choices.**
  6. Help them **explore support options**.

  Let them know you believe in them and stand by them. Being **brushed off** could decide whether they get help or stay in a harmful situation.
- **If you're worried that *you* are gaslighting someone** [p5]: support services exist, and **taking responsibility is the first step** toward change.

## 3. Key terms glossary (one line each)

**Teams and collaboration [7.5]**

### 4.1 Teams [7.5]

### Task 2: Spot the ineffective behaviours, then rewrite *(invented)*
> Subject: update
> Guys, the client thing got moved. Some of you obviously didn't read my last email, which is why the report draft was a total mess. Send me your parts soon. Meeting is Thursday, show up if you can be bothered. R.

**Model answer: the problems** (7.8.2's (a)–(h) checklist plus 7.5's feedback rules):
- **(a)** The subject line "update" is vague and says nothing about the meeting or the deadline.
- **(b)** "Guys," is too casual.
- **(c)** There's no background: which client event? Moved to when? Readers who missed it are lost.
- **(d)** The tone is **accusatory** ("obviously didn't read"), which puts readers on the defensive [7.8.2 (d)]. "A total mess" is **destructive feedback**: unspecific, with no guidance [7.5-p9]. Table 2.2 says be specific, discuss improvements, keep it impersonal.
- **(d)** The request isn't actionable: "your parts" and "soon" don't say **who does what by when**.
- **(e)** It gives the day but no **date, time or place**.
- **(f)** "If you can be bothered" guilt-trips people. There's no remote option and no invitation to ask questions beforehand.
- **(g)** There's no closing, so the tone is abrupt.
- **(h)** There's no signature or alternative contact.

**Model rewrite:**
> **Subject: Client review moved to Thu 29 Oct, 10:00 – report sections due Tue 27 Oct**
> Hi team,
> The client has moved our report review from Monday to **Thursday, 29 October, 10:00–11:00, Room B-305**.
> To make the combined draft consistent, please check your section against our agreed outline, especially the headings and figure numbering, and **email your revised section to me by Tuesday, 27 October, 18:00**. I'll merge the sections on Wednesday.
> If you can't attend in person, you can join online; I'll send the link on Wednesday. If you have questions before then, just message or call me.
> Thanks,
> R. [Full name] · Team lead · phone · email

### Task 3: Intercultural scenarios (A: from Exercise 3-17 [7.8.1-p22]; B: invented)
**A.** A manager in Dubai drafts a welcome email to new staff transferred from Jordan, Egypt and Lebanon, who understand English. It uses "ASAP", "our little family", "It is high time", "shook hands in person and not just across countries", "do my level best" and "see you well". What would you change, and is it audience-centred?

**Model answer:**
- **Problems:**
  - **Idioms** ("high time", "do my level best", shaking hands "across countries") don't travel well across languages. [7.8.1-p17]
  - "**ASAP**" and "our little family" are informal; "**see you well**" is unclear, and a word or phrase with several possible meanings complicates translation. [p15]
  - The sentences pack several ideas together; aim for **one idea per sentence**. [p16]
  - It gives no **concrete information** (what happens next, when, whom to contact).
- **Is it audience-centred?** Only in intent. It is written in the writer's own idiomatic style, not adapted to readers who use English as a second language. Write clearly and respect their style preferences. [p11–17]
- **Model rewrite:**
  > Dear colleagues,
  > Welcome to our department in Dubai. We are glad that you have joined us from Jordan, Egypt and Lebanon.
  > I look forward to meeting each of you in person at [date, time, room].
  > If you need help as you settle in, please contact me at [phone/email].
  > Kind regards, [Name, title]
- 🔎 The exercise hints at researching which style of English these readers are likely to speak. That isn't in the readings, so look it up in Track B.

**B.** Your firm will negotiate a supply contract with a manufacturer in **Japan** (a high-context culture in the reading's examples). Your manager wants the contract **signed at the first meeting** and plans slides full of idioms and jokes. What do you advise?

**Model answer:**
- **Expect relationship-building first.** High-context negotiators may spend most of their time building relationships, and they focus on the **means** of deciding, not only the results. [p5]
- **Time is more flexible**: the relationship can matter more than a deadline. [p6]
- In many Asian cultures a **contract may be seen as more fluid**, with renegotiation common. [p6]
- **Observe nonverbal norms**: greetings (a bow is more appropriate in Japan [7.5-p28]), formality, eye contact. [7.8.1-p7]
- **Cut the idioms and jokes.** Use short sentences, numerals and local date formats. [p15–17]
- **Speak slowly**, don't rephrase right away, ask for feedback, and double-check agreement at the end. [p19]
- Consider an **interpreter** for an important meeting, and confirm in **writing**. [p20]
- Treat them the way **they** want to be treated, and be **patient**. [p10]

### Task 4: Identify gaslighting *(invented, workplace)*
Nodira wrote the data-logging module for her team project. In the demo, Jasur presented it as his own work. Afterwards:
1. N: "You presented my module as yours." J: "That never happened. Your memory is terrible."
2. J: "You're overreacting. It's just a class project."
3. J: "Anyway, did you finish the test cases?"
4. J: "Typical. You new students always take things personally."
5. The next day: "I'm not discussing this anymore."
6. Weeks later, Nodira apologises for "making a fuss" and checks her commit history before trusting her own memory.

**Questions:** (a) Name the tactic in lines 1–5. (b) Which signs appear in line 6? (c) What can Nodira do? (d) How can a teammate help? (e) Which 7.5 tools apply to the underlying conflict?

**Model answer:**
- **(a) Tactics** [7.10-p2]:
  1. **Countering**: he denies her version of reality, using a typical phrase ("can't trust your memory").
  2. **Trivialising**: he brushes off her concern ("You're overreacting" is a listed phrase).
  3. **Diverting**: he changes the subject to avoid responsibility.
  4. **Stereotyping**: he uses her identity (a new student) to dismiss her concern.
  5. **Withholding**: he refuses to discuss her concern.
- **(b) Signs:** **apologising a lot** and **second-guessing her own thoughts and memory**. [7.10-p2] Watch also for isolation and low self-esteem, because it **builds gradually**. [p3]
- **Caution:** the definition says the abuser **deliberately** confuses or manipulates. A single disagreement isn't automatically gaslighting; look for the deliberate pattern. [7.10-p1]
- **(c) First steps for Nodira** [7.10-p4]:
  - **Trust her instincts**; the commit history supports them.
  - Talk to a **trusted person** (a friend, family member or counsellor).
  - Contact a **support service**.
  - Make a **safety plan**.
  - Keep a **record somewhere secure** (this advice appears in the "help someone" list).
- **(d) A teammate can** [7.10-p4]:
  - create a safe, private space
  - ask how she felt
  - **validate and believe** her
  - encourage her to keep a record
  - avoid judging her choices
  - help her explore support options
- **(e) 7.5 tools for the conflict:**
  - It mirrors the Ethics Detective case "How Did 'We' Turn into 'I'?" [7.5-p3].
  - Use the **5 conflict steps** (examine yourself, find the true origin, establish common ground, choose a strategy) with **calm, respectful** communication [7.5-p6].
  - Give **constructive, impersonal** feedback [7.5-p9].
  - Formal policies offer **speak-up and no-retaliation** channels, as in the Apple sample [7.2-p4].

### Task 5: Team conflict, Tuckman stage and collaborative writing *(invented)*
It is week 3 of a 4-person report project, due in 10 days.
- Aziz wants to write every paragraph together on video calls; Lola wants to split the sections. They argue at every meeting.
- Sardor stays silent and agrees with whoever spoke last.
- Kamila quietly rewrites everyone's paragraphs in her own style.

**Model answer:**
- **Stage: storming.** Disagreements arise as members assert their roles. This conflict is becoming destructive (it diverts energy from the deadline), so resolve it actively. [7.5-p4–5]
- **Risks:**
  - Sardor shows **groupthink** behaviour: he withholds his own opinions. [p3]
  - The team lacks **psychological safety**. [p4]
  - Someone needs a **team-maintenance** role to make sure everyone is heard. [p4]
- **Apply the 5 steps** [p6]:
  1. It is worth addressing, because the deadline is at risk.
  2. Each person checks their own behaviour.
  3. The true origin may be control over quality vs efficiency.
  4. Common ground: everyone wants a good report on time.
  5. Strategy: **collaborate** (win-win).
- **The collaborative plan** [p8]:
  - **Plan, research and outline together** on one call.
  - **Split the writing** using a **detailed outline**.
  - Have **one person do the final revision pass** for a consistent style.
  - Agree on goals, map out the work, play to strengths, set **frequent checkpoints**, and accept different styles.
- **Kamila:** clarify roles and expectations up front (who may rewrite and who only comments) [p7]. Use **comments and change tracking**, so each writer accepts or rejects edits [p8–9]. "If something works, support it" [p8]. Phrase all feedback the Table 2.2 way [p9].
- **Virtual-team habits:** give each tool one purpose, and don't rely only on chat; talk by video. [p7]

### Task 6: Choose the listening style (from Exercise 2-13 [7.5-p32])
You manage a software lab that has added dozens of new employees. At lunch, a senior developer vents about having to do his own work while mentoring "a bunch of newbies". Which listening style or styles do you use?

**Model answer:**
- **Start with empathic listening.** He is venting, so understand his feelings and needs without judging. Being heard may matter more than the information. [7.5-p22]
- **Switch to content listening** to collect the facts (how many new hires, how much time mentoring takes, which of his tasks are blocked), asking clarifying questions without challenging him. [p21]
- **Use critical listening later**, when you evaluate his claims or proposals (evidence, implications, omissions). [p21]
- **Throughout**, be open and positive, stay engaged, **respect silence**, notice nonverbal cues, don't interrupt, and paraphrase. [p22–23]
- **Avoid defensive listening**: as his manager, don't tune out criticism of the hiring plan. [p21]
- **Notes:** taking notes may be inappropriate in a personal conversation at lunch [p23]. Record the key facts afterwards instead of relying on memory.

### Task 8: Apply the conduct policy (Apple sample [7.2]), then rewrite a rule
**Classify each case:**
1. A customs officer offers to clear your shipment faster for a small cash fee. → a **Facilitating payment**, which is a bribe and prohibited. The only exception is an imminent threat to health or safety, which you must report to Business Conduct immediately. [p17]
2. A supplier offers you $300 concert tickets unrelated to your job, and your organization uses the $150 rule. → **Over the limit** and not a business-related event, so **decline or return** them. If that's impossible, notify your manager and People Business Partner, then contact Business Conduct. [p14–15]
3. A vendor gives you a conference ticket that is **free to all attendees** (you're not in Operations or Apple Store). → **Permitted**, as a key exception. [p15]
4. A customer wants to pay in a currency the contract doesn't specify, from a personal account, in repeated cash instalments. → **Money-laundering red flags**, so report it. [p18]
5. A reseller offers you a "commission" for passing on Apple's unreleased pricing. → A **kickback** (value given in exchange for information passed to a third party) [p17], and also a breach of **confidential information** rules [p7].
6. Your cousin's company bids for a contract you influence. → A **conflict of interest** (significant personal relationship). Don't conduct that business; **disclose** it to your manager and People Business Partner. [p11]
7. A journalist asks you to comment on a new Apple product. → **Refer** the inquiry to Corporate Communications or Investor Relations; you don't comment on Apple's behalf yourself. [p9]

**Rewrite this vague rule in the policy's style:** "Staff should be careful about accepting gifts from suppliers."

**Model:**
> "**Do not** accept gifts worth more than **$150** from current or potential suppliers. **Never** accept cash or gift cards. If you cannot refuse or return a gift, **tell your manager** immediately and **contact Business Conduct**."

It uses an imperative, a concrete threshold and a named contact (lessons 5–7 in §2.4).
