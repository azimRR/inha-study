## 1. Big picture

- **Week 4 = what you may NOT publish.** The media-law slides (U.S. law) cover defamation (slander = spoken, libel = published), the four things a plaintiff must prove, the four categories of damaging statements, the three traditional defenses (truth, "the best defense", plus privilege and fair comment), the actual-malice standard, invasion of privacy (four risky situations, two defenses, three exceptions), copyright and fair use, and political correctness. Ken Paulson's four self-check questions sum it up: did I report fully, factually, fairly, in good faith?
- **Week 5 = what counts as news, and in what order to tell it.** News is judged by Relevance, Usefulness and Interest. A good story has some of eight elements: Impact, Conflict, Novelty, Prominence, Proximity, Timeliness, Engagement, Solutions [5.1]. The inverted pyramid puts information in descending order of importance, opened by a one-sentence lead of 30–35 words maximum [5.2].
- **Week 6.1 = the menu of NEWS leads (8 types):** summary, partial summary, multiple element/roundup, localized, follow-up, immediate identification, delayed identification, feature.
- **Week 6.2 = FEATURE openings:** a lead has four jobs (attract attention, introduce the topic, set the tone, establish the point of view). The lead and the ending are "bookends". A nutgraf (billboard paragraph) goes right after a creative lead. Six feature leads: anecdote, scenario, shock, blind, direct/indirect quote, direct address.
- **Weeks 6.3–6.4 = before you write: the idea and the angle.** Test an idea with 10 questions (you need "yes" to the majority). Narrow a broad topic with the angle tree: four possible angles, then one main focus plus one sidebar. A focused angle has unity, action and concreteness.
- **Week 6.5 = a model story** that combines all of this: a follow-up update on top, a descriptive lead, a nutgraf, attributed expert quotes, conflict and novelty, and a reader-service ending.
- **How it connects** *(My note)*: news values decide **what** goes in the lead. The angle decides the **focus**. The lead type decides **how** you open. The inverted pyramid decides the **order**. The 5.2 checklist polishes the **style**. Media law decides what you must **not** publish.
- **Exam routine** *(My note)*: fact sheet → circle the strongest news values → choose the angle → choose a lead type and write it (one sentence, 35 words or fewer, past tense, attributed) → order the remaining facts by importance → run the 5.2 checklist → run Paulson's four questions.

#### 2.1 Media Law [ML-p1–p24]

> **Scope note:** the slides describe **U.S. law**. They mention "most states", "US courts", "the Supreme Court" and "the president of the U.S." 🔎 Look up in Track B: how Uzbekistan's law treats defamation, privacy and copyright (not covered in class).

**A. Why defamation law exists [ML-p2]**
- Defamation law balances two competing interests:
  - nobody should be able to ruin someone's life by telling lies about him or her;
  - people should be able to speak freely without fear of being sued over every insult, disagreement or mistake.

**B. Definitions [ML-p3–p4]**
- **Defamation**, quoted from an "American Dictionary": "the action of damaging the good reputation of someone; slander or libel." [ML-p3]
- ⭐ **Slander** (noun) = the action of making a false **spoken statement** that damages a person's reputation. Verb: to make false and damaging statements about someone. [ML-p4, bold on the slide]
- ⭐ **Libel** (noun) = a **published false statement** that damages a person's reputation; "a written defamation". Verb: to defame someone by publishing a libel. [ML-p4, bold on the slide]
- *My note, memory hook:* **S**lander = **S**poken. Libel = published (in print or writing). A journalist's main risk is libel, because journalists publish.

**C. Defamation in action [ML-p5]** ⭐ (the three key terms are bold)
- In most (U.S.) states defamation is **not a crime**. It is a **"tort"**: a civil wrong, rather than a criminal wrong.
- The person who has been defamed (the **"plaintiff"**) can sue the person who did the defaming (the **"defendant"**) for damages.
- *My note:* so a defamation case is one person suing another (civil), not the state prosecuting a crime.

**D. ⭐ What a plaintiff must show [ML-p6]** (the four key words are bold)

A plaintiff suing for defamation typically must show:

| # | Element | What the slide says |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Published** | The defendant published a statement about the plaintiff. |
| 2 | **False** | It must be *objectively* false; "saying one's opinion does not count". |
| 3 | **Injurious** | It caused harm, e.g. the person was fired, lost customers, was shunned by friends, or it caused online harassment. |
| 4 | **Unprivileged** | The statement was not protected by a privilege. |

- *My note:* mnemonic **P-F-I-U** (Published, False, Injurious, Unprivileged). The plaintiff typically needs all four.
- *My note:* element 2 explains why a clearly labelled opinion is safer than a false statement of fact, and why the "fair comment" defense (G3 below) exists.

**E. Libel and press freedom [ML-p7]**
- Traditionally, most of the laws that limit "the absolute principle of freedom of the press" have dealt with libel.
- These laws come from the wish of legislatures and courts to help individuals **protect their reputations**.

**F. ⭐ Four categories of damaging statements [ML-p8]** ("four" is bold on the slide)

U.S. courts use four categories to help jurors decide whether someone's reputation was damaged by bringing him or her into "hatred, contempt or ridicule":
1. accusing someone of a **crime**;
2. damaging a person in his or her **public office, profession or occupation**;
3. accusing a person of **serious immorality**;
4. accusing someone of having a **loathsome (that is, contagious) disease**.

- *My note:* when you write about a named person, treat these four as red flags: crime, job, morals, disease.

**G. ⭐ Three traditional defenses against libel [ML-p9]:** **truth**, **privilege**, **fair comment and criticism**.

**G1. Truth [ML-p9–p10]** ⭐ (said on two slides)
- "Truth is the best defense against libel."
- In libel cases involving **matters of public concern**, the **burden of proof is on the plaintiff**.
- *My note:* the person who sues has to do the proving, not the reporter.

**G2. Privilege [ML-p11–p12]**
- This defense applies when a reporter is covering any of the **three branches of government**. [p11]
- **Absolute privilege** for officials: the courts allow legislators, judges and government executives to say anything, true or false, when acting in their **official capacities**. The reason: the public interest is served when an official can speak "freely and fearlessly" about making laws, carrying them out, or punishing those who disobey them. [p11]
- Likewise, a participant in a **judicial proceeding** (an attorney, court clerk or judge) is absolutely privileged to make false and even defamatory statements about another person during that proceeding. [p11]
- **Privilege as a reporter = qualified privilege**, also called **neutral reporting** or **conditional privilege**. It exists at the federal level and in some states, and it covers reporting what public officials say. [p12]
  - The condition: your report must give **full, fair and accurate** coverage of the court session, the legislative session or the president's press conference, even if one of the participants made defamatory statements. [p12]
  - You can quote anything the president of the U.S. says without fear of losing a libel suit, even when the president is not acting in an official capacity. Reporters have a qualified privilege to report unofficial statements. [p12]
- *My note:* officials have **absolute** privilege (protected for whatever they say in the official role). Reporters have only a **qualified, conditional** privilege: protected only if the report is full, fair and accurate. A distorted or one-sided report of what an official said fails that condition.

**G3. Fair comment and criticism [ML-p13–p14]**
- **Fair comment** = a privilege that lets the media publish **opinions** about **public officials and public figures** without fear of a libel suit. It also lets the general public share their opinions about these people. [p13]
- The courts have protected writers who comment on and criticize the **"public offerings"** of anyone in the public eye: actors and actresses, sports figures, public officials and other newsworthy people. [p14]
- You are protected as long as you (1) do **not misstate any of the facts** your comment is based on, and (2) do **not wrongly imply** that you have undisclosed, damaging information behind your opinion. [p14]
- *My note:* "The singer's new album is lazy and repetitive" is an opinion about a public offering, so fair comment can protect it. "The singer stole the songs" is a factual accusation of a crime, so it is not fair comment and needs the truth defense.

**H. Two other constitutional standards [ML-p15]** (under the same heading, "How to Defend oneself from Libel Suit")
- **Actual malice.** The Supreme Court's definition: "actual knowledge that the statement is false or reckless disregard for the truth."
- **Negligence tests.** Named only, not explained.
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: what the "negligence tests" are, and which plaintiffs (public officials and figures, or private people) must prove actual malice. The slide doesn't say.
- ⚠️ The slide says "the actual malice" (stray "the").

**I. ⭐ The keys to avoiding libel, by Ken Paulson [ML-p16]** (its own slide, with his photo)

Paulson suggests journalists ask themselves:
- Have I reported **fully**?
- Have I reported **factually**?
- Have I reported **fairly**?
- Have I reported **in good faith**?

*My note:* four F-sounds: Fully, Factually, Fairly, in good Faith. Use them as the last check on every story you write.

**J. Invasion of privacy [ML-p17–p18]**
- Definition: "a violation of a person's right to be left alone." [p17]
- As a reporter you risk an invasion-of-privacy suit if you:
  1. **physically intrude** into a private area to get a story or picture, "an act closely related to trespass"; [p17]
  2. ⭐ publish a story or photograph about someone that is **misleading**, and so portray the person in a **"false light"**; [p17, bold]
  3. ⭐ disclose something about a person's private affairs that is **true** but also **offensive** to a person of ordinary sensibilities; [p17, bold]
  4. use someone's **name or picture in an advertisement** or for similar purposes of trade. [p18]

**K. Defenses against an invasion-of-privacy suit, and the three exceptions [ML-p19]**
- **Consent** is a basic defense. But make sure your use of the material does **not exceed the consent given**.
- **Newsworthiness** is the other basic defense: you are a reporter covering a newsworthy situation. The courts usually protect the press when it reports matters of **legitimate public interest**.
- **But there are three exceptions**, where newsworthiness will not protect you: **trespassing**, portraying someone in a **"false light"**, and **causing unwanted publicity**.
- *My note:* the three exceptions match the first three situations in J: physical intrusion = trespassing; misleading = false light; true-but-offensive private facts = unwanted publicity.

**L. Trespassing [ML-p20]**
- The first exception: entering private property **without permission** to get a story. You cannot trespass to get a story or take a picture, **even if it is newsworthy**. The courts will not protect a trespasser.
- **Case example (Life magazine):** two Life staffers **posed as patients** to get into a man's home for a story about a **faith healer**. They lost the invasion-of-privacy suit, even though they were working with the district attorney and the state board of health.
- The rule: enter private property **only when invited by the owner or renter**, and never use **deception** to get into property you otherwise have no legal right to enter.

**M. False light [ML-p21]**
- The courts will not protect you if you invade someone's privacy by publishing **misleading** information about that person.
- **Example:** a photo or information from a true story about a **careful pedestrian** hit by a **careless driver** is used again with a story about **careless pedestrians**. The pedestrian who was hit could sue for libel, for "false light" invasion of privacy, or for both in some states.
- *My note:* every single fact can be true, yet the new context misleads. That is false light.
- ⚠️ The slide title has no question mark ("What is Portraying in a 'False Light'"), unlike the other "What is…?" titles.

**N. Copyright and fair use [ML-p22]**
- Copyrightable works are protected **from the moment they are fixed in tangible form**, whether published or unpublished.
- Protection begins at the work's creation and lasts for **the life of the author plus 70 years** after the author's death (quoted on the slide).
- **Works for hire, and anonymous and pseudonymous works:** protected for **95 years from publication or 100 years from creation, whichever is shorter**.
- **Fair use** is a limitation on the copyright owner's exclusive rights: "it may be permissible to quote **small excerpts** from a copyrighted work without permission."
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: (1) check the "100 years from creation" figure against the current statute; (2) the factors courts use to decide fair use (the slide gives none).
- *My note:* for the exam, a short quote with credit fits the fair-use idea on the slide. Copying a whole article or chapter goes far beyond "small excerpts".

**O. Political correctness [ML-p23]**
- The concept rests on the belief that speech or behavior that is **offensive to various groups' sensibilities** should be eliminated, by means of regulations or penalties if necessary. (The source on [ML-p24] is Hudson, *First Amendment Encyclopedia*, 2017.)
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: how this applies to word choice in news writing. The slide gives only the definition.
- ⚠️ The title is all lower-case ("political correctness"), unlike the other titles.

#### 2.2 Nature of News [5.1-p1–p10]

Slide map: p1 title · p2 discussion question · p3 what news is · p4 elements of a good news story · p5 impact · p6 conflict · p7 novelty · p8 prominence · p9 proximity · p10 "Thank you!". The source line on p4–p9 is "Textbook on News Reporting and Writing by The Missouri Group".

**A. Opening question [5.1-p2]**
- "What is the similarity between a geologist and journalist?" The slide shows a geologist studying a rock sample and a reporter holding a microphone. **It gives no answer.**
- *My note (my interpretation, not on the slide):* both examine a lot of raw material and judge which pieces are valuable. The journalist "tests" events against the news criteria on the next slides.

**B. ⭐ What news is [5.1-p3]** (capitalized heading "WHAT NEWS IS")
- The criteria professional reporters and editors use to decide what news is can be summarized in **three words**: **Relevance**, **Usefulness**, **Interest**.

**C. ⭐ Elements of a good news story [5.1-p4]**
- Reporters look for more specific elements in each potential story. "The most important elements are these":

  **Impact · Conflict · Novelty · Prominence · Proximity · Timeliness · Engagement · Solutions**

- Only the first five get their own slides (p5–p9).
- ⚠️ **Timeliness, Engagement and Solutions are listed but never explained** in the deck. 🔎 Look up in Track B: what these three elements mean.

**D. Impact [5.1-p5]**
- The potential impact of a story is "another way of measuring its **relevance and usefulness**".
- Questions to ask: **How many** people are affected by the event or idea? **How seriously** does it affect them?
- "The wider and heavier the impact, the better the story."
- But impact is sometimes **not immediately obvious**, and sometimes **not very exciting**. The challenge for good journalism is making such **"dull but important"** stories lively and interesting. "That may require relying on **the next three elements**."
- *My note:* "the next three elements" are conflict, novelty and prominence (the next three on the list). So when an important story is dull, look for the conflict in it, the unusual detail, or the well-known name.

**E. Conflict [5.1-p6]**
- A recurring theme in **all storytelling**: journalism, literature or drama.
- Struggles **between people, among nations, or with natural forces** make fascinating reading and viewing.
- **Warning:** conflict is such a basic element of life that journalists must **resist the temptation to overdramatize or oversimplify** it.

**F. Novelty [5.1-p7]**
- Another element shared by journalism and other kinds of stories.
- People or events may be interesting, and therefore newsworthy, just because they are **unusual or bizarre**.

**G. Prominence [5.1-p8]**
- "**Names make news.** The bigger the name, the bigger the news."
- Ordinary people have always been intrigued by the doings of the rich and famous.
- **Warning:** prominence and novelty can be, and often are, **exaggerated** to produce "news" that lacks real relevance and usefulness.

**H. Proximity [5.1-p9]**
- People are generally more interested in and concerned about what happens **close to home**.
- When they read or hear national or international news, they often want to know **how it relates to their own community**.
- Journalists and scholars now recognize that **communities organized around a particular interest** (a sport, a hobby or an issue) are at least as important as geographic communities.

#### 2.3 Inverted Pyramid Checklist [5.2]

A one-page worksheet, "Inverted Pyramid Checklist", credited to jprof.com. Its stated use: "When you write an inverted pyramid news story, use the following checklist to make sure that you have done it correctly." The headings highlighted in orange on the sheet are marked ⭐ here.

**A. ⭐ The core rule:** "Information should be presented in **descending order of importance**."
- *My note:* the most important information comes first, and each later paragraph matters less than the one before. In [6.2-p4] the textbook makes the reader-side point: after a good summary lead, readers can stop and still have the essence.

**B. ⭐ Leads**
- **One sentence.**
- **30–35 words maximum.**
- The lead tells **the most important information** in the story and gives **specific facts**.
- *My note:* the class example leads fit this rule. By my count (dateline left out): DOVER crash lead 34 words, earthquake lead 35, Bush–Kwasniewski lead 33, Ohio roundup lead 26 [6.1].

**C. ⭐ Second paragraph**
- **Expand or develop** some idea introduced in the lead.
- Should **not drop the story into a chronological narrative**.
- *My note:* don't follow the lead with "It all started at 9 a.m. when…". Explain or support the lead's main point instead.

**D. ⭐ Attribution**
- **All major information should be attributed**, unless it is commonly known or the information itself strongly implies the source.
- **Don't dump a string of direct quotations** on the reader.
- Direct quotations should be **no more than two sentences** long.
- Punctuate direct quotations and their attribution properly. The class model:
  > "John did not go with her," he said.
- The elements of a direct quotation come in this order, as in the model: **direct quote, speaker, verb**.

**E. ⭐ AP style**
- "**Always.** Check numbers, dates, locations, titles, etc."
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: the AP Stylebook rules for numbers, dates, titles, locations and datelines. The class only says to use AP style, and [6.1-p5] says to "look up dateline in the AP Stylebook".

**F. Check the following** (grammar)
- Pronoun–antecedent agreement.
- It, its, it's.
- Avoid "it is…", "there is…" and "there are…" structures: "They are passive and vague."
- Use the **past tense**, not the present.
- No comma splices or run-on sentences. The sheet's examples of the error, both "grammatically incorrect":
  - "He picked up the ball, he ran down the field." (comma splice)
  - "Sally does not know where he is he is not here." (run-on)
  - *My note, fixed versions:* "He picked up the ball and ran down the field." / "Sally does not know where he is. He is not here."
- **Plurals:** don't make them with an "apostrophe s" (*My note:* "two students", not "two student's").

**G. ⭐ Short paragraphs**
- Any paragraph of **more than three sentences** is **definitely too long**.
- Any paragraph of **three sentences** is **probably too long**.
- *My note:* so aim for one or two sentences per paragraph.

**H. Wordiness**
- Check for too much verbiage, redundancies and unnecessary repetition.

**I. ⭐ Name, title**
- When the **title comes before the name**, do **not** separate them with commas. The sheet's WRONG example: "Game warden, Brad Fisher, arrested the trespassers." (*My note, corrected:* "Game warden Brad Fisher arrested the trespassers.")
- When the **name comes before the title**, set the title off **with commas**: "Brad Fisher, the game warden, arrested the trespassers."

**J. ⭐ Transitions**

#### 2.4 Types of Leads [6.1-p1–p43]

Deck facts: header "Inha University in Tashkent | EC | Instructor Khasan Khankeldiyev"; footer "Source: Professor Annette Taylor, University of Dayton, 2002". Topics [6.1-p2]: Summary · Partial Summary · Multiple Element & Roundup · Localized · Follow up · Immediate Identification · Delayed Identification · Feature.

**1. Summary lead [6.1-p3–p8]**
- Tells **the most important information** about a situation or event: **the results, or the climax**, of the story.
- Answers **Who? What? Where? When?** and usually tries to answer **How? Why?**
- It is the equivalent of a **thesis** in a scholarly paper, except that in news it appears as **the first sentence** of the story.
- It does **not** include every minute detail. The details are saved for the body of the story.
- Examples:
  - [p5] "DOVER – A speeding car passed a minivan on a state highway and slammed into a car traveling in the other direction near this eastern Ohio town, killing four people, the Ohio Highway Patrol said Friday." (Associated Press, July 6, 2002)
    - The slide's own breakdown. Readers know: (1) **what** happened: a crash killed four people; (2) **how**: crossed the centerline; (3) **where**: the dateline, Dover; (4) **who**: four people (unnamed, see delayed identification); (5) **who said**: the Ohio Highway Patrol; (6) **when**: Friday; (7) **why**: "speed and bad judgment perhaps", though it is not yet known why the driver took the risk to pass.
  - [p6] "Southern Californians from the desert to the beach were unnerved but mostly unharmed Wednesday by a 5.5-magnitude earthquake that shattered windows, triggered rockslides and caused millions of dollars in damage in Pomona and surrounding communities."
  - [p7] "It wasn't as sweet as modern hot chocolate, but the Mayans were drinking cocoa 2,100 years before Columbus landed in the New World, or about 1,000 years earlier than previously thought, researchers say." (Associated Press, July 17, 2002)
  - [p8] "Identity theft has become a national crisis, with more Americans than ever losing personal information to scam artists, Congress was told Tuesday."
- *My note:* the DOVER breakdown is a model for checking any lead. Tick off what, how, where, who, who said, when and why. If the "why" isn't known, say so or leave it out; don't invent it.
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: "dateline". The slide itself says to look it up in the AP Stylebook.
- ⚠️ [p4] "Who? What? Where? When" has no question mark after "When".

**2. Partial summary lead [6.1-p9–p13]**
- ⭐ "often used in **feature stories and news stories emphasizing a particular angle**" (orange). It **omits a few details**.
- Why leave details out?
  - In some cases, including all the major points would make the lead **crowded and/or confusing**.
  - In other cases, the writer wants to create **suspense**.
- The writer sets a **tone** through word choice, which gives readers another clue to what the story is about: something serious? a murder? something lighthearted? "Another stupid criminal does something stupid?"
- **Pitfalls [p11]:**
  - the significance of the story to the reader may be **vague**;
  - readers may be **confused** about the subject of the story;
  - there may not be enough **precise information** to hook the reader.
- Examples:
  - [p12] "Oprah Winfrey is keeping Miami Valley Hospital busy."
    - The slide's analysis: readers know that the famous talk show host has some connection to Dayton. What is it? Readers will have to read more. Interest is raised by **Oprah's prominence**, the **hospital's local prominence**, and the **unusualness** of Oprah having anything to do with Dayton.
  - [p13] "The jump from high school to college can be a tough transition for any teenager, but for students with learning disabilities, the hurdles are even higher." (Chicago Tribune, July 14, 2002)
- *My note:* a partial summary leaves a deliberate gap. It only works if what you *do* give is precise enough to hook the reader (see the pitfalls).

**3. Multiple element and roundup leads [6.1-p14–p20]**
- ⭐ They are essentially **summary leads** that include a lot of information, often about **separate incidents or distinct aspects linked by time, location, type or cause** (orange).
- They summarize and set up the important information to follow. Most complex, in-depth stories about big issues have multiple elements.
- **Pitfall:** the lead may become **crowded or disorganized**.
- How to write one [p16]:
  1. The best approach: identify **what the elements have in common** and write a **main clause** reflecting that point.
  2. Then **group** the other aspects, identify them by key words or phrases, **prioritize** the more important ones, and turn them into **subordinate phrases**.
  3. Think of the lead as the **table of contents** for the rest of the story.
  - ⭐ Another approach: **prioritize** (orange) the elements. Deal with the most important one in a summary lead, then write a **second paragraph that summarizes the other points**.
- Examples:
  - [p17] "State police reported at least 10 people died in Ohio traffic accidents over the weekend, including three who died in an accident Friday in Greene County." (Dayton Daily News, July 2002)
    - The slide's comment: the writer **grouped** all the weekend deaths into one story, then **added a localized element**.
  - [p18] "Thousands of workers are leaving large cities that border Indiana to settle in Indianapolis, attracted by cheaper housing and shorter, less-congested commutes than in Chicago, Cincinnati and Louisville." (Associated Press, July 6, 2002)
- ⭐ **Summary lead vs multiple element lead [p19–p20]:**
  - Summary leads are used when the **whole** is more important than any one part.
  - Multiple element leads are used when **two or more actions are of equal importance**. (Both phrases are orange.)
- *My note:* in the Ohio example, the common point (at least 10 weekend traffic deaths) is the main clause, and the Greene County crash is the subordinate "including…" phrase.

**4. Localized lead [6.1-p21–p25]**
- A **summary or partial summary lead** that emphasizes the **local aspect** of a national, regional or even international event or situation.
- The lead and a significant part of the story's body emphasize what's important to **your specific readers**: what's close to home.
- "**Proximity** is an important news value."
- Localizing a story is **itself a story angle**. It's just one of the many ways to approach any event or situation.
- Examples (written for Dayton-area readers):
  - [p23] "More than 40 people, including a woman from Dayton, were killed in an air show crash Tuesday in southwest Kentucky."
  - [p24] "An Oakwood attorney was killed and his wife injured in a plane crash that killed two other people near Green castle on Friday, authorities said." ⚠️ "Green castle" is probably the one-word place name "Greencastle".
  - [p25] "Dayton is one of 50 locations in the United States to provide residents with a new technology in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) systems." (Dayton Daily News, July 8, 2002)
- *My note:* the local element ("a woman from Dayton", "an Oakwood attorney", "Dayton") goes up front. For Tashkent readers you would localize to Tashkent, Uzbekistan or Inha.

**5. Follow-up lead [6.1-p26–p29]**
- Like the lead of any news story, it emphasizes **the latest and most important information** about an **earlier** event or situation.
- "**Most stories are follow ups.**"
- Most follow-up leads include some **background, in a word or phrase**, so readers can recall the earlier situation.
- Examples:
  - [p28] "Stolen school computer equipment worth thousands of dollars was recovered from five Fairborn High School Students, police said Tuesday." (Dayton Daily News, July 3, 2002) ⚠️ "Students" is wrongly capitalized.
  - [p29] "A 6-year-old boy taken from a train station platform Monday was found unharmed about two hours later, and a suspect was facing kidnapping charges." (Dayton Daily News, July 3, 2002)
- *My note:* the background words are "Stolen" (it recalls the theft) and "taken from a train station platform Monday" (it recalls the abduction). The new information is "recovered" and "found unharmed… facing kidnapping charges".

**6. Immediate-identification lead [6.1-p30–p33]**
- The **who** is identified **by name**.
- Identifying people in the lead is usually **reserved for prominent people**.
- Sometimes **little-known people** are named in the lead when the emphasis really is on **what happened to this person**.
- Examples:
  - [p32] "President Bush and Polish President Aleksander Kwasniewski agreed during a state visit Wednesday to continue the fight against threats to freedom and to boost military and economic cooperation between the two NATO allies." (New York Times, July 17, 2002)
  - [p33] "Florida Gov. Jeb Bush's 24-year-old daughter Noelle was jailed Wednesday for not meeting the conditions of a court-ordered drug treatment plan." (Associated Press, July 18, 2002) ⚠️ Only this example starts with a stray "EX:".
- *My note:* the second example borrows prominence: the daughter is identified through her famous father.

**7. Delayed-identification lead [6.1-p34–p38]**
- The **who is not named** but is **described** in some way: by **title, age, residence or past notoriety**.
- Why delay the name?
  - The person is **not well known**, so the name has little meaning to readers.
  - It puts the emphasis on **the what** of the story, which is more important.
- Examples (*My note:* the bracket says how the person is described):
  - [p36] "A 10-year-old Grand Ledge boy was seriously injured Saturday when he ran in front of a pickup truck on Center Street and was struck by the vehicle." [age + residence]
  - [p37] "A Troy accountant went on trial Monday in Montgomery County Common Pleas Court for failing to repay $1.6 million to investors." [residence + title/job]
  - [p38] "A fired construction worker suspected of ambushing and shooting his former boss in an Irvine parking garage surrendered at his Riverside County home Wednesday night after a three-hour standoff with police." (Associated Press, July 2002) [job + what he is suspected of]
- *My note:* the name comes later in the story; that is what "delayed" means. The DOVER summary lead's "four people" uses the same move, as the slide itself points out [p5].

**8. Feature lead [6.1-p39–p42]**
- "Basically, leads with **flair** are feature leads."
- They come in various formats, including the **anecdote, description, staccato, zinger, flashback, direct address and representative example**.
- They might essentially be considered **partial summaries** that emphasize the **novelty** of the situation or person.
- They give some **precise information** and then suggest the story's **theme, tone and genre** through **language, rhythm and style**.
- Examples:
  - [p41] "In preparation for tonight's poetry reading, Charles Bukowski is out in the parking lot, vomiting."
  - [p42] "The jury had left. / The sentence had been read. / Jerry Scott Heidler's face was still as stone." It continues with one long sentence: only when the judge actually sentenced Heidler to death for the murder of the Daniels family did Heidler break down for the first time and cry. (Leonora Bohen LaPeter, Savannah Morning News, Sept. 4, 1999)
- *My note:* the Bukowski lead is pure novelty: a surprising contrast (a poet "preparing" for a reading by vomiting), in the present tense. The Heidler lead shows rhythm: three very short sentences build tension, then one long sentence releases it. That is "rhythm and style" in action.

#### 2.5 Where to Begin: The First 100 Words [6.2-p1–p9]

Chapter 8 of Sumner and Miller (2013), book pp. 109–117. The instructor colour-highlighted it in green, yellow and blue; everything highlighted is marked ⭐.

**B. ⭐ Why the opening matters [p1]** (green)
- If the opening paragraphs of a feature story don't grab readers' interest, the content of the article, **regardless of its importance**, may remain **forever unread**.

**C. ⭐ The four jobs of an introduction [p1]** (yellow)
- **Attract attention · introduce the topic · set the tone · establish the point of view.**
- The best leads occupy **limited space**. The opening sentence of a newspaper feature may be as short as **12 to 15 words**. A magazine feature's opening sentence may double or even triple that, depending on the audience.
- ⭐ (yellow) The textbook's instruction for its two examples: "ask yourself whether they accomplish the four tasks of effective leads."

**D. Two model leads tested against the four jobs [p1–p2]**
- Example 1 (*In Trust* magazine, for the governing boards of seminaries): of about 600 students at New York Theological Seminary, only 15 are residential, "but they are *extremely* residential". They are 15 men from New York prisons who take a master's class inside Sing Sing Correctional Facility.
- Example 2 (*The Wall Street Journal* opinion page): the writer opens in the first person with a TSA agent's wordplay joke about Steve Jobs, Bob Hope and Johnny Cash ("outta jobs, outta hope and outta cash"), a comment on the stalled economy.
- ⭐ (green) The analysis: both introduce **serious topics** (educating prisoners; the stalled economy). Both use a **humorous tone** to lighten their heavy topics. Both quickly establish **point of view**: story one in the **third person**, story two in the **first person**.
- Both opening sentences have **fewer than 25 words**. Neither writer "dumbs down" her vocabulary, because both publications serve well-educated, knowledgeable readers. College students might not find either lead engaging, but both **work for their intended audiences**.

**E. ⭐ Coming to terms with leads [p2–p3]** (green heading)
- ⭐ Organizing a feature is like solving a **complex jigsaw puzzle**: you have gathered disjointed bits and pieces of information and must assemble them into a seamless "picture" for readers. ⭐ You must also decide which piece to set aside as **the perfect wrap-up**.
- ⭐ Many journalists agree that **the most difficult part** of writing articles is creating **attractive leads and satisfying endings**.
- ⭐ **The airplane comparison:** feature articles, like airplanes, are most vulnerable to crashing during **takeoff and landing**.
  - ⭐ An article can contain great information, but if the opening paragraph (the "takeoff") doesn't grab and hold readers' interest, they may turn the page.
  - ⭐ The ending (the "landing") should be **smooth rather than abrupt, fast-paced rather than prolonged**.
- ⭐ "Like pilots who know their destinations before taking off, writers should **identify their endings before beginning** their articles." Otherwise they risk getting lost, taking detours or wandering in circles.

**F. Three names for the opening [p3]**
- **Lead**: an effective first paragraph *leads* readers into the story.
- **Hook**: a strong opening paragraph *hooks* readers' attention and nudges them to continue.
- **Introduction**: the first paragraphs *introduce* the topic and set the tone and point of view.
  - If the writer tells the story from his own point of view (a personal essay, say), the word "**I**" will probably appear in the lead.
  - If the tone will be **conversational** rather than academic, that shows from the first sentence.

**G. The writing process [p3]**
- ⭐ (green) "Writers often **labor for hours** over their introductory paragraphs."
- They fine-tune, add, delete and exchange words, and search their notes for hooks: **anecdotes, provocative quotations and surprising facts** found during research.
- Some writers can't move on until the lead is exactly right; others finish a rough draft first and return to fine-tune the lead. "Whichever process works best for you is the process you should follow."

**H. Bookends [p3]**
- Taken together, leads and endings are the **bookends** of an article. They hold everything in place and set its boundaries.
- The lead paragraph gives you a **direction**; the closing paragraph gives you a **destination**. The next challenge is to arrange the rest of the content in logical order.

**I. Beyond the summary lead [p3–p4]**
- ⭐ (green) Unlike reporters who cover **breaking news** and get to the point quickly, feature writers have the luxury of **well-crafted and engaging opening paragraphs**.
- Feature writers still use the familiar, no-frills **summary lead** that answers who, what, where, when, why and how. They simply have the flexibility to try more engaging approaches.
- Critics call summary leads "Boring!", but "what summary leads lack in creativity they make up for in **information**".
- Example 1, *Rolling Stone*: "Four-dollar-a-gallon gas prices are eating away at the summer-concert business, with top festivals and tours taking unexpected box-office hits over the past few months."
  - The textbook calls this a **29-word** opening sentence that contains the essence of the article and answers the six basic questions: Who? Gasoline. What? Is eating into profits. Where? On the concert circuit. When? This summer. How? By reducing attendance. Why? Prices have soared to $4 per gallon.
  - Readers can stop after the lead or read on for the details.
- Example 2, *USA Today*: a two-sentence summary lead that opens "Don't expect relief from high gasoline prices by flying to your spring break vacation" and adds that airfares are already up and will stay high.
  - It is aimed squarely at its audience (airline passengers). Who? "You (understood)."
  - It succeeds because it gives **the gist in two sentences**. Later paragraphs add statistics, expert quotes and anecdotes, and readers who don't want them can turn the page.

**J. Nutgraf, also called the billboard paragraph [p4–p5]**
- Some feature writers with writer's block write a **summary lead first**, just to get words on the screen and some momentum. Later they **top it with a more creative lead**, but they **keep** the summary as the second or third paragraph.
- That paragraph is the **billboard paragraph** or **"nutgraf"**. It tells readers what the story is about, **in a nutshell**.
- **Many editors expect every article** to contain a nutgraf or billboard paragraph. Its logical place is **immediately after the lead**.

**K. Leads that succeed [p5]**
- "Few rules are unbendable" in feature writing. Try several lead paragraphs and pick the best. Factors to consider:
  - **The publication.** Many newspapers and magazines prefer short articles, limited to a page, that are quick to read. Their editors tend to rely on "the old workhorse, the summary lead".
  - **The topic.** How-to articles usually get to the point quickly, because readers want the advice and care little about creative introductions. Profiles and articles on complex issues often allow a more leisurely opening.
  - **The audience.** Age, profession and education level can shape readers' preferences. People who read for entertainment may enjoy descriptive openings; people who read for information may prefer a straightforward approach.

**L. There are no universal lead "types" [p5]**
- Every journalism textbook has its own vocabulary for classifying leads. Apart from the well-recognized **anecdote lead**, no lead "types" are universally recognized.
- In the authors' informal survey of about **60 articles** in a dozen popular consumer magazines, about **80 percent** mentioned **a person** in the lead, through an anecdote, quote, narrative or scenario.

**M. ⭐ Don McKinney's four basic ways to begin a story** (from his book *Magazine Writing That Sells*) [p5–p6] (green)
1. Start by **telling a story**.
2. Start by **letting someone else talk**.
3. Start by **thrusting us into the action**.
4. Start by **telling us what you're going to tell us**.

**N. Six leads that work regardless of publication, topic or audience [p6–p9]**

1. ⭐ **Anecdote lead** [p6–p7] (green definition, blue examples)
   - Anecdotes are **little stories about people** that happened in **a specific place at a specific time**.
   - Often called the "**chocolate chips of feature writing**", they are perhaps **the most frequently used** leads in feature and magazine stories.
   - Readers love them because they **illustrate rather than dictate** the important facts.
   - Example A (blue), from "Why China's Weight-Loss Industry Is Gaining": Simon Wang, an only child in Shanghai, was fed dumplings, ice cream and Kentucky Fried Chicken by his parents and grandparents. After reaching 220 pounds, the 26-year-old joined Weight Watchers. Then comes his quote about his fear of a heart attack. The specific details illustrate the larger theme, China's weight-loss industry.
   - Example B (blue), Josh Peter's *Times-Picayune* series on the desegregation of a Southern high school: "Friday night. Homecoming. An hour before kickoff." These **three sentence fragments set the scene**. Then, in a hushed locker room, a white defensive lineman and a black running back share a pair of headphones: "a moment once unthinkable" at that school.

2. ⭐ **Scenario lead** [p7] (blue)
   - By accurately **recreating a place**, the scenario lead lets readers "see" the backdrop against which the story unfolds.
   - The key: **avoid too much description**. Don't include every detail; be **selective**; use a **light hand**; let the reader's imagination fill in the gaps.
   - Example (blue): a *Vogue* profile of actress Meryl Streep. The textbook says its "newspeg" is the release of *The Iron Lady* (*My note:* from the context, the current event the profile hangs on). It opens with Streep saying, "Oooh, they have oysters," and sets the scene precisely: not just "the afternoon" but **4:20**; not just "a restaurant" but the restaurant on **the mezzanine of Union Station**, Washington, D.C.
   - ⭐ (blue) The textbook's comment: sparse but precise details, and "the use of **present tense** gives a feeling of **immediacy**."

3. ⭐ **Shock lead** [p7–p8] (green)
   - Lead paragraphs with a shock element are likely to grab and hold readers' attention, "at least for a little while".
   - They succeed if, during research, the writer uncovers **truly surprising information**. Chances are that what surprised the writer will also surprise readers.
   - Example 1: an overview of new gadgets opens with a pre-Valentine's Day study claiming that 47 percent of British men would give up sex for a big-screen plasma TV. The lead is short, and its humorous tone continues through the article.
   - Example 2 (green): Wendy Brenner's *Oxford American* feature. In 1971, 14-year-old Dean Ripa was "performing surgery" on a cottonmouth snake at home and it bit him; the bite meant his parents would find out about the 50 snakes he kept in their spare room. It is **part shock, part anecdote**: writers may **combine** lead categories. The tone is humorous, understated and casual.

4. ⭐ **Blind lead** [p8–p9] (blue)
   - It raises readers' curiosity by **omitting a key piece of information**, which keeps them reading.
   - It is "blind" because it **doesn't immediately identify by name** the person or people the article is about. That fact stays a mystery until a later sentence or paragraph.
   - Example (blue): "Business majors can party. They can get wasted, wake up the next morning, hit 18 holes, and still make afternoon classes." The subject, Duff McKagan (bassist for Guns N' Roses and Velvet Revolver, and a business student at Seattle University), is named only in the **third sentence**.

5. ⭐ **Direct or indirect quote lead** [p9] (blue)
   - A quotation makes an effective lead if the speaker said something **noteworthy that sums up the article's main idea**.
   - The **indirect quotation lead** is a variation. It works when you want the **essence** of what someone said without the speaker's exact words.
   - Example 1: a fitness expert, Rodolpho Reyes, says that unless you're training for a marathon, bike race or triathlon, "Cardio is an inefficient waste of a guy's time." It **combines the shock/surprise and direct quote** categories.
   - Example 2 (blue), a profile of romance novelist Janette Oke: "The trouble with writing at home, says author Janette Oke over the din of her dishwasher, is the ever-present lure of housework." The paragraph opens with an indirect quotation and ends with a direct one ("Once a housewife, always a housewife"), so it mixes **scenario, indirect quote and direct quote**.

6. ⭐ **Direct-address lead** [p9] (blue)
   - It **invites the reader to participate** in the story: the writer speaks directly to the reader using "**you**" in the opening paragraph. The lead is much like **a conversation** between writer and reader. Often the narrator is offering **friendly advice**.
   - Weaknesses:
     - the writer can appear **condescending** and fail to respect readers' intelligence;
     - the writer can wrongly **assume** that the described scenario is common or familiar to readers.
   - That's why it is so important to **know the characteristics of your readers**.
   - ⚠️ The handout stops here, so no direct-address example is included. *My note:* the headline of [6.5] ("…here's what you should know") uses the same "you" move.

**O. News leads [6.1] and feature leads [6.2]: how they relate** *(My note)*
- **Two names appear in both** materials: **anecdote** and **direct address**. [6.1-p40] lists them as feature-lead formats, and [6.2] defines them.
- **Delayed identification [6.1] vs blind lead [6.2]:** both hold back the name, for different reasons. Delayed identification is used because the person is **not well known** and the *what* matters more. A blind lead holds the name back to create **curiosity**, even when the person is famous (Duff McKagan).
- **Partial summary [6.1] vs shock and blind leads [6.2]:** all of them create suspense by holding something back. [6.1-p11] warns of the cost: vague significance, confused readers, too little information to hook them.
- **The summary lead appears in both:** it is the news story's "thesis" [6.1-p4] and the feature writer's "old workhorse" [6.2-p5].
- **Scenario lead [6.2] vs "description" [6.1]:** they sound alike, but [6.1] doesn't define "description", so don't treat them as the same thing without checking (🔎 Track B).

#### 2.6 How to Find an Original Idea [6.3]

One book page (p. 18): "Sidebar 2.2: How to Know Whether You Have an Original Article Idea". Questions 1 and 3–9 are highlighted green (⭐); questions 2 and 10 are not.

**A. ⭐ The rule:** you should be able to answer "**yes**" to **at least the majority** of the following 10 questions.
- *My note:* a majority of 10 means at least 6.

**B. The 10 questions** (paraphrased; my short label in bold)
1. ⭐ **New?** Is the topic so new and original that you can't find any books written on it?
2. **Fits a magazine?** Will it appeal to the special-interest group that reads the magazine you want to write for? Or is there a magazine that focuses on what you want to write about?
3. ⭐ **Fundamental life issue?** Does it deal with death, love, sickness, money, careers or health: issues that affect **millions of people**?
4. ⭐ **Unifying theme?** Do you have a strong, central unifying theme?
5. ⭐ **One sentence, action verb?** Can you state your theme in one sentence using an **action verb**?
6. ⭐ **Insight?** Does your angle let you offer **intelligent insight**, rather than something obvious, common sense, or something readers have read many times?
7. ⭐ **Drama or conflict?** Are there elements of drama or conflict to attract and sustain the reader?

### 3.1 Media law as taught [ML]

**Key terms (class definitions)**

| Term | Class meaning | Slide |
|---|---|---|
| Defamation | "the action of damaging the good reputation of someone; slander or libel" | [ML-p3] |
| Slander | a false **spoken** statement damaging to a person's reputation | [ML-p4] |
| Libel | a **published** false statement damaging to a person's reputation; a written defamation | [ML-p4] |

| Copyright | protection from the moment a work is fixed in tangible form | [ML-p22] |

| Political correctness | the belief that speech or behavior offensive to groups' sensibilities should be eliminated, by regulations or penalties if necessary | [ML-p23] |

**Lists to know by heart**
- **Four things a plaintiff must show (P-F-I-U):** Published · False (objectively; opinion doesn't count) · Injurious · Unprivileged [ML-p6].
- **Four categories of damage:** crime · public office, profession or occupation · serious immorality · loathsome (contagious) disease [ML-p8].
- **Three traditional defenses:** truth (the best) · privilege · fair comment and criticism [ML-p9]. **Two other constitutional standards:** actual malice · negligence tests [ML-p15].
- **Paulson's keys:** reported fully? factually? fairly? in good faith? [ML-p16]
- **Four privacy risks:** physical intrusion (trespass) · misleading story or photo (false light) · true but offensive private facts · name or picture used in an ad or for trade [ML-p17–p18].
- **Two privacy defenses:** consent (don't exceed it) · newsworthiness [ML-p19]. **Three exceptions:** trespassing · false light · unwanted publicity [ML-p19].
- **Copyright terms:** life of the author + 70 years. Works for hire, anonymous and pseudonymous works: 95 years from publication or 100 from creation, whichever is shorter [ML-p22].

**Spot-the-problem routine** *(My note: a study method that applies the class rules in order)*
1. Does the text say something about a **person**? Is it a **fact claim** or an **opinion**?
2. A fact claim that hurts a reputation: does it fall into one of the **four categories**? Check **P-F-I-U**. Can you prove it is **true**? (Truth is the best defense.)
3. Did it come from an **official**, or from a **court session, legislative session or press conference**? Then privilege applies: absolute for the official, qualified for you **if** your report is full, fair and accurate.
4. An opinion about a public figure's **public offering**? That is **fair comment**, as long as you don't misstate the facts or imply hidden damaging information.
5. **How was the material gathered?** On private property without an invitation, or by deception, it is **trespassing**, and courts won't protect it even if it is newsworthy (the Life magazine case).
6. Could true material **mislead in its new context** (a reused photo, for example)? That is **false light**.
7. Is it a **true but offensive private fact**? That is **unwanted publicity**, and newsworthiness won't protect you.
8. Is a **name or picture used to sell** something? You need **consent**, and you must not exceed it.
9. Is someone's **work reproduced**? Copyright applies; only **small excerpts** may count as fair use.
10. Finish with **Paulson's four questions**.

**Answer template for a media-law question** *(My note)*: (1) name the problem with the class term; (2) give the class definition; (3) apply the test (elements, categories) to the facts; (4) name the defense or exception that applies; (5) say what the writer should do instead.

### 3.3 The inverted pyramid and its checklist [5.2]

```
██████████████████████  LEAD: 1 sentence, 30–35 words max, the most important facts
  ██████████████████    2nd paragraph: develops an idea from the lead (no chronology)
    ██████████████      supporting facts + attributed quotes (max 2 sentences each)
      ██████████        background and less important details
        ██████          least important
```

**Lead formula** *(My note, built from the class examples)*: [who/what] + [what happened / the result] + [where] + [when] + (how / why if known) + [attribution]. The class examples put attribution at the **end** of the lead: "…, the Ohio Highway Patrol said Friday" [6.1-p5]; "…, researchers say" [p7]; "…, Congress was told Tuesday" [p8]; "…, authorities said" [p24]; "…, police said Tuesday" [p28].

**Checklist before you hand in a news story** [5.2]
- [ ] Information is in **descending order of importance**.
- [ ] Lead: **one sentence**, **30–35 words maximum**, the most important information, **specific facts**.
- [ ] Second paragraph **develops an idea from the lead**; it does **not** turn into a chronological narrative.
- [ ] All major information is **attributed** (unless it is commonly known or the information itself implies the source).
- [ ] No string of quotes; each direct quote is **two sentences or fewer**.
- [ ] Quotes punctuated and ordered as **quote, speaker, verb**: "John did not go with her," he said.
- [ ] **AP style** for numbers, dates, locations, titles (🔎 the rules themselves).
- [ ] Pronoun–antecedent agreement; it / its / it's.
- [ ] No "**it is… / there is… / there are…**".
- [ ] **Past tense**.
- [ ] No comma splices or run-ons.
- [ ] No **apostrophe-s plurals**.
- [ ] **Short paragraphs**: three sentences is probably too long, four or more is definitely too long.
- [ ] No **wordiness**, redundancy or repetition.
- [ ] **Title before name → no commas** ("Game warden Brad Fisher…"); **name before title → commas** ("Brad Fisher, the game warden, …").
- [ ] **Transitions** tie the paragraphs together.
- [ ] **Your own words**, not the information sheet's.
- [ ] **Names** spelled correctly (check twice).

**D. Choosing a lead** *(My note: a decision guide built from the class rules)*
- Clear breaking news → **summary**.
- Several equally important incidents → **multiple element / roundup**.
- A national or world event with a local link → **localized**.
- A new development in an old story → **follow-up**.
- A famous person, or the person is the story → **immediate identification**. An ordinary person → **delayed identification**.
- An angle story, too many details, or a need for suspense → **partial summary**.
- A feature about a person with a story → **anecdote**; a vivid place → **scenario**; a truly surprising fact → **shock**; curiosity about who → **blind**; a quote that sums it all up → **quote lead**; advice to the reader → **direct address**.
- Always weigh the **publication, topic and audience** [6.2-p5].

### 3.5 Where to begin [6.2]

- **Four jobs of a lead:** attract attention · introduce the topic · set the tone · establish the point of view [p1].
- **Length:** a newspaper feature's opening sentence can be as short as 12–15 words; a magazine's may be two or three times longer [p1]. A news lead: 30–35 words maximum [5.2].
- **Three names for it:** lead (leads readers in) · hook (hooks attention) · introduction (introduces topic, tone, point of view) [p3].
- **Airplane rule:** articles crash at takeoff (lead) and landing (ending). Make the ending smooth, not abrupt, and fast-paced, not prolonged. **Know your ending before you begin** [p2–p3].
- **Bookends:** the lead gives you a direction, the ending a destination; arrange the middle logically [p3].
- **Nutgraf (billboard paragraph):** tells the story in a nutshell; place it **right after a creative lead**; many editors expect one in every article [p4–p5].
- **Choose the lead by:** publication (short pieces → summary lead) · topic (how-to → get to the point; profiles and complex issues → more leisurely) · audience (entertainment readers → description; information readers → straightforward) [p5].
- **Process:** writers labor for hours; try anecdotes, provocative quotations and surprising facts; write the lead first or last, whichever works for you [p3].

### 3.6 Finding original ideas [6.3]

- **Rule:** answer "yes" to the **majority** of the 10 questions.
- **Quick grid** *(My note: the 10 questions in three groups)*:
  - *Is it fresh?* (1) no books on it yet · (6) intelligent insight, not the obvious · (9) questions the conventional wisdom.
  - *Will readers care?* (2) fits a magazine's special-interest readers · (3) fundamental life issues affecting millions · (7) drama or conflict · (8) colorful anecdotes, human interest.
  - *Can I write it?* (4) strong central unifying theme · (5) theme in one sentence with an action verb · (10) access to participants, keen observers or experts.
- **Where ideas come from:** read continually, including publications few others read: professional quarterlies, association newsletters, academic journals, annual reports, almanacs. They seem boring, but they carry original thinking long before the public hears of it.

### 3.7 Tightening an angle: steps [6.4, with 6.3]

1. Write the **broad topic** in a center circle.
2. Brainstorm **four angles** around it. *My note:* the Sidebar 3.1 patterns help: a **person with first-hand experience**, **one method** (often as a question), **a number**, **a contrarian twist**.
3. **Don't cover all four** (no depth). Choose **one main angle** and **one sidebar**.
4. Remember: the more a topic has been covered, the **sharper and fresher** your angle must be.
5. Test the main angle for **unity** (everything hangs together around one central idea), **action** and **concreteness** (🔎 definitions). State the theme **in one sentence with an action verb** [6.3 Q5].
6. Run the **10 original-idea questions**; aim for a majority of "yes".
7. Decide **whom to interview** and what **background research** to do.
8. Write a working title in the style of Sidebar 3.1, e.g. "An Ex-Burglar Tells How to Burglar-Proof Your Home".

- **The ending** is a planned "landing" [6.2-p2–p3]: a short closing quote that answers the reader's practical worry ("Is the hornet I saw one of these?"). It is smooth, not abrupt.
- **The second-paragraph test [5.2]:** in the original May 4 story, the paragraph after the lead (¶3) **develops the lead's idea** (why the hornets matter) and doesn't slip into chronology ✓. With the update on top, the next paragraph (¶2) steps back to late 2019. ### 4.6 Media-law check [ML]

- **No person is accused of anything.** The criticism is aimed at a nickname, not a person, so the story raises no defamation problem under [ML-p6, p8].
- **Opinions are labelled and attributed as opinions:** "It's Takahashi's opinion that…" (¶19); "he doesn't love it" (¶7). An opinion is not an "objectively false" statement [ML-p6].
- **The update reports what state officials said at a news conference**, with attribution. That is the kind of official statement a reporter may report under **qualified privilege**, provided the coverage is full, fair and accurate [ML-p12].
- **Paulson's test** [ML-p16]: *fully* (several sources and both views of the nickname), *factually* (numbers, places, species names), *fairly* (Looney's caution balances the alarm), *in good faith* (it corrects false alarms in the east) ✓.

### 4.7 What to copy, and what not to copy, in an exam news story

- **Copy:** the newest and most important fact first · one vivid, specific detail in a short lead · a nutgraf right after a creative lead · attribution for every claim, with name and title set off by commas · conflict balanced with caution · an ending quote that answers the reader's question.
- **Don't copy** (for a straight news story): present-tense "says" (write "said") · four-sentence paragraphs · "there is / it is" structures · verb-before-speaker attribution.

**Task 10: Spot the media-law problem.** For each fictional item, name the class concept, explain it, and say what the writer should do.

**(a)** A student blog post says: "Café owner Davron Toshev has been stealing his workers' wages." It is based on one anonymous rumor.
- **Libel.** It is published, and it accuses someone of a **crime** (category 1) [ML-p8]. If it is false, injurious (lost customers) and unprivileged, P-F-I-U is met [ML-p6].
- Truth is the best defense [ML-p9], but one anonymous rumor proves nothing.
- **Do:** don't publish until you can prove it. Get named sources and Toshev's response (Paulson: fully, factually, fairly [ML-p16]).

**(b)** A court reporter quotes the prosecutor at an open trial calling the defendant "a liar and a thief", and also reports the defense lawyer's reply.
- **Privilege.** Participants in a judicial proceeding are absolutely privileged during it [ML-p11].
- The reporter has a **qualified privilege** if the coverage is full, fair and accurate [ML-p12]. Reporting both sides helps meet that condition.

**(c)** A concert review: "The pop star's new show was dull, overpriced and badly sung."
- **Fair comment and criticism** of the public offering of a person in the public eye [ML-p13–p14].
- It is protected as long as the facts behind it (for example, the ticket price) are stated correctly and the writer doesn't hint at hidden damaging information.

**(d)** A photographer pretends to be a plumber to get into a businessman's apartment and photograph his documents for a newsworthy corruption story.
- **Trespassing by deception.** Courts won't protect a trespasser, even for a newsworthy story. Compare the Life magazine "faith healer" case [ML-p20].
- **Do:** enter private property only when invited by the owner or renter.

**(e)** A photo of a student hit by a speeding car at a crosswalk (first used in a story about the careless driver) is reused to illustrate an article called "Students who ignore traffic lights."
- **False light**, and possibly libel. This is the careful-pedestrian example from class [ML-p21].
- **Do:** use a photo that fits the story, or one with no identifiable person.

**(f)** A café puts a well-known professor's photo on its advertising flyer without asking.
- **Invasion of privacy:** a picture used in an advertisement, for purposes of trade [ML-p18].
- **Do:** get consent and stay within it [ML-p19].

**(g)** A student newspaper story on exam stress reveals, from gossip, that a named classmate (not a public figure) is being treated for a private medical condition.
- **True but offensive private facts, i.e. "unwanted publicity".** Newsworthiness won't protect it [ML-p17, p19].
- If the claim were false and about a "loathsome (contagious)" disease, it would also fall under libel category 4 [ML-p8].
- **Do:** leave the name out, or get consent.

**(h)** A student magazine reprints a whole textbook chapter "for educational purposes".
- **Copyright.** The chapter is protected (fixed in tangible form; life + 70 years) [ML-p22], and fair use covers only small excerpts.
- **Do:** quote a short excerpt with credit, or get permission.

1. "Write a summary lead (35 words or fewer) from this fact sheet." → the lead formula in §3.3, then the checklist. (Task 1)
2. "Write three different leads for these facts and label each" (e.g. summary, delayed identification, localized, follow-up, partial summary). (Tasks 1, 2, 6)
3. "Write a short news story (lead + 4–6 paragraphs) in inverted-pyramid form from this information sheet." → the [5.2] checklist; don't copy the sheet's wording. (Tasks 3, 4)
4. "Write a feature lead (anecdote, scenario, blind, quote or direct address) and a nutgraf for this profile." → [6.2]. (Task 7)
5. "Narrow this broad topic into a tight angle with an angle tree; choose a main angle and a sidebar." → [6.4]. (Task 8)
6. "Correct the errors in this paragraph using the inverted pyramid checklist." (Task 4)

**B. Identify and explain**
7. "Name the lead type of each example and explain why" (class examples or new ones). → table in §3.4.
8. "What is the difference between a summary lead and a multiple-element lead?" (the whole vs two or more equally important actions [6.1-p20]); "…between immediate and delayed identification?" [6.1-p31, p35].
9. "What are the pitfalls of a partial summary lead?" (vague significance; confused readers; not enough precise information to hook [6.1-p11]).
10. "What is news? Name and explain the elements of a good news story." [5.1]
11. "What are the four jobs of a lead? What is a nutgraf and where does it go? What are 'bookends'?" [6.2]
12. "Is this idea original? Apply the 10 questions." [6.3]

**C. Media law**
13. Define defamation, slander and libel; explain tort, plaintiff and defendant [ML-p3–p5].
14. List the four things a plaintiff must prove, the four categories and the three defenses; define actual malice [ML-p6–p15].

**D. Analysis**
20. "Analyze this story (for example [6.5]): lead type, structure, news values, angle." (§4)

### 6.2 Grading checklist: check your answer before you hand it in

**A lead**
- [ ] One sentence, 30–35 words maximum [5.2].
- [ ] The most important information first, with specific facts (numbers, places, the day).
- [ ] Who, what, where, when (+ how, why if known; never invent them) [6.1-p4–p5].
- [ ] Attribution, usually at the end ("…, police said Tuesday").
- [ ] Past tense; no "there is / it is" [5.2].
- [ ] The right type for the facts: prominent person → named; ordinary person → described; local link → localized; new development → follow-up; several equal events → roundup [6.1].
- [ ] Labelled correctly, if the question asks for named types.

**A news story**
- [ ] Descending order of importance [5.2].
- [ ] The second paragraph develops the lead, with no chronological narrative.
- [ ] Each quote is two sentences or fewer, in quote, speaker, verb order: "…," he said.
- [ ] Every major fact is attributed.
- [ ] Paragraphs of one or two sentences.
- [ ] Name and title: title before name → no commas; name before title → commas.
- [ ] Transitions between paragraphs.
- [ ] No apostrophe-s plurals, comma splices or run-ons; pronoun agreement; it / its / it's correct.
- [ ] Your own words, not the fact sheet's; names spelled correctly.
- [ ] **Media-law check:** no unproven accusation; opinions labelled as opinions; nothing gathered by trespass or deception; no misleading photo or context; no offensive private facts; Paulson's four questions [ML].

**A feature lead**
- [ ] It does the four jobs: attention, topic, tone, point of view [6.2-p1].
- [ ] It fits its type: an anecdote has a specific person, time and place; a scenario has few, precise details; a shock lead has a truly surprising fact; a blind lead names the person soon after; a quote lead has a quote that sums up the idea; a direct-address lead doesn't talk down to readers [6.2-p6–p9].
- [ ] A nutgraf follows right after it [6.2-p4–p5].
- [ ] You know your ending (the second bookend) [6.2-p3].

**An angle answer**
- [ ] An angle tree with four angles [6.4].
- [ ] One main angle and one sidebar chosen, with a reason.
- [ ] The theme in one sentence with an action verb [6.3 Q5].
- [ ] Unity explained; the people you would interview named.

**A media-law answer**
- [ ] The class term named → the definition → the elements or categories applied to the facts → the defense or exception → a fix (§3.1 template).

### 6.3 One-look memory sheet

- **Defamation:** slander = spoken · libel = published · tort · plaintiff vs defendant · **P-F-I-U** · crime / job / immorality / contagious disease.
- **Defenses:** truth (the best; on public-concern matters the plaintiff carries the burden of proof) · privilege (absolute for officials; qualified for reporters: full, fair, accurate) · fair comment (opinion on public offerings; no misstated facts; no hinted secrets) · actual malice = knowing falsity or reckless disregard for the truth · negligence tests (🔎).
- **Privacy:** the right to be left alone · intrusion / false light / true-but-offensive private facts / name or picture in an ad · defenses: consent, newsworthiness · exceptions: trespassing, false light, unwanted publicity · Life magazine faith-healer case · careful-pedestrian photo.
- **Copyright:** protected from fixation · life + 70 years · works for hire: 95 years from publication or 100 from creation, whichever is shorter · fair use = small excerpts.
- **Paulson:** fully, factually, fairly, in good faith.
- **News:** Relevance, Usefulness, Interest · Impact, Conflict, Novelty, Prominence, Proximity, Timeliness, Engagement, Solutions.
- **Inverted pyramid:** descending importance · lead = 1 sentence, 30–35 words max · 2nd paragraph develops, no chronology · quotes of 2 sentences max · quote, speaker, verb · past tense · paragraphs of 1–2 sentences.
- **8 news leads:** summary · partial summary · multiple element/roundup · localized · follow-up · immediate ID · delayed ID · feature. Summary = the whole beats the parts; multiple element = two or more equal actions.
- **Features:** 4 jobs (attention, topic, tone, point of view) · nutgraf right after the lead · bookends · know your ending · 6 leads: anecdote, scenario, shock, blind, direct/indirect quote, direct address · McKinney: tell a story, let someone talk, thrust into action, tell what you'll tell.
- **Ideas and angles:** majority "yes" of 10 · angle tree: 4 angles → 1 main + 1 sidebar · unity, action, concreteness.
