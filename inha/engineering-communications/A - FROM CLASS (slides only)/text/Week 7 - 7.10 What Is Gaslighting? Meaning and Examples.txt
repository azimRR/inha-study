       What is gaslighting?
       Gaslighting is a form of emotional and psychological
       abuse where someone deliberately tries to confuse
       you, manipulate you, or make you doubt yourself.
       Find out more about waht gaslighting means in
       relationships, and how to recognise the signs.

       Gaslighting can make you doubt yourself, your sense of reality and your
       memories. It can involve denial, twisting facts, or making you feel like
       you’re overreacting. It can leave you feeling confused, isolated, and
       powerless. When it's used in a pattern of repeated behaviours, it's a form
       of coercive control, which is family and domestic violence.

       Statistics from the Australian Bureau of Statistics tell us that 23% of
       women and 14% of men have experienced partner emotional abuse.
       Gaslighting in relationships is a real and dangerous issue for many
       Australians.

       Why is it called "gaslighting"?
       The term gaslighting originates from Patrick Hamilton's 1938 play Gas
       Light, in which a husband slowly makes his wife lose trust in her reality -
       dimming the gas lights in their home and then denying the change.
       Gaslighting has since become widely used to describe this type of

       What are some examples of gaslighting in a
       relationship?
       Perpetrators use tactics to make you question your reality. Some common
       tactics include:

               Countering: Denying your version of reality.
               Diverting: Changing the subject to avoid responsibility.
               Trivialising: Brushing off your concerns and feelings.
               Withholding: Refusing to have a conversation about your concerns.
               Stereotyping: Using stereotypes based on your identity to dismiss
               your concerns.

       With these tactics, gaslighting can sound like many things. Some more
       examples of what it might sound like include:

               “You’re so sensitive.”
               “You’re overreacting about this.”
               “You know I would never do that to you.”
               “You need to get some help.”
               “You can’t trust your memory.”
               “You’re exaggerating what actually happened.”
               “You’re crazy.”

       What are the signs of being gaslighted?
       Gaslighting has many mental or emotional impacts. When you’re
       experiencing gaslighting, you might feel:

               like you’re second guessing your own thoughts and emotions
               a sense of dependency on the abuser
               like you need to apologise a lot

       Gaslighting can happen gradually. This can make it hard to see the signs
       until the effects on your life become severe. For example, increased
       anxiety and depression.

       Gaslighting doesn't only happen in intimate relationships. It can also
       happen at work with colleagues, with a neighbour, between family
       members or friends, and even by medical professionals.

       Why is gaslighting dangerous?
       Gaslighting someone is emotional abuse, and it is used to manipulate and
       control another person. Gaslighting is dangerous because it can:

               erode your confidence, self-worth and sense of reality
               take away your independence and create doubt in your mind
               make you dependent on your abuser as a source of truth
               result in you becoming isolated from friends or family

       How can I help someone who is experiencing
       gaslighting?

       Talking about gaslighting or coercive control can be tricky. Especially
       because these behaviours can be so subtle that they’re hard to notice.

       It can be easy for someone to dismiss or rationalise the manipulation as
       “just caring” or “just how they are.”

       If you notice someone in your life is showing signs of being gaslit, you can
       offer support and be there to listen without judgement.

       You can start a conversation with that person in a few ways:

               Create a safe space for them to feel a bit more comfortable opening
               up to you without fear of anyone else overhearing.
               Ask them how they feel when that person said or did that thing.
               Validate their feelings and believe what they say.
               Encourage them to keep a record of what’s happening somewhere
               secure.
               Avoid judging their choices.
               Help them to explore support options available to them.

       What to do if you’re worried you are gaslighting in your
       relationship

       If you’re concerned about your behaviour, there are support services and
       resources available to help. Taking responsibility is the first step towards
       positive change.

       Help and support

       If you, or someone you know, is in immediate danger, call the police now
       on 000.

       If it’s not an emergency and you need support, you can call support
