## 1. News values: what makes something news?

### Takeaway
News values (newsworthiness criteria) are the tests editors use to decide what gets covered and how prominently. The standard journalism-course list is timeliness, proximity, prominence, impact/consequence, conflict, novelty/unusualness and human interest. Training sources add currency, relevance and a "so what?" test. Academic lists go back to Galtung & Ruge (1965), updated by Harcup & O'Neill (2001, 2017). The more values a story combines, and the closer it is to *your* audience, the stronger it is.

### Cited Findings
**Purdue OWL ("The Components of 'Newsworthiness'")**
- Gatekeepers decide coverage using fairly predictable values. Purdue presents two models. — [Purdue OWL: Newsworthiness](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/components_of_newsworthiness.html)
- **Model 1, attributed to Galtung and Ruge (dated 1973 on the page):**
  - Relevance: "How relevant is a news story to the audience in question?" Example: California earthquakes matter more to West Coast audiences than ones in Calcutta.
  - Timeliness: "How recently did the event unfold?"
  - Simplification: easily summarized stories get more coverage than complex ones.
  - Predictability: scheduled events (elections, sports, legal decisions) gain news value as they approach.
  - Unexpectedness: disasters, accidents, crimes.
  - Continuity: wars, elections and protests stay newsworthy over time.
  - Composition: editors balance soft human-interest stories with hard news.
  - Elite People.
  - Elite Countries.
  - Negativity: "Editors deem bad news more newsworthy than good news."
  - — [Purdue OWL: Newsworthiness](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/components_of_newsworthiness.html)
- **Model 2, attributed to Shoemaker et al. (1987):**
  - Timeliness.
  - Proximity: "The closer an event takes place to the intended audience, the more important it is."
  - Importance/Impact/Consequence: how many people does it affect? Climate change is the global-impact example.
  - Interest: does it have special human interest, e.g., overcoming the odds?
  - Conflict or Controversy.
  - Sensationalism.
  - Prominence: public figures' actions are more newsworthy than non-public figures'.
  - Novelty/Oddity/Unusual: "Man bites dog" versus the routine "dog bites man".
  - — [Purdue OWL: Newsworthiness](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/components_of_newsworthiness.html)

**The News Manual (Ingram & Henshall, a free online journalism textbook), Ch. 1 "What is news?"**
- Its tests:
  - Is it **new**? "If it is not new, it cannot be news." Older events count if newly disclosed.
  - Is it **unusual**? "Dog bites man" is not news; "Man bites dog" is news. What counts as unusual varies by society.
  - Is it **interesting**?
  - Is it **significant**? Does it affect people's lives?
  - Is it **about people**? Natural disasters become news "when they affect people's lives."
  - — [The News Manual, Ch. 1](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_01.htm)
- "The same event can have different levels of interest in different societies, and will be talked about in different ways." — [The News Manual, Ch. 1](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_01.htm)

**Media Helping Media (MHM), "Quick Guide: Assessing news value" (27 Mar 2026)**
- Ten criteria:
  - Impact: "Ask yourself how many people are affected by the event. The more people it touches, the bigger the news."
  - Conflict.
  - Timeliness: "News must be new. If an event happened a week ago and everyone knows about it, it is no longer news unless there is a fresh development."
  - Proximity.
  - Prominence: "Actions involving well-known people, such as leaders or celebrities, are often more newsworthy than those involving unknown individuals."
  - Unusualness.
  - Currency: "Some topics become news because they are already being talked about by the public."
  - Human interest.
  - The "so what" test: why should readers care?
  - Evidence: "Ensure the story is based on facts that you can prove. News must be accurate and never based on gossip or unsourced rumours."
  - — [MHM Quick Guide: Assessing news value](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/spotting-a-news-story/)
- MHM's "News writing for beginners" (John Allen) starts from the question "Is there a story?". Its judgment factors are source, subject, people, audience, surprise, knowledge, timing and saturation. It stresses choosing an angle and deciding what to leave out. — [MHM: News writing for beginners](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/news-writing-tips-for-beginners/)

**Academic lineage (background)**

### Inferences
#### Exam-ready core list (mnemonic: "CHIP PUT")
| Value | Definition (exam wording) | Test question | Campus example (constructed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C**onflict | Disagreement or struggle between people, groups or ideas | "Who disagrees, and about what?" | Students petition against a new attendance rule |
| **H**uman interest | Emotional appeal; ordinary people coping with or overcoming difficulty | "Is there a person whose story makes readers feel something?" | A student builds a prosthetic hand for her brother |
| **I**mpact / consequence | How many people are affected, how seriously, for how long | "Who is affected, and how much?" | Tuition rises 20% for all 3,000 students |
| **P**rominence | Well-known people, institutions or places raise the value | "Is anyone famous or powerful involved?" | A minister speaks at graduation |
| **P**roximity | Geographic *or emotional/cultural* closeness to the audience | "How close is this to *my* readers?" | A road closure outside the main gate |
| **U**nusualness / novelty | Rare, odd, first, last, biggest ("man bites dog") | "Would people say 'really?'" | First all-female team to win a national robotics title |
| **T**imeliness | It is new: just happened, newly revealed, or about to happen | "Why are we publishing this *now*?" | Results announced this morning |

Add-ons that examiners also accept: **currency** (already being talked about), **relevance/usefulness** (helps readers decide or act), **negativity/bad news** (Galtung & Ruge), and **prominence of place** ("elite countries").

#### How to use news values in an exam answer
1. Name the value.
2. Define it in one line.
3. Point to the exact fact in the scenario that triggers it.
4. Say how it shapes the lead. The strongest value usually goes first.

- Stronger stories stack several values. Example: "A famous engineer (prominence) was injured (impact) when a bridge collapsed in our city (proximity) this morning (timeliness)."
- Proximity is not only about kilometres. Emotional or cultural closeness also counts, such as an Uzbek student abroad. This follows from the News Manual's point that the same event interests different societies differently.
- Timeliness includes "newly revealed" (News Manual). A two-week-old event first disclosed today is timely.
- Currency is different from timeliness (MHM). A topic can be news because people are already discussing it, even without a new event.

#### Applying the values to the "murder hornets" story (sourced facts in Section 4)
- **Novelty:** the first detections of the Asian giant hornet in North America came in 2019. The "murder hornet" nickname was itself a novelty and sensationalism hook.
- **Timeliness:** each new detection or nest (2019, 2020, 2021) and the December 2024 eradication declaration gave a fresh news peg.
- **Proximity:** strongest for Washington state and British Columbia readers. A Tashkent outlet needs a local angle (Section 4).
- **Impact:** a reporter must *source* why it matters, for example the threat to honeybees and agriculture, from the state agriculture department. These notes did not retrieve that, so do not assert it in an exam without a source.
- **Human interest / conflict:** these come only from real reported people such as beekeepers and scientists. Never invent them.

#### Common mistakes
- Listing values without linking each one to a fact in the scenario.
- Confusing prominence (who is involved) with impact (how many people are affected).
- Treating "interesting to me" as newsworthy without passing the "so what?" test.
- Ignoring audience. The same event ranks differently for a campus paper and a national paper.

## 2. The inverted pyramid: 5W1H, ordering by importance, the nut graf, attribution, and cutting from the bottom

### Takeaway
An inverted-pyramid story puts the most newsworthy facts in the lead: usually one sentence answering the *most important* of who/what/when/where/why/how, not all six. Every later paragraph is less important than the one before, so editors can cut from the bottom without losing the essentials. A nut graf (the "why this matters" paragraph) is essential after any soft or delayed lead. Attribute all opinions and anything that is not undisputed fact, using "said".

### Cited Findings
**Definition, purpose, history**
- Purdue: the structure places "the most fundamental information in the lead paragraph of the story, and then arrang[es] the remaining details, from most important to least important."
  - Essential information = Who, What, When, Where, Why.
  - Editors can cut from the bottom without losing essential content.
  - The telegraph made sending vital information first practical.
  - — [Purdue OWL: The Inverted Pyramid](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html)
- Purdue also records criticisms. Some call it "artless", say it removes reporter autonomy, contradicts narrative principles and may confuse readers. Online journalism has made it less dominant. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html)
- Poynter (Chip Scanlan, 20 Jun 2003): "The inverted pyramid puts the most newsworthy information at the top, and then the remaining information follows in order of importance, with the least important at the bottom." — [Poynter: Writing from the top down](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/writing-from-the-top-down-pros-and-cons-of-the-inverted-pyramid/)
- Poynter, history: the form developed with the telegraph in the 1800s and was in common newspaper use by the early 1900s. An early example is the AP lead after Lincoln was shot (14 Apr 1865): "The President was shot in a theater to-night and perhaps mortally wounded." — [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/writing-from-the-top-down-pros-and-cons-of-the-inverted-pyramid/)
- Poynter, pros and cons:
  - **Pros:** useful for breaking news; serves impatient readers; easy to rewrite as events change; builds analytical skill; works well online.
  - **Cons:** critics call it "outdated, unnatural, boring, artless"; it "tells stories backward".
  - **Cutting:** in the hot-type era editors could "cut stories from the bottom up: no news judgment required."
  - **Mitchell Stephens (journalism historian):** "The inverted pyramid organizes stories not around ideas or chronologies but around facts."
  - — [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/writing-from-the-top-down-pros-and-cons-of-the-inverted-pyramid/)
- News Manual Ch. 3:
  - The main news comes at the start, "the rest of the detail following in decreasing order of importance".
  - The intro gives "the newest, most unusual, most interesting and most significant" information "told clearly and simply".
  - The second paragraph elaborates on the first.
  - Editors can trim because "the least important facts are at the end. They can then be cut without harming the story."
  - — [News Manual Ch. 3](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_03.htm)
- News Manual's model story tapers in four sentences: "A Palauli woman whose body was found in the sea is believed to have drowned. Police say the 35-year-old woman, whose name has not been released, was an epileptic. Her body was found floating in the sea near Palauli, Savai`i, on Monday. A post mortem examination will be conducted today." — [News Manual Ch. 3](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_03.htm)
- MHM (John Allen, 2021):
  - Put "key news first, followed by supporting details, with the least important background information at the base".
  - Readers can "stop after the headline and summary knowing the basic facts".
  - The writer must "decide what the top line is, what comes second, third, and so on".
  - Example top line: "The price of cotton has fallen by 15 per cent." Layers on producers, the textile industry, the economy, consumer prices and jobs follow.
  - — [MHM: The inverted pyramid](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/essential-elements-of-a-news-story/)
- Wikipedia (News style): "The essential and most interesting elements of a story are put at the beginning, with supporting information following in order of diminishing importance." Readers can stop at any point, and editors can trim to fit. — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)

**5W1H**
- News Manual Ch. 4: a story must answer "Who? What? Where? When? Why? and How?" ("WWWWWH"), but "they should not all be answered in your intro".
  - Method: list all key points, test each against the news criteria, rank them by newsworthiness, and pick the most newsworthy as your angle.
  - Worked result: "Six people were killed and more than 100 left homeless when Cyclone Victor hit the Solomon Islands yesterday."
  - — [News Manual Ch. 4](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_04.htm)
- Purdue: decide which aspect (who, what, when, where, why, how) is most important and emphasize it in the lead. — [Purdue OWL: How to Write a Lead](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)

**Nut graf**
- Poynter (Chip Scanlan, 19 May 2003):
  - "The nut graf tells the reader what the writer is up to; it delivers a promise of the story's content and message."
  - It is named so because, "like a nut, it contains the 'kernel,' or essential theme, of the story."
  - Functions: it justifies the story (why readers should care), moves the reader from the lead to the body, often explains timeliness, and often includes supporting material.
  - Placement: typically right after an anecdotal lead.
  - Ken Wells (*Wall Street Journal*): "a paragraph that says what this whole story is about and why you should read it."
  - Origin: the WSJ pioneered it under managing editor Barney Kilgore in 1941.
  - — [Poynter: The nut graf, part I](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/the-nut-graf-part-i/)
- Wikipedia: a soft lead is "typically followed by a nut graph". In features, the nut graph often comes as the third or fourth paragraph. — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)
- **Terminology conflict:** Purdue's pyramid page uses "nut graphs" loosely for the body paragraphs after the lead (details, quotes, statistics, background). Poynter and Wikipedia use "nut graf" for the *single* paragraph stating the story's point and significance. Use the Poynter meaning in exams. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/the-nut-graf-part-i/)

**Attribution and style**
- News Manual Ch. 9:
  - "Attribute ALL opinions and any information which is not a clear and undisputed fact."
  - Name speakers before the first time they are quoted, or when the speaker changes. Very short quotes (10 words or fewer) may take attribution after.
  - Vary "said" only when the alternative is accurate ("warned", "urged", "asked").
  - Avoid "stated" and "pointed out", which imply undisputed fact, and "claimed", which suggests disbelief. "According to" is neutral; use it sparingly.
  - "If you do not attribute an opinion to an individual, your audience will assume it is your own."
  - Clear, undisputed facts may go unattributed in the intro but must be sourced later.
  - — [News Manual Ch. 9](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_09.htm)
- SPJ Code (2026 text): "Never plagiarize. Always attribute." — [SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
- AP style basics (via Purdue):
  - For an event the day before publication, don't write "yesterday"; use the day of the week.
  - Use figures for dates, with no st/nd/rd/th.
  - With a date, abbreviate only Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., Dec.
  - Write times as "1 p.m., 3:30 a.m."; spell out noon and midnight.
  - Spell out numbers below 10.
  - Capitalize formal titles only before a name.
  - — [Purdue OWL: AP Style](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/ap_style.html)

**Other structures (for contrast)**
- News Manual Ch. 25 covers chronological structure and a "pyramid of pyramids" for complex stories. Ch. 50 describes feature structure as a "bead necklace". — [News Manual Ch. 25](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%202/volume2_25.htm); [News Manual Ch. 50](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%202/volume2_50.htm)
- The "1-2-3 lead" runs a 5W opening paragraph, then an indirect quote supporting it, then a direct quote supporting the indirect quote. — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)

### Inferences
#### Step-by-step method for writing an inverted-pyramid story (synthesized from News Manual Ch. 3–5 and 9, Purdue, Poynter, MHM)
1. **Collect and verify** all facts. Mark each as verified or attributed, and note who said what.
2. **List the key points**, one line each. Ignore order for now.
3. **Answer 5W1H** for the event as a whole. Any missing W is a reporting gap: go back and ask.
4. **Rank by news value.** Ask which fact is newest, affects the most people, is most unusual, and passes "so what?". Number the facts 1, 2, 3...
5. **Write the news point** in one plain sentence of 12 words or fewer, as if telling a friend. This is your angle.
6. **Write the lead.**
   - Use the top one or two W's.
   - Active voice, subject-verb-object, a concrete subject and a strong verb.
   - About 20 words; ceiling about 30–35.
   - Put the time element after the verb ("...on Monday").
7. **Paragraph 2: support the lead.** Add the next most important W's (often how and why) and attribute the source of the lead's claims.
8. **Significance and context (the nut graf).** Why it matters, with numbers and scale. After a hard lead it can be brief. After a soft lead it is mandatory by paragraph 2–4.
9. **Quotes, response, other side.** Put the best quote by paragraph 3–4. Use quotes for emotion, opinion or vivid language, not routine facts. Give anyone criticized a chance to respond (SPJ).
10. **Background and least important details last.** History, previous incidents, technical specifications.
11. **Cut test.** Delete the last paragraph. Does the story still make sense? Repeat. If something essential disappears, move it up.
12. **Style, accuracy and legal pass.**
    - AP style: day of the week, numerals, titles.
    - Name spellings.
    - Attribution for every opinion or disputed fact.
    - Ask whether anything is defamatory, invades privacy, or copies someone else's expression (Section 5).
13. **No conclusion.** A hard-news pyramid ends on its least important fact, not a summary or moral. (Features often end with a "kicker" instead; see Wikipedia: News style.)

#### Paragraph-role template (6 paragraphs)
| Para | Role | Usual content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lead | Most important W's: what happened, to whom, where, when |
| 2 | Support / source | How and why; attribution of key facts; second-tier facts |
| 3 | Consequence / response | Decisions, what happens next, who else is affected (often doubles as the nut graf) |
| 4 | Best quote / human element | Emotion, vivid detail, a person |
| 5 | Numbers / secondary details | Costs, closures, timelines |
| 6 | Background | History; least important; cut first |

**Raw reporter's notes (deliberately jumbled):**
- The robotics lab (Room 312) opened in 2023. The institute's last fire drill was in September.
- Damage is estimated at 180 million soums (facilities office). The lab is closed for at least two weeks. Classes in Building B resume Tuesday, except on the third floor.
- The fire began at about 2:15 p.m. Monday in Room 312, third floor, Engineering Building B, Chorsu Technical Institute (CTI), Tashkent.
- Preliminary cause: a robot's lithium-ion battery pack, left charging unattended on a workbench, overheated. Source: Capt. Aziz Rakhimov, the fire officer in charge.
- About 120 students and staff were evacuated. The alarm worked. Firefighters put the fire out within 20 minutes, and it did not spread beyond the lab.
- Two students were treated for smoke inhalation at the campus clinic and released within hours. Source: Dr. Malika Yusupova, clinic head.
- The fire destroyed a rescue robot built over five months by the four-student "Team Lochin" for the national student robotics championship, which starts in 11 days.
  - Captain Dilnoza Karimova, 20, a third-year mechatronics student: "We had 11 days left. Now we have a box of melted wires."
- Witness Jasur Ergashev, 19, a second-year student at the next bench, heard "a loud pop, then a hissing sound" before smoke filled the room.
- Dean of Engineering Bakhtiyor Usmonov announced Monday an immediate ban on unattended battery charging in all CTI labs, and fireproof charging cabinets for all 14 labs by December.
  - Usmonov: "Nobody was badly hurt, and we intend to keep it that way."
- Reporter's own observations on Tuesday: yellow tape across the door; black soot on the ceiling above the bench; a smell of burnt plastic in the corridor.

**Ranking by news value:**
1. Fire, evacuation and injuries: impact, timeliness, proximity.
2. The attributed cause: answers "how".
3. The new campus-wide ban: impact on every student; the forward-looking "what next".
4. The destroyed robot before the championship: human interest.
5. Damage and closures: consequence details.
6. Background: least important.

**The 6-paragraph story:**

> **(1)** A fire blamed on a charging robot battery forced about 120 people out of a Chorsu Technical Institute engineering building on Monday and sent two students to the campus clinic.
> *[30 words. What, who, where, when. "Blamed on" signals an official, preliminary explanation, not the reporter's finding.]*
>
> **(2)** The fire started at about 2:15 p.m. in the third-floor robotics lab of Engineering Building B, where a lithium-ion battery pack had been left charging unattended, said Capt. Aziz Rakhimov, the fire officer in charge. Firefighters put it out within 20 minutes, and it did not spread beyond the lab, he said. The two students were treated for smoke inhalation and released within hours, said Dr. Malika Yusupova, who runs the clinic.
> *[How and where in detail; two named sources; reassures readers on the injuries.]*
>
> **(3)** Hours after the fire, Dean of Engineering Bakhtiyor Usmonov banned unattended battery charging in all of the institute's labs and said fireproof charging cabinets would be installed in all 14 labs by December. "Nobody was badly hurt, and we intend to keep it that way," Usmonov said.
> *[Consequence for every reader; does the nut-graf job of explaining why it matters beyond one lab.]*
>
> **(4)** The fire destroyed a rescue robot that four students had spent five months building for the national student robotics championship, which begins in 11 days. "We had 11 days left. Now we have a box of melted wires," said team captain Dilnoza Karimova, 20, a third-year mechatronics student.
> *[Human interest; the best quote.]*
>
> **(5)** The institute's facilities office estimated the damage at 180 million soums. The lab will stay closed for at least two weeks; classes elsewhere in Building B resume Tuesday, except on the third floor.
> *[Secondary details.]*
>
> **(6)** The robotics lab opened in 2023. The institute held its last fire drill in September, and the building's alarm worked as designed, Usmonov said.
> *[Background: first to be cut.]*

**Why this order works:**
- **Cut test:** cut paragraph 6 and nothing essential is lost. Cut 5 and 6 and readers still know what happened, why, who was hurt, and what changes. Paragraph 1 alone works as a news brief.
- **Attribution:** the cause is attributed because it is preliminary. Every opinion is attributed with "said".
- **Quotes:** they carry emotion and the official stance, not raw facts.
- **AP style:** "2:15 p.m.", "two students", "14 labs", "Monday".

**Variation: the second-day (follow-up) story.** The next morning, the newest fact is the ban and the cabinets, so the lead moves forward in time:
> "Chorsu Technical Institute will install fireproof battery-charging cabinets in all 14 of its labs by December, after Monday's lab fire sent two students to the clinic." (26 words)

This applies the News Manual's "up-to-date" golden rule: future or present-perfect tense reads as news.

#### Inverted-pyramid checklist (exam-ready)
- [ ] The lead is the single most newsworthy point; about 20–30 words; active voice; NOT all 5 W's.
- [ ] Paragraph 2 supports or explains the lead and names the source.
- [ ] Each paragraph is less important than the one before (the cut test passes).
- [ ] A nut graf (why it matters / so what) appears by paragraph 2–4. It is mandatory after a soft lead.
- [ ] The best quote appears by paragraph 3–4. Quotes add emotion or opinion, not routine facts.
- [ ] Every opinion and disputed claim is attributed, with "said" as the default verb.
- [ ] Anyone criticized gets a response, and the story says what happens next.
- [ ] Background and history are at the bottom.
- [ ] Paragraphs are short (1–3 sentences), one idea each.
- [ ] AP style: day of week (not "yesterday"), numerals from 10 up, titles before names.
- [ ] Legal and ethical pass: provable facts, privacy, no copied expression.
- [ ] No concluding summary paragraph.

#### Common mistakes
- Chronological order ("At 2 p.m.... then... finally...") instead of importance order.
- Background (the history of the lab) in paragraph 2.
- An essential fact that appears only in the last paragraph, so the cut test fails.
- Quotes that repeat facts already given ("'There was a fire,' he said").
- Unattributed cause or blame. This is both a fairness problem and a legal risk.
- Using "claimed" or "stated" for "said" (News Manual), or editorial adjectives ("tragic", "shocking").
- A moralizing or summarizing "conclusion".

## 3. Lead types, lead rules, common lead mistakes, and where to begin a story

### Takeaway
The lead is the opening sentence or paragraph and the most important part of the story. Hard-news (summary) leads give the key W's at once in about 20–30 words. Soft or delayed leads (anecdotal, descriptive, question, quote, contrast, "you", staccato) hook the reader first and must be followed quickly by a nut graf. The classic mistakes: burying the lede; opening with the date, time or speaker instead of the news; overusing quote and question leads; passive voice; cramming in every W; and hype the story can't deliver.

### Cited Findings
**Definition and length**
- Purdue: "The lead, or opening paragraph, is the most important part of a news story." It should "give readers the most important information in a clear, concise and interesting manner." Leads are "often one sentence, sometimes two" and "generally 25 to 30 words and should rarely be more than 40." — [Purdue OWL: How to Write a Lead](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)
- News Manual Ch. 4–5:
  - Intros should be "no longer than 20 words" (KISS: Keep It Short and Simple), with one or two key points per sentence.
  - "Two short sentences are better than one long, crowded and confused sentence."
  - — [News Manual Ch. 4](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_04.htm); [News Manual Ch. 5](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_05.htm)
- Wikipedia (News style): ideally 20–25 words. It quotes Charnley (1966): a lead is "a brief, sharp statement of the story's essential facts". — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)
- Spelling: "lede" is a deliberate newsroom misspelling. The OED's earliest citation is 1951, and the spelling did not appear in journalism manuals until the 1980s. — [Wikipedia: Lead paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_paragraph)

**Lead types named in the fetched sources**
- Purdue:
  - **Summary lead:** "Two Hamilton County Commissioners plan to force the county's top administrator out of office today."
  - **Anecdotal lead:** "From Dan Ralescu's sun-warmed beach chair in Thailand, the Indian Ocean began to look, oddly, not so much like waves but bread dough."
  - **Question lead:** "What's increasing faster than the price of gasoline? Apparently, the cost of court lobbyists."
  - — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)
- Wikipedia:
  - **Hard lead:** gives the thesis up front.
  - **Soft lead:** a creative, attention-seeking opening, "typically followed by a nut graph".
  - Features "attempt to lure readers in" with anecdotal leads.
  - **1-2-3 lead:** see Section 2.
  - — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)
- Poynter: the anecdotal lead is typically followed immediately by the nut graf. — [Poynter: The nut graf](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/the-nut-graf-part-i/)

**Rules and mistakes**
- News Manual's five "golden rules", with its RIGHT/WRONG examples:
  1. **KISS.** RIGHT: "A Port Moresby union leader yesterday condemned politicians..." WRONG: "Mr Mug Wump, president of the Port Moresby Waterside Workers' Union, yesterday condemned..." Use a short general description in the lead and the full title in paragraph 2.
  2. **Active voice.** RIGHT: "Copra growers have demanded a new subsidy scheme." WRONG: "A new copra subsidy scheme has been demanded by growers." Exception: use the passive when the object is more newsworthy.
  3. **Facts first.** RIGHT: "Thieves broke into the Prime Minister's official residence last night, while he was attending a concert." WRONG: "While the Prime Minister was attending a concert last night, thieves broke into his official residence."
  4. **Up to date.** RIGHT: "Prince Charles will visit Tuvalu in August." WRONG: "It was announced in Funafuti last night that Prince Charles would visit Tuvalu in August."
  5. **No quotes.** "Never begin with direct quotation." RIGHT: "Income tax is to rise by seven percent in January." WRONG: the Finance Minister's direct quote.
  - Its six-question check: most newsworthy point? under 20 words? active voice? facts first? correct tense for currency? no opening quote?
  - — [News Manual Ch. 5](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_05.htm)
- Purdue:
  - Use active sentences with strong verbs. Be specific. Consider audience and context. Stay honest: the lead is "an implicit promise to your readers".
  - Avoid flowery language and excess adjectives and adverbs, unnecessary words, formulaic or mechanical leads, and starting with "it".
  - — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)
- **Burying the lede** means opening with background or secondary information instead of the essential point, so readers must dig for it. Wikipedia notes it is common in press releases. — [Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style); [Wikipedia: Lead paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_paragraph)
- **Time element (AP, via Purdue):** don't write "yesterday"; use the day of the week. — [Purdue OWL: AP Style](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/ap_style.html)

### Inferences
#### Lead-type catalogue: definition, when to use it, two examples each
*Examples marked "constructed" are invented for practice. For a real story, every fact and quote must be verified. Definitions for types 5–10 are standard journalism-course categories written up as synthesis; see Gaps.*

1. **Summary (hard-news) lead**
   - *Definition:* one sentence giving the most important W's (usually who/what, plus when/where) in about 30 words or fewer.
   - *Use for:* breaking news, accidents, decisions, results, crime, meetings, anything readers need fast.
   - *Examples:* (a) Purdue: "Two Hamilton County Commissioners plan to force the county's top administrator out of office today." (b) News Manual: "Six people were killed and more than 100 left homeless when Cyclone Victor hit the Solomon Islands yesterday." The News Manual (Commonwealth practice) uses "yesterday"; AP style would use the day of the week.
   - *Historic model (Poynter):* "The President was shot in a theater to-night and perhaps mortally wounded."
2. **Delayed (soft) lead**: the umbrella category.
   - *Definition:* holds back the main news for one to three sentences or paragraphs to hook the reader with a person, scene, puzzle or contrast, then delivers the point in a nut graf.
   - *Use for:* features, trends, profiles, second-day stories, and stories readers already know the basics of.
   - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "For 30 years the clock on the old station tower showed 4:17. On Friday it started moving again, thanks to three engineering students and a 3D printer." (b) "The email arrived at 2 a.m. with one line: 'Your server is on fire.' It was not a metaphor." Each needs a nut graf next.
3. **Anecdotal lead**
   - *Definition:* opens with a brief, true mini-story about one person that illustrates the larger issue.
   - *Use for:* human-interest pieces, trend stories, and policy stories where one person stands for many. Follow it immediately with a nut graf (Poynter).
   - *Examples:* (a) Purdue: "From Dan Ralescu's sun-warmed beach chair in Thailand, the Indian Ocean began to look, oddly, not so much like waves but bread dough." (b) Constructed: "Aziza Tursunova opens her laptop at 5 a.m., two hours before her bakery shift, to finish the coding course she hopes will get her out of it."
4. **Question lead**
   - *Definition:* opens with a question that the story answers, ideally at once.
   - *Use for:* sparingly, when the question is genuinely intriguing and answered in the next sentence.
   - *Risk:* readers answer "no" or "who cares?". It also delays the news.
   - *Examples:* (a) Purdue: "What's increasing faster than the price of gasoline? Apparently, the cost of court lobbyists." (b) Constructed: "How hot does a phone get on a Tashkent rooftop in July? Hot enough to shut itself down in 11 minutes, a student experiment found."
5. **Quote lead**
   - *Definition:* opens with a direct quotation.
   - *Use for:* rarely, when the quote is short, extraordinary, and captures the story better than any paraphrase. The News Manual's hard-news rule is "Never begin with direct quotation."
   - *Risk:* the reader doesn't know who is speaking or why it matters, and dull quotes waste the most valuable sentence.
   - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "'I didn't build it to win. I built it so my grandmother could hear the phone,' said Bekzod Alimov, 17, whose $12 hearing amplifier took first prize at Saturday's Young Inventors Fair." (b) "'Don't drink the tap water,' read the text message 4,000 residents received at dawn Tuesday."
6. **Contrast lead**
   - *Definition:* sets then against now, expectation against reality, or one extreme against another, to highlight change or irony.
   - *Use for:* change, trends, turnarounds, irony.
   - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "The bridge took four years to build. It took four minutes to close." (b) "In 2016 the faculty owned one 3D printer. Today it owns 40, and students still wait a week to use one."
7. **Descriptive (scene-setting) lead**
   - *Definition:* paints a concrete scene with sensory detail before giving the news.
   - *Use for:* features, aftermath stories, and stories where the place is central. Details must be observed or sourced, never imagined.
   - *Examples:* (a) Built only from the sourced hornet timeline in Section 4: "Two and a half metres up a tree near Blaine, Washington, hidden in a hollow, sat a nest of about 500 live Asian giant hornets, including some 200 queens." (b) Constructed: "Dust hangs in the afternoon light of the old textile mill, where 200 looms once ran around the clock."
8. **"You" (direct-address) lead**
   - *Definition:* speaks directly to the reader to show personal relevance.
   - *Use for:* service and consumer stories, and rules or policies that affect readers.
   - *Risk:* gimmicky if the story does not actually affect "you".
   - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "If you bike to campus, you will have 300 new parking spaces by spring." (b) "Your student ID card will soon open more than doors: from January it will also pay for lunch."
9. **Staccato / punch lead** (bonus)
   - *Definition:* short fragments that build rhythm, followed immediately by the explanation.
   - *Use for:* dramatic events and features only.
   - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "Twelve hours. Three hundred volunteers. One flooded basement." (b) "A pop. A hiss. Then smoke." (See the fact set below.)
10. **Startling-statement / statistic lead** (bonus)
    - *Definition:* opens with a surprising fact or number.
    - *Use for:* data and research stories. The number must be accurate and put in context.
    - *Examples (constructed):* (a) "One in three first-year engineering students had never written a report longer than one page, a faculty survey found." (b) "A phone app built by two students can spot a cracked bridge beam faster than a trained inspector, according to their field tests."

#### Lead-length rules, reconciled
- The sources differ: News Manual says 20 words or fewer; Wikipedia says 20–25; Purdue says 25–30, rarely over 40.
- **Exam-safe rule:** one sentence, ideally about 20–25 words, never more than about 35. Two short sentences beat one crowded sentence.
- **Cut from the lead first:** full names and titles of little-known people (write "a union leader" and give the name in paragraph 2), exact dates, addresses, ages, and secondary W's.

#### Lead checklist
1. Does it state the single most newsworthy point? For a soft lead, is the hook tied directly to that point?
2. Is it about 20–30 words, with every word earning its place?
3. Active voice, concrete subject, strong verb (not "is", "held", "took place")?
4. Facts first, not the date, time, place, speaker's name or title, "it", or "there is"?
5. Up to date: present perfect or future tense where accurate ("has banned", "will install")?
6. No opening direct quote, unless it is exceptional and the piece is a feature?
7. Are accusations, causes and opinions attributed ("blamed on", "police said")? Are there any unverified claims that could be defamatory?
8. Specific rather than vague ("120 people", not "many people")?
9. Honest: does the body deliver what the lead promises, with no hype?
10. For a soft lead: does the nut graf arrive by paragraph 2–4?

#### Common lead mistakes and fixes (constructed examples)
- **Burying the lede.**
  - Before: "The Student Council met Monday in Room 105 to discuss the cafeteria, the library and parking..."
  - After: "The library will stay open until midnight starting next month after the Student Council voted 9–2 on Monday to extend its hours."
- **Starting with the date or time.**
  - Before: "On Monday, Oct. 19, at 10 a.m., ..."
  - After: move the time element after the verb ("...voted Monday...").
- **Overused quote lead.**
  - Before: "'It was a very productive meeting,' said the rector."
  - After: lead with what was decided.
- **Empty question lead.**
  - Before: "Have you ever wondered about recycling?"
  - After: lead with the news: "Students recycled 4 tonnes of plastic in September, double the campus target."
- **Speaker-first (name and title) lead.**
  - Before: "Professor Akmal Nazarov, head of the Department of Electrical Engineering, said Tuesday that..."
  - After: "Final-year electrical engineering students will be able to write their theses at partner companies from spring, the department head said Tuesday."
- **Agenda or "process" lead.** "A meeting was held..." or "A study was conducted..." Fix: lead with the decision or the finding.
- **Cramming in all 5 W's.** Pick the top two; the rest go in paragraphs 2–3.
- **Passive voice.**
  - Before: "A new policy was approved by the council."
  - After: "The council approved..."
- **Starting with "it" or "there".** Purdue flags "it" as imprecise and disorienting.
- **Unattributed blame.**
  - Before: "A careless student started the fire." This is a defamation and fairness risk.
  - After: "Officials blamed a battery left charging unattended."

#### Where to begin a story (synthesized from the News Manual's "facts first" and "up-to-date" rules, Purdue's most-important-W rule, Poynter's nut graf and MHM's "Is there a story?")
- **Begin with what changed.** Lead with the newest consequential fact (the decision, result, finding or injury), not the process that led to it.
- **Meetings:** begin with the decision or its effect, not "The council met...".
- **Speeches and press conferences:** begin with the most newsworthy thing said, paraphrased, not "X gave a speech".
- **Research and engineering stories:** begin with the finding and why it matters to people ("A phone app can spot bridge cracks..."), not with the method ("Using a convolutional neural network, researchers...").
- **Features:** begin with the moment or person that best embodies the story's point (an anecdote or scene), then the nut graf. Start as close to the action as possible, not with background.
- **Follow-ups:** begin with the newest development (reaction, outcome, next step), not a rerun of the original news. The News Manual's Ch. 24 example uses the opposition's reaction to a tax as the new angle.
- **Quick tests:**
  - *Tell-a-friend:* what would you say first to a friend?
  - *Headline:* write the headline first; the lead must deliver it.
  - *Cut:* would the story survive if only paragraph 1 ran?

## 4. Finding original story ideas and narrowing a broad topic into a tight angle

### Takeaway
A topic ("climate change", "AI", "murder hornets") is not a story. A story is a specific, new, answerable question about particular people, a place and a change, with a clear "so what?". Ideas come from:
- curiosity and follow-ups;
- records and data;
- people overlooked at public meetings;
- localizing national or global news.

An angle gets tighter as you narrow *who, where, when and what's new*. Test it with "so what?", then add numbers and a second source.

### Cited Findings
- **The "so what" factor** (MHM, David Brewer, 2017, updated 2025):
  - An observation alone is thin journalism: "Numbers, second sources, and added information provide the context that turns an observation into a piece of journalism."
  - Dump example: young people making a living from a city dump becomes a story once the reporter asks how long production takes, what the margins are, how many items sell daily, and what a day's earnings buy. Answer: one day's work feeds a family of four for a week.
  - Flood example: adding a health official as a second source lifts a personal tragedy into a possible public-health story.
  - — [MHM: The 'so what' factor](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/news-sources-numbers-and-the-so-what-factor/)
- **Developing angles** (MHM, David Brewer, 2012, modified 2026). Ten strategies:
  - cultivate curiosity (ask "why?" and "what if?");
  - question press releases;
  - build source relationships;
  - follow financial trails (public records, corporate filings);
  - use data journalism;
  - ask difficult questions;
  - collaborate;
  - stay persistent;
  - think systemically about long-term implications;
  - maintain ethics.
  - Key questions: "Who is benefiting from this narrative?" and "What information is missing?"
  - — [MHM: Developing important news angles](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/how-to-find-and-develop-important-news-angles/)
- **From a small moment to an original story** (MHM, 2025, a fictional case study):
  - At a routine city council meeting, an elderly woman is silenced. The reporter follows her and hears about discoloured water and stomach problems.
  - Verification: public-health websites, neighbourhood visits, water samples, the utility, a Freedom of Information request, a local nurse.
  - Then: social-media complaints, data analysis of three years of water tests, experts, a whistleblower.
  - The angle sharpens from one complaint into "a systemic failure in public infrastructure".
  - — [MHM: The evolution of an original news story](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/the-evolution-of-an-original-news-story/)
- **Follow-ups** (News Manual Ch. 24):
  - Review stories from six months, one year and five years ago: "There will be many stories which have developed since, but you have not covered recently."
  - Use anniversaries: "If a politician promised action a year ago, now is the time to ask him what he has achieved."
  - Ask yourself, "Is there anything else I want to know about it?"
  - Find *new* sources rather than returning to the original ones.
  - Example: the opposition's reaction to a consumption tax becomes the new angle.
  - — [News Manual Ch. 24](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_24.htm)
- **Basis for localizing:** Purdue's relevance and proximity values (e.g., California earthquakes matter more to West Coast readers). The News Manual: "The same event can have different levels of interest in different societies." — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/components_of_newsworthiness.html); [News Manual Ch. 1](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_01.htm)
- **Choosing an angle includes deciding what to leave out** (MHM). — [MHM: News writing for beginners](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/news-writing-tips-for-beginners/)
- **Sourced background for the class's "murder hornets" example:**
  - A 2 May 2020 *New York Times* article by Mike Baker, "'Murder Hornets' in the U.S.: The Rush to Stop the Asian Giant Hornet", helped popularize the nickname.
  - 2019: hornets found in Nanaimo, British Columbia (August), and a nest there was destroyed. Single workers were found in Blaine, Washington (late September, October, December).
  - October 2020: a nest 2.5 m above ground in a tree cavity in Blaine, with about 500 live specimens, including about 200 queens.
  - 2021: three more nests near Blaine (one in August, two in September).
  - July 2022: the Entomological Society of America adopted "northern giant hornet" as the official common name, citing concerns about "potentially discriminatory language".
  - December 2024: the Washington State Department of Agriculture declared the hornet eradicated.
  - — [Wikipedia: Asian giant hornet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_giant_hornet)

### Inferences
#### The funnel: Topic → Issue → Angle → Story question → Lead
1. **Topic**, a broad subject area: "air pollution".
2. **Issue**, a problem or tension within it: "indoor air in crowded classrooms".
3. **Angle**, a specific, new, local, human focus in one sentence: "Afternoon lectures in our oldest building may exceed indoor CO2 guidelines, and no one is measuring."
4. **Story question**, answerable by reporting before deadline: "What do CO2 sensors in Building A's largest hall read at 9 a.m. vs 3 p.m. this week, and what does the facilities office say?"
5. **So what?** Who is affected, how, and why now?
6. **Sources and data**: documents, measurements, and people on all sides.
7. **One-sentence pitch / lead** (25 words or fewer), plus a working headline.

#### Methods for finding original ideas (synthesis of the sources above)
- **Localize** national or global news. Who here is affected? What do local numbers show? Which local person lives it?
- **Follow up** old stories: anniversaries, promises, court dates, "what happened next?" (News Manual).
- **Follow the money or data**: budgets, procurement, public records, sensor or usage data (MHM).
- **Listen at the edges**: the person cut off at a meeting, complaints under official posts (MHM's water case).
- **Ask the MHM questions**: "Who benefits?" "What's missing?"
- **Look for change and conflict**: what is different from last year, and who disagrees?
- **Find people, not topics**: the person living the issue (human interest).
- **Engineering-specific ideas**:
  - test a claim (battery life, Wi-Fi speed, energy use);
  - explain how a local system works (metro signalling, water treatment);
  - profile a student build;
  - turn a lab result into "what it means for people".

#### Angle-tightening tests
- **So what?** If you can't answer it in one sentence, narrow further.
- **What's new?** Find the news peg: something just happened, is about to happen, or has just been found or revealed.
- **Who exactly?** A named group or person, not "people" or "society".
- **Can you report it by deadline?** Can you name three sources right now?
- **Is it one story?** If the pitch joins two different questions with "and", split it.

#### Worked example 1: "Murder hornets" (the class's sample topic; sourced facts above)
- **Topic:** murder hornets. This is too broad: biology, invasion, media hype, eradication and beekeeping are all separate stories.
- **Angle A, news peg plus explanation:** how Washington went from its first sighting (2019) to an eradication declaration (Dec 2024).
  - *Story question:* "How were the four Washington nests (one in 2020, three in 2021) found and destroyed, and what do state officials say made eradication possible?"
  - *Sources:* WSDA announcements, entomologists, beekeepers.
  - *Possible lead:* "Five years after the first Asian giant hornet turned up near Blaine, Washington, state officials say the insect once dubbed the 'murder hornet' is gone." Check WSDA's exact wording before publishing.
- **Angle B, media and ethics:** the nickname itself.
  - *Story question:* "How did the 'murder hornet' label spread after May 2020, and why did entomologists replace it with 'northern giant hornet' in 2022?"
  - This connects to news values (sensationalism, novelty) and to the SPJ Code ("Avoid stereotyping"; "Identify misinformation and disinformation").
- **Angle C, localized to Uzbekistan:**
  - *Story question:* "Which hornets threaten beekeepers in the Tashkent region, and who, if anyone, monitors invasive insects?"
  - *Sources:* local beekeepers, university entomologists, plant-quarantine authorities.
  - These notes contain no facts about hornets in Uzbekistan. This is a question to report, not a claim.

#### Worked example 2: "Artificial intelligence"
- **Topic → issue → angle:** AI → AI in university coursework → first-year programming labs changing how they grade because students use AI assistants.
- **Story question:** "Since September, how have instructors of [named course] changed programming assignments and grading in response to AI tools, and what do students say?"
- **So what:** it affects every first-year engineering student's grade.
- **Sources:** syllabi and policy documents, two instructors, five students, an academic-integrity officer.
- **Possible lead (constructed):** "First-year programming students at [university] now defend their code out loud, a change instructors say is meant to make AI-written homework pointless."
- **Avoid:** "AI is changing education". That is a topic, not a story.

#### Worked example 3: "Air pollution / climate"
- **Topic → issue → angle:** air pollution → indoor air quality → CO2 build-up in crowded lecture halls. It is measurable, which suits an engineering course.
- **Story question:** "What do CO2 readings in the three largest lecture halls show across one teaching day this week, and does ventilation meet the guideline the facilities office uses?"
- **So what:** concentration and health for thousands of students.
- **Sources:**
  - your own sensor readings (state the method);
  - the facilities manager;
  - an environmental-engineering lecturer;
  - the guideline document itself (verify the threshold before stating it).
- **Possible lead (constructed):** "By the end of a 3 p.m. lecture in Hall 101, carbon dioxide levels were more than double their 9 a.m. reading, student measurements this week showed."

#### Worked example 4: "Electric mobility / batteries" (spinning off the Section 2 fire)
- **Topic → issue → angle:** electric scooters → battery-fire safety → overnight e-scooter charging in student dormitories.
- **Story question:** "How many e-scooters are charged inside the dormitories each night, what are the rules, and are they enforced?"
- **So what:** a fire risk where students sleep. The news peg is the (fictional) lab fire and the new charging ban.
- **Sources:** dorm administration, the fire service, students who ride, a battery-engineering lecturer.
- **Possible lead (constructed):** "Days after a battery fire emptied an engineering building, scooters are still charging overnight in dormitory hallways, where no rule covers them."

#### Quick "so what?" drill (constructed, modelled on MHM)
- **Observation:** "Many students ride e-scooters to campus."
- **So what?** Add numbers (how many scooters, which entrance, how many injuries the clinic has treated) and a second source (the security chief, a clinic doctor).
- **Result:** "Scooter injuries treated at the campus clinic doubled this semester, the clinic says, as rental scooters arrived at the main gate."

#### Common mistakes
- Pitching a topic ("I'll write about pollution") instead of a question.
- Angles too big to report by deadline, or with no human face.
- Localizing by name only, without local facts, numbers or people.
- Calling something a "trend" on the strength of two anecdotes. Get the numbers.
- Repeating the original story in a follow-up instead of reporting the new development.

## 5. Media law and ethics for writers: defamation, privacy, copyright vs fair use, the SPJ Code, jurisdiction differences, and Uzbekistan

### Takeaway
**Defamation** is a false statement of fact that identifies someone, is published to a third party, and harms their reputation. Libel is written or recorded; slander is spoken. The core defences are truth, opinion and privilege.
- **US:** public officials and figures must prove "actual malice" (NYT v. Sullivan, 1964).
- **Commonwealth systems, and Uzbekistan's civil law:** the publisher must prove truth.

**Privacy** in US law means four torts: intrusion, private facts, false light and appropriation.

**Copyright** protects expression, not facts or ideas. Fair use (US, four factors) and fair dealing (Commonwealth) allow limited use. Crediting a source is not permission.

**Ethics** (the SPJ Code) asks more of you than the law does.

**Uzbekistan:**
- slander and insult are administrative offences;
- they become crimes for repeat offenders or when published in the media or online;
- publicly insulting or slandering the President is a separate crime;
- the 2023 Constitution guarantees free speech and bans censorship.

### Cited Findings
#### 5.1 Defamation: definitions and elements
- **Definition (Cornell LII):** "Defamation is a statement that harms another person's reputation." It covers libel ("written or otherwise recorded statements") and slander ("spoken statements"). — [Cornell LII Wex: Defamation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)
- **What the plaintiff generally must show (LII):**
  - a false statement presented as fact;
  - communication to a third party;
  - the required degree of fault;
  - reputational harm or legally cognizable damage.
  - — [Cornell LII Wex: Defamation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)
- **Defamation per se:** some categories are treated as inherently harmful, so damages can be presumed. Traditional examples are accusations of a serious crime, a stigmatized disease, serious sexual misconduct, or professional incompetence. — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)
- **The six US elements (DMLP):**
  1. **Publication** to at least one person other than the plaintiff.
  2. **"Of and concerning"** the plaintiff. Someone can be identified without being named if "those who know the person will recognize the statement".
  3. **Defamatory meaning**: it exposes the person to "hatred, ridicule or contempt" or "lowers him in the esteem of his peers".
  4. **Falsity**: "substantially true" statements are protected even if minor details are wrong.
  5. **Fault**: actual malice for public figures and officials; negligence for private figures in many states.
  6. **No applicable privilege.**
  - — [DMLP: Defamation](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation)
- **The Commonwealth approach (News Manual Ch. 69):**
  - Three elements: defamatory words, identification, publication.
  - Quoting someone else is **no defence**.
  - The **burden of proof is on the journalist** to prove the words true.
  - Innuendo, irony, gestures and cartoons can all defame. Joking doesn't protect you.
  - Vague allegations about a group can lead to several claims.
  - — [News Manual Ch. 69](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_69.htm)

#### 5.2 Defamation: defences
- **Truth.** "Truth is generally a complete defense to a defamation claim" (LII). Substantial truth is enough (DMLP). — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation); [DMLP: Privileges and defenses](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation-privileges-and-defenses)
- **Opinion (LII):** statements that cannot reasonably be read as asserting verifiable facts are protected. — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)
- **Opinion (DMLP):**
  - "Statements of pure opinion, which cannot be proven true or false, cannot form the basis of a defamation claim."
  - But "in my opinion, the mayor killed her husband" is *not* protected, because readers assume hidden facts.
  - Stating the observed facts first, and then the opinion, lets readers judge for themselves (DMLP's "Carol must be an alcoholic" example).
  - Rhetorical hyperbole in satire is protected.
  - — [DMLP: Opinion and fair comment](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/opinion-and-fair-comment-privileges)
- ***Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.***, 497 U.S. 1 (21 June 1990, 7–2):
  - There is no separate constitutional privilege for anything labelled "opinion". "In my opinion John Jones is a liar" can imply facts and harm reputation.
  - Statements on matters of public concern must be "provable as false" before liability attaches.
  - "Rhetorical hyperbole" is protected.
  - — [Cornell LII: Milkovich](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/497/1)
- **Privilege (LII):**
  - *Absolute privilege* covers statements made in judicial and legislative proceedings.
  - *Qualified privilege* covers communications where speaker and recipient share a duty or interest.
  - — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)
- **Other US defences (DMLP):**
  - *Fair report privilege*: may apply when you rely on a public document or an official's statement and present it fairly.
  - *Neutral reportage*: may cover accurately reporting accusations between public figures on public-interest issues.
  - *Wire-service defence*: may apply when you republish a reputable source such as AP.
  - *Statutes of limitations*: bar late claims.
  - — [DMLP: Privileges and defenses](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation-privileges-and-defenses)
- **Commonwealth defences (News Manual Ch. 70):**
  - *Truth / justification*. Some countries require truth *plus* public interest.
  - *Fair comment*: an honestly held opinion with a reasonable factual basis. Harsh language is allowed.
  - *Privilege*: fair, accurate and contemporaneous reports of parliament, the courts and, in many countries, public meetings and official records.
  - *Public interest*.
  - *Consent*. "No comment" is not consent.
  - **Malice** (ill will, revenge, scandal-mongering) defeats these defences.
  - *Unintentional defamation* may be defended if you took reasonable precautions and promptly corrected and apologised.
  - A *serious-harm threshold* applies in the UK (since 2013) and in NSW and Victoria (since 1 July 2021).
  - Don't admit fault to a complainant without legal advice.
  - — [News Manual Ch. 70](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_70.htm)

#### 5.3 Public figures and actual malice (US background)
- ***New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*** (1964): public officials must prove "actual malice", meaning publication "with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not."
- ***Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*** (1967) extended the rule to public figures.
- ***Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*** (1974): private plaintiffs need not meet the Sullivan standard, and states may impose negligence instead.
- — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)

#### 5.4 Invasion of privacy (US: four torts)
- **The four torts:** intrusion on seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light, and appropriation. They share "the common thread of an unwelcome or unauthorized intrusion upon a person's private life." — [Cornell LII Wex: Invasion of privacy](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/invasion_of_privacy); [DMLP: False light](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/false-light)
- **Public disclosure of private facts (DMLP). The four elements:**
  1. Public disclosure: to the public at large, or to so many people that it will likely become public knowledge.
  2. A private fact: "an intimate detail of one's private life that is not generally known".
  3. "Highly offensive to a reasonable person of ordinary sensibilities."
  4. Not newsworthy: not a matter of "legitimate public concern".
  - Typical private facts: medical conditions, sexual orientation and history, financial status, unlisted ID or phone numbers.
  - Newsworthiness is read broadly (crimes, accidents, public figures' lives), but it does not cover "a morbid and sensational prying into private lives for its own sake".
  - Accurately publishing information from public court records is protected.
  - It is state law, so it varies from state to state.
  - — [DMLP: Publication of private facts](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/publication-private-facts)
- **False light (DMLP, citing Restatement (Second) of Torts §652E):**
  - Elements: wide publication; it identifies the plaintiff; it places them in a false light "highly offensive to a reasonable person"; the publisher was at fault.
  - It protects against emotional distress, whereas defamation protects reputation.
  - Not all states recognize it (California does).
  - Example: a stock street photo used in a story about prostitution implies that a random passer-by was a client.
  - — [DMLP: False light](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/false-light)
- **Misappropriation / right of publicity (DMLP):**
  - Using "an individual's name, likeness, or other personal attributes without permission for an exploitative purpose", typically in ads or promotion.
  - Many states exempt news reporting.
  - A rival's photo in your advertising creates liability. The same photo in a news article generally does not.
  - — [DMLP: Publishing personal and private information](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/publishing-personal-and-private-information)
- **Ethics view (News Manual Ch. 62):**
  - Intruding on private life may be justified when it reveals character relevant to public duty, involves public funds, or creates a risk of blackmail.
  - People who seek public positions accept more scrutiny than those thrust into the news.
  - "The media should respect the privacy of even the most prominent public figure if a loved one dies."
  - — [News Manual Ch. 62](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_62.htm)

#### 5.5 Copyright vs fair use
- **What copyright does not protect:** "Copyright does not protect facts, ideas, systems, or methods of operation, although it may protect the way these things are expressed." It also does not protect "names, titles, slogans, or short phrases." — [U.S. Copyright Office FAQ: What does copyright protect?](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-protect.html)
- **Fair use** is "a legal doctrine that promotes freedom of expression by permitting the unlicensed use of copyright-protected works in certain circumstances." The purposes listed in Section 107 are criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research. — [U.S. Copyright Office: Fair Use](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)
- **The four factors (17 U.S.C. §107), as the Copyright Office explains them:**
  1. **Purpose and character.** Nonprofit, educational and noncommercial uses are favoured. Commercial use is not automatically unfair. "Transformative" uses are favoured.
  2. **Nature of the work.** Creative works get more protection than factual ones. Unpublished works are less likely to be fair use.
  3. **Amount and substantiality.** Small portions favour fair use. Taking the "heart" of the work weighs against it.
  4. **Market effect.** Harm to the existing or potential market for the original.
  - "There is no formula to ensure that a predetermined percentage or amount of a work—or specific number of words, lines, pages, copies—may be used without permission."
  - — [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)
- **Copyright Office FAQ:**
  - Limited portions, including quotes, may be used for "commentary, criticism, news reporting, and scholarly reports".
  - There are "no legal rules permitting the use of a specific number of words".
  - "In cases of doubt, the Copyright Office recommends that permission be obtained."
  - — [Copyright Office FAQ: Fair use](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html)
- **Key US cases (LII):**
  - Fair use is an affirmative defence.
  - *Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises* (1985): about 300 words taken from an *unpublished* work were "substantial" and weighed against fair use. This is the classic journalism case: a magazine scooped a forthcoming memoir.
  - *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* (1994): asks whether the new work is transformative, adding "new expression, meaning, or message".
  - — [Cornell LII Wex: Fair use](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/fair_use)
- ***Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*** (18 May 2023, 7–2):
  - Licensing Warhol's "Orange Prince" to Condé Nast for a magazine served substantially the same purpose as Goldsmith's photo, so factor one did not favour fair use.
  - Syllabus: "The 'purpose and character' of AWF's use of Goldsmith's photograph in commercially licensing Orange Prince to Condé Nast does not favor AWF's fair use defense to copyright infringement."
  - — [Cornell LII: Warhol v. Goldsmith](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-869)
- **Commonwealth practice (News Manual Ch. 73):**
  - Copyright covers "the way the news is written", not the news itself. "Copyright does not cover ideas until they have been put in some permanent form."
  - Duration is typically 50 or 70 years after the author's death.
  - **Fair dealing** allows short extracts with attribution.
  - You may report what another paper claimed if you name it. Copying the whole story infringes.
  - Photos need permission from their owner.
  - The Berne Convention extends protection across member states.
  - "Simply rearranging the paragraphs is not enough to avoid copyright."
  - — [News Manual Ch. 73](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_73.htm)

#### 5.6 Ethics: the SPJ Code of Ethics
- **The four principles:** **Seek Truth and Report It · Minimize Harm · Act Independently · Be Accountable and Transparent.** — [SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
- **What the code says about itself:** it "is not a set of rules, but rather a guide"; it "should be considered as a whole"; and it "is not, nor can it be under the First Amendment, legally enforceable." — [SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
- **Selected bullets from the current (2026) text:**
  - **Seek Truth:**
    - "Ethical journalism should be accurate, fair and in context."
    - "Take responsibility for the accuracy of their work before releasing it."
    - "Remember that speed, technology or medium do not excuse inaccuracy or unfairness."
    - "Use original sources whenever possible."
    - "Reserve anonymity or confidentiality as a last resort for sources with information of substantial public interest who may face danger, retribution or other harm."
    - "Without exception, diligently provide fair opportunity for subjects of news coverage to respond to criticism or allegations of wrongdoing."
    - "Avoid stereotyping."
    - "Identify misinformation and disinformation. Challenge provable falsehoods and avoid uncritically repeating them. Avoid false equivalence."
    - "Label opinion journalism and news analysis."
    - "Never publish fabricated content that could be confused with reality."
    - "Never plagiarize. Always attribute."
  - **Minimize Harm:**
    - "Balance the public's need for information against potential harm."
    - "Use heightened sensitivity when dealing with crime victims (especially victims of sex crimes), juveniles, and sources or subjects who are inexperienced or unable to give consent."
    - "Respect the presumption of innocence while a criminal case is being resolved."
    - "Recognize that legal access to information differs from the ethical justification for reporting it."
    - "Private people have a greater right to control information about themselves than public figures and others who seek power, influence or attention."
    - "Avoid pandering to lurid curiosity, even if others do."
  - **Act Independently:**
    - "Avoid conflicts of interest, real or perceived."
    - "Refuse presents, favors, fees, free travel or special treatment aimed at unduly influencing coverage."
    - "Do not pay sources in exchange for interviews or information."
    - "Distinguish news from advertising and shun hybrids that blur the lines between the two. Prominently label sponsored content and partnerships."
  - **Be Accountable and Transparent:**
    - "Explain ethical choices and processes to audiences."
    - "Clearly identify the sources and technology that shape the creation of news."
    - "Acknowledge mistakes. Correct them promptly and prominently."
    - "Expose unethical conduct in journalism, including within their organizations."
    - "Abide by the same high standards they expect of others."
  - — [SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
- **History:** Sigma Delta Chi's first code (1926) was borrowed from the American Society of Newspaper Editors. In 1973 it wrote its own code, "which was revised in 1984, 1987, 1996, 2014 and 2026" (live code page). — [SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
- **The 2026 revision (SPJ revision-project page):**
  - The proposal keeps the four principles unchanged.
  - It adds emphasis on character (integrity, curiosity, conscientiousness, humanity, humility).
  - It adds guidance on verifying where information comes from and whether it is authentic, and on human responsibility for work made with any technology, including AI.
  - It addresses misinformation and disinformation, and updates language on power and marginalization.
  - It clarifies guidance on fabricated images, sponsored content, crime coverage, anonymity, and news vs analysis vs opinion.
  - Timeline: the board vote was scheduled for 25 Sept 2026, with members voting online 1–4 Oct 2026.
  - "The 2014 Code remains in effect unless and until SPJ members approve a revision."
  - — [SPJ Ethics Code Revision Project 2026](https://www.spj.org/spj-ethics-code-revision-project-2026/)
- **Purdue's ethics summary (drawing on AP and SPJ):**
  - "Check the accuracy of information from all sources to avoid error."
  - "When mistakes are made, they must be corrected – fully and quickly."
  - Grant anonymity only when the information is vital and there is no alternative.
  - "Never plagiarize."
  - "Always refuse gifts, favors, fees, free travel and special treatment."
  - Private individuals have greater privacy expectations.
  - Be cautious about identifying juvenile suspects.
  - — [Purdue OWL: Media Ethics](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/media_ethics.html)

#### 5.7 Uzbekistan: legal framework (background; primary texts on lex.uz, translations below are mine and unofficial)
- **Constitution (new edition, 30 Apr 2023):**
  - **Art. 33.** Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, speech and belief, and the right to seek, receive and disseminate any information. The state creates conditions for Internet access. The right to seek, receive and disseminate information may be restricted only by law, and only as far as necessary to:
    - protect the constitutional order, public health, public morality, and the rights and freedoms of others;
    - ensure public security and order;
    - prevent disclosure of state secrets or other secrets protected by law.
  - **Art. 81.** Mass media are free and operate in accordance with law. The state guarantees their freedom of activity and their rights to seek, receive, use and disseminate information. "Mass media are responsible for the reliability of the information they provide."
  - **Art. 82.** "Censorship is not permitted." Obstructing or interfering with mass media's activity entails liability under law.
  - **Art. 31.** Rights to:
    - inviolability of private life, personal and family secrets, and protection of honour and dignity;
    - secrecy of correspondence, phone conversations and electronic messages, restrictable only by law and a court decision;
    - protection of personal data, and correction or destruction of inaccurate or unlawfully collected data.
  - — [lex.uz: Constitution of the Republic of Uzbekistan (2023)](https://lex.uz/docs/6445145)
- **Code of Administrative Responsibility:**
  - **Art. 40 "Slander" (туҳмат):** knowingly spreading false fabrications that disgrace another person. Fine of 20–60 times the base calculation amount (BCA). Sanction as amended by Law ZRU-586 of 3 Dec 2019.
  - **Art. 41 "Insult" (ҳақорат қилиш):** intentional humiliation of a person's honour and dignity. Fine of 20–40 BCA.
  - — [lex.uz: Code of Administrative Responsibility](https://lex.uz/docs/97664)
- **Criminal Code, Art. 139 "Slander" (клевета):**
  - **Part 1:** spreading knowingly false, discrediting fabrications "committed after an administrative penalty for the same actions". Fine up to 200 BCA, up to 300 hours of community service, or up to 2 years of correctional labour.
  - **Part 2:** slander in print or other reproduced form, "including in mass media, telecommunication networks or the World Wide Web (Internet)". Fine of 200–400 BCA, 300–360 hours of community service, 2–3 years of correctional labour, or up to 1 year's restriction of liberty. Wording as amended 25 Dec 2020.
  - **Part 3 (aggravated):** combined with an accusation of a grave crime, causing grave consequences, by a dangerous recidivist, or from mercenary or base motives. Fine of 300–500 BCA, 360–400 hours of community service, or 1–3 years' restriction of liberty.
  - — [lex.uz: Criminal Code](https://lex.uz/docs/111457)
- **Criminal Code, Art. 140 "Insult" (оскорбление):**
  - **Part 1:** intentional humiliation of honour and dignity "in an indecent form", after an administrative penalty. Fine up to 200 BCA, up to 240 hours of community service, or up to 1 year of correctional labour.
  - **Part 2:** in print, the media, telecom networks or the Internet. Fine of 200–400 BCA, 240–300 hours of community service, or 1–2 years of correctional labour.
  - **Aggravated forms:** e.g., insult connected with the victim's official or civic duty. The penalties for these were not captured.
  - — [lex.uz: Criminal Code](https://lex.uz/docs/111457)
- **Criminal Code, Art. 158 Part 3:** "Public insult or slander against the President of the Republic of Uzbekistan", including through print or other mass media, telecom networks or the Internet. Up to 3 years of correctional labour, 2–5 years' restriction of liberty, or up to 5 years' imprisonment. The current wording dates from Law ZRU-679 of 30 Mar 2021. — [lex.uz: Criminal Code](https://lex.uz/docs/111457)
- **Civil law:**
  - Civil Code Art. 100 protects honour, dignity and business reputation. Remedies include refutation, a right of reply, and damages. — [lex.uz: Civil Code](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111189)
  - **Supreme Court Plenum Resolution No. 21 (24 Nov 2025)**, on protecting personal non-property rights:
    - **The defendant must prove truth:** "Обязанность доказывания соответствия действительности распространенных сведений в силу статьи 100 ГК возлагается на ответчика" (under Civil Code Art. 100, the defendant bears the burden of proving that the disseminated information is true).
    - **Opinion vs fact:** opinions and value judgements cannot be ordered refuted; the remedy is a right of reply. Opinions expressed in a degrading form can amount to actionable insult.
    - **Media:** they must publish refutations in the same outlet within one month.
    - **Online:** website owners and bloggers must "immediately remove false information".
    - **Moral-damage awards** weigh severity, fault, audience size, the plaintiff's position, and the balance between free expression and reputation.
    - The fetched summary found no explicit rule that public figures must tolerate more criticism.
    - — [lex.uz: Plenum Resolution No. 21 (2025)](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/7901166)
- **Copyright:**
  - Law on Copyright and Related Rights (No. 42 of 20 Jul 2006, amended through 21 Aug 2021).
  - Uzbekistan is party to the Berne Convention (in force for Uzbekistan since 19 Apr 2005) and to the WIPO Copyright Treaty and the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty (17 Jul 2019).
  - — [WIPO Lex: Uzbekistan](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/members/profile/UZ)
- **Other laws Freedom House mentions:** Law on Mass Media (1997, amended 2007), Law on Informatization (2014 amendments), Law on Personal Data. — [Freedom House FOTN 2024: Uzbekistan](https://freedomhouse.org/country/uzbekistan/freedom-net/2024)
- **Press-freedom context, RSF:**
  - World Press Freedom Index: Uzbekistan ranked **147/180 in 2026** (score 34.95) and 148/180 in 2025 (35.24).
  - RSF: "Authorities have yet to implement the reforms needed to end repressive media laws." It reports widespread censorship, surveillance and self-censorship, and says some independent outlets avoid official registration.
  - — [RSF: Uzbekistan](https://rsf.org/en/country/uzbekistan)
- **Press-freedom context, Freedom House (Freedom on the Net 2025, abridged):**
  - Indicator C1 (legal protections) scored 0/6; C2 (laws assigning penalties for online activity) scored 1/4.
  - "In the first 10 months of 2024, over 30,000 people were prosecuted under defamation and insult charges in the Administrative Code, with several cases concerning online speech. More than 200 of those prosecuted were reportedly sent to prison."
  - — [Freedom House FOTN 2025: Uzbekistan](https://freedomhouse.org/country/uzbekistan/freedom-net/2025)

### Inferences
#### Jurisdiction comparison (exam table)
| Question | United States | UK / Commonwealth (News Manual) | Uzbekistan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who must prove truth or falsity? | Plaintiff proves falsity (an element), at least for speech on public concerns | The journalist/defendant must prove truth | Civil: the defendant must prove truth (Plenum No. 21, 2025) |
| Fault | Actual malice for public officials and figures; negligence for private figures (many states) | Malice defeats defences; unintentional defamation needs reasonable care plus correction | Administrative and criminal slander require *knowingly* false statements |
| Opinion | Protected if not provably false; the label "opinion" alone doesn't help (Milkovich) | Fair comment / honest opinion based on true facts | Opinions cannot be ordered refuted, but there is a right of reply; a degrading form can be insult |
| Reporting official proceedings | Fair report privilege | Privilege if fair, accurate and contemporaneous | Not covered in the texts fetched; check locally |
| Civil or criminal? | Civil claims brought by a plaintiff (per LII and DMLP) | Mainly civil (in the sources reviewed) | Civil (Art. 100), administrative (Arts. 40–41), and criminal (Arts. 139–140 after an administrative penalty, harsher for media or online publication; Art. 158(3) for the President) |
| Harm threshold | Proven damages, or per se categories where damages are presumed | Serious harm (UK 2013; NSW/Victoria 2021) | Not specified in the texts fetched |

#### Simple case examples (constructed hypotheticals with model analysis)
1. **A false crime accusation (libel per se).** A student site writes: "Lab technician Rustam Q. was fired for stealing oscilloscopes." In fact he resigned, and there is no evidence of theft.
   - **Elements:** it was published, it identifies him, it is defamatory (it accuses him of a crime), and it is false.
   - **US:** he is a private figure, so he needs to show only negligence (Gertz). Relying on a rumour is likely negligent.
   - **Commonwealth:** the site would have to prove truth, and it can't.
   - **Uzbekistan:** he can seek a civil refutation and damages, and the site bears the burden of proof. If the writer *knew* the claim was false, administrative slander (Art. 40) applies. Criminal Art. 139 applies after a prior administrative penalty or in the aggravated forms.
2. **Opinion vs implied fact.**
   - "The cafeteria plov is the worst in Tashkent" is protected opinion, because it can't be proven false.
   - "The cafeteria uses expired meat" is a factual claim and needs proof.
   - "In my opinion the cafeteria uses expired meat" is *still* a factual implication (Milkovich; DMLP).
3. **Fair report.** Accurately reporting a prosecutor's accusation made in open court is privileged (US fair report; Commonwealth privilege), provided the report is fair and accurate. Repeating the same accusation from a rumour outside court is not privileged.
4. **A public figure.** A national politician sues over a false bribery claim. In the US, they must prove the outlet knew the claim was false or acted with reckless disregard for the truth (Sullivan). Careful, documented reporting normally defeats this.
5. **Private facts.** Publishing a classmate's medical condition learned from a leaked clinic record.
   - It is private, highly offensive and not newsworthy: classic private-facts liability in the US.
   - It also breaches SPJ's Minimize Harm principle.
   - In Uzbekistan it engages Constitution Art. 31 (privacy and personal data).
6. **Intrusion.** Hiding a camera in a dorm room, or secretly recording a private meeting. The wrong lies in *how the material was gathered*, so it can be actionable even if nothing is published. This is a general teaching point; the elements were not fetched (see Gaps).
7. **False light.** A story on campus theft illustrated with an identifiable photo of random students implies they are thieves. Fix it with careful captions or neutral images.
8. **Appropriation.** Using a famous footballer's photo in an ad for your newsletter without permission is a misappropriation risk. Using the same photo in a news story about his transfer generally is not (DMLP).
9. **Copyright.**
   - Copy-pasting another site's 800-word story *with credit*: the credit avoids plagiarism (an ethics problem) but does not license the copying (a legal problem). Copying a whole work weighs heavily against fair use.
   - Rewriting the *facts* in your own words, with attribution: fine, because facts are not protected.
   - Quoting two sentences of a speech you are reporting on: likely fair use or fair dealing.
   - Using a photo from someone's Instagram: you need the owner's permission (News Manual).

#### Ethics vs law (exam framing)
- **Law sets the minimum** (what you can be sued or prosecuted for). **Ethics sets the standard** (what you should do).
- SPJ: "legal access to information differs from the ethical justification for reporting it." Example: a juvenile suspect's name may be legally obtainable, but the ethical default is caution.
- **Plagiarism** is an ethical offence even when the copying is legal, for example with public-domain text. **Copyright infringement** is a legal wrong even when the source is credited.

#### Practical risk checklist for student writers (synthesis)
1. Can I prove every factual claim about a named person, with documents or on-record sources?
2. Have I separated fact from opinion, and attributed every opinion?
3. Did the criticized person get a fair chance to respond (SPJ)?
4. If I am reporting an official proceeding, is the report fair and accurate (privilege)?
5. Am I revealing private facts that serve no public interest? Is anyone involved a minor, a crime victim or grieving?
6. Is every photo, chart or long quote my own, licensed, or clearly fair use or fair dealing?
7. In Uzbekistan:
   - Is anything insulting in *form*, especially toward officials or the President?
   - Is it published online, which raises criminal exposure?
   - Can I correct or remove it quickly if it turns out to be wrong (Plenum 2025)?
8. If a complaint arrives, correct the error promptly and openly (SPJ), but don't admit legal liability without advice (News Manual).

#### Common mistakes
- Thinking "allegedly" or "according to sources" protects you. Repeating a defamatory claim republishes it (News Manual: quoting others is no defence).
- Thinking "it's just my opinion" protects a factual accusation (Milkovich).
- Thinking credit makes copying legal.
- Thinking anything public on social media is free to reuse.
- Mixing up libel (written or recorded, including posts and broadcasts) with slander (spoken).
- Forgetting that a statement can identify someone without naming them ("of and concerning").
- Applying US actual-malice rules to the UK or Uzbekistan.

### Inferences
#### 20 practice items with model answers (constructed; scenarios and names are fictional unless sourced)

**Q1. (Definition)** Define the inverted pyramid and give two reasons newsrooms use it.
*Model answer:*
- **Definition:** a structure that puts the most newsworthy facts first (the lead) and arranges the rest in descending order of importance (Purdue; Poynter).
- **Reason 1:** readers get the essentials at once and can stop at any point.
- **Reason 2:** editors can cut from the bottom without losing essential information (Purdue; News Manual).
- *Bonus:* it suits breaking news and fast updates, and it originated with the telegraph (Poynter).

**Q2. (News values)** Event: "A 19-year-old student from Namangan beat 3,000 entrants to win an international coding contest on Saturday." Identify four news values for a Tashkent student newspaper and say which belongs in the lead.
*Model answer:*
- **The four values:**
  - Timeliness: Saturday; it is new.
  - Proximity: an Uzbek student, relevant to a Tashkent audience.
  - Unusualness/novelty: a rare achievement, beating 3,000 entrants.
  - Human interest: a young person's success.
- **Prominence** is low, unless the contest itself is famous.
- **The lead:** lead with the achievement (novelty + proximity): "A 19-year-old from Namangan beat 3,000 programmers to win [contest] on Saturday."

**Q3. (Concept)** Explain "dog bites man" vs "man bites dog" and name the news value.
*Model answer:* the value is unusualness/novelty. Routine events are not news; rare reversals of expectation are. What counts as unusual depends on the society and the audience (News Manual Ch. 1; Purdue/Shoemaker).

**Q4. (Rewrite a buried lede)** "On Monday, Oct. 19, at 10 a.m., the Student Council held its weekly meeting in Room 105, where members discussed several issues, including the cafeteria, the library and parking, and voted 9–2 to extend library hours to midnight starting next month." Rewrite it as a summary lead and name three errors.
*Model answer:*
- **Rewrite:** "The library will stay open until midnight starting next month after the Student Council voted 9–2 on Monday to extend its hours." (22 words)
- **Errors:**
  1. It opens with the date and time.
  2. It is an agenda or process lead ("held its weekly meeting... discussed").
  3. The decision is buried at the end of a 41-word sentence.
- **Also:** the room number is trivial, and AP style uses the day of the week, not the full date, for events within a week.

**Q5. (Quote lead)** "'This is the worst flooding I have seen in 30 years,' said resident Karim Saidov, 64, as water filled streets in the Yunusobod district on Tuesday." (fictional) Rewrite it as a hard-news lead. When is a quote lead acceptable?
*Model answer:*
- **Rewrite:** lead with the facts: "Floodwater filled streets in Tashkent's Yunusobod district on Tuesday, damaging [number] homes, officials said." The resident's quote moves to paragraph 3 or 4.
- **Rule:** the News Manual says "Never begin with direct quotation"; give the facts or reported speech first.
- **When acceptable:** only in a feature or soft story, when the quote is short and extraordinary and the speaker is identified immediately.

**Q6. (Identify the lead type)**
- (a) "How hot does a phone get on a Tashkent rooftop in July?"
- (b) "The bridge took four years to build. It took four minutes to close."
- (c) "If you bike to campus, you will have 300 new parking spaces by spring."
- (d) "Yellow tape seals the door of Room 312..."
- (e) "Six people were killed and more than 100 left homeless when Cyclone Victor hit the Solomon Islands yesterday."
- (f) "Twelve hours. Three hundred volunteers. One flooded basement."

*Model answer:*
- (a) question;
- (b) contrast;
- (c) "you" / direct address;
- (d) descriptive (scene-setting) delayed lead;
- (e) summary / hard-news lead (News Manual);
- (f) staccato.

**Q7. (Summary lead from real facts)** Using these facts from the Wikipedia timeline, write a summary lead of 30 words or fewer. Facts: October 2020; a nest 2.5 m above ground in a tree cavity in Blaine, Washington; about 500 live Asian giant hornets inside, including about 200 queens.
*Model answer:*
- **Lead:** "A nest holding about 500 live Asian giant hornets, including some 200 queens, was found in a tree near Blaine, Washington, [state officials said]." (24 words with attribution)
- **Why passive:** the nest is more newsworthy than whoever found it (the News Manual's exception to its active-voice rule).
- **Real story:** attribute to the agency.

**Q8. (Delayed lead + nut graf)** Write a descriptive lead and a nut graf for the same hornet story, using only sourced facts.
*Model answer:*
- **Lead:** "Two and a half metres up a tree near Blaine, Washington, hidden in a hollow, sat a nest of about 500 live Asian giant hornets."
- **Nut graf:** "The nest, found in October 2020 after more than a year of sightings and trapping in the area, belonged to the insect popularly nicknamed the 'murder hornet', which entomologists renamed the northern giant hornet in 2022."
- **Why:** the nut graf explains significance and context, and it comes straight after the soft lead (Poynter).

**Q9. (Ordering)** Put these fictional paragraphs into inverted-pyramid order:
- (A) The bridge opened in 1998 and was last inspected in 2021.
- (B) City engineers closed the Riverside footbridge on Wednesday after finding a crack in a main support beam, the city transport department said.
- (C) About 6,000 people a day use the footbridge, the department said; pedestrians are being directed to a crossing 400 metres north.
- (D) "We will not reopen it until we know exactly why the crack formed," said chief engineer Nodira Rahimova.
- (E) Repairs are expected to take at least three weeks.

*Model answer:*
- **Order:** **B → C → E → D → A.**
  - B is the lead: what, where, when, source.
  - C gives the impact (who is affected, the detour).
  - E gives what happens next (duration).
  - D is the official quote.
  - A is background and is cut first.
- **Acceptable variation:** some editors put D before E; either works as long as A comes last.

**Q10. (Nut graf)** What is a nut graf, where does it go, and why is it essential after a delayed lead?
*Model answer:*
- **What:** the paragraph that states what the story is about and why the reader should care, the story's "kernel" (Poynter). WSJ's Ken Wells: "a paragraph that says what this whole story is about and why you should read it."
- **Where:** right after an anecdotal or other soft lead, usually paragraph 2–4.
- **Why essential:** without it, readers of a soft lead don't learn the news or why it matters, so they stop reading.

**Q11. (Attribution)** Fix each: (a) "The suspect fled the scene, police claimed." (b) "The minister stated that fuel prices will rise." (c) "The new policy is unfair to students." (written by the reporter in a news story)
*Model answer:*
- (a) Use "police said". "Claimed" signals disbelief.
- (b) Use "said". "Stated" implies undisputed fact and sounds stiff (News Manual).
- (c) Attribute the opinion: "The new policy is unfair to students, Student Union President [name] said." Unattributed opinion reads as the reporter's own (News Manual). Add the administration's response (SPJ: a fair chance to respond).

**Q12. (Angle narrowing)** Narrow "climate change" into a campus-paper story: topic → issue → angle → story question → lead.
*Model answer:*
- **Topic:** climate change.
- **Issue:** extreme summer heat on campus.
- **Angle:** exam rooms without working cooling in the oldest building during July finals.
- **Story question:** "How many exam rooms exceeded [the temperature guideline] during July finals, and what is the facilities office's plan for next summer?"
- **Lead (constructed):** "Temperatures topped 35°C in four exam rooms during July finals, student measurements show, and the facilities office says new cooling will not arrive before 2028."
- Every number must come from real measurements and sources.

**Q13. ("So what?")** Apply the "so what?" test to: "Many students now ride e-scooters to campus."
*Model answer:*
- **Add numbers:** scooters per day; injuries treated at the clinic this semester vs last.
- **Add a second source:** the security chief, a clinic doctor.
- **Add consequences:** parking, safety rules.
- **Revised angle:** "Scooter injuries treated at the campus clinic doubled this semester, the clinic says, as rental scooters arrived at the main gate."
- **Rule (MHM):** numbers and second sources turn an observation into journalism.

**Q14. (Libel elements)** A campus newsletter prints: "Lab technician Rustam Q. was fired for stealing equipment." He resigned voluntarily, and nothing was stolen. Which elements of defamation are present, and what is the likely US fault standard?
*Model answer:*
- **Elements present:**
  - Publication: the newsletter reached readers.
  - Identification: he is named.
  - Defamatory meaning: it accuses him of a crime, so it is defamatory per se.
  - Falsity: he resigned and nothing was stolen.
- **US fault standard:** he is a private figure, so negligence (Gertz, 1974). Printing a rumour without checking is likely negligent.
- **Defences:** none available. It is not true, not opinion, and not privileged.
- **Other jurisdictions:**
  - Commonwealth: the newsletter would have to prove truth.
  - Uzbekistan: a civil claim under Civil Code Art. 100 puts the burden of proving truth on the publisher (Plenum No. 21, 2025).

**Q15. (Opinion)** Which statements are actionable, and why? (a) "The rector's speech was the dullest I've ever heard." (b) "In my opinion, the rector took bribes from contractors." (c) "The rector's budget plan is a 'train wreck'."
*Model answer:*
- (a) **Not actionable.** It is pure opinion and can't be proven false.
- (b) **Potentially actionable.** It implies a verifiable fact (bribery), and the "in my opinion" label doesn't protect it (Milkovich, 1990; DMLP).
- (c) **Generally protected** as rhetorical hyperbole or opinion on a public matter, especially if the underlying facts are disclosed.

**Q16. (Public figures)** What is "actual malice", who must prove it, and where does the rule come from?
*Model answer:*
- **Definition:** publishing "with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not." It does *not* mean ill will.
- **Who must prove it:**
  - public officials (*NYT v. Sullivan*, 1964);
  - public figures (*Curtis v. Butts*, 1967);
  - private figures need show only negligence in many states (*Gertz*, 1974).
- **Scope:** it is a US constitutional rule. It does not apply in the UK or Uzbekistan.

**Q17. (Privacy torts)** Match each scenario to a privacy tort.
- (a) A reporter hides a recorder in a professor's office.
- (b) A site publishes a student's HIV status from a leaked clinic file.
- (c) A café uses a famous singer's photo in its ad without permission.
- (d) A story on exam cheating runs with an identifiable photo of random students.

*Model answer:*
- (a) **Intrusion upon seclusion.** The wrong is in how the material was gathered.
- (b) **Public disclosure of private facts.** The fact is private, highly offensive, and not newsworthy.
- (c) **Appropriation / right of publicity.** A commercial use of someone's likeness.
- (d) **False light.** The photo implies those students cheated. It could also be defamation if the implication is false and harms their reputations.

**Q18. (Copyright vs fair use)** (a) Can you republish a competitor's full 800-word story if you credit it? (b) Can you report the same facts? (c) Can you quote two sentences of a minister's speech in your report? (d) Can you use a photo from a stranger's Instagram?
*Model answer:*
- (a) **No.**
  - Credit avoids plagiarism, but it is not permission.
  - Copying a whole work weighs against fair use.
  - "Simply rearranging the paragraphs is not enough" (News Manual).
- (b) **Yes, in your own words.** Facts and ideas are not protected (Copyright Office). Ethics still requires attribution if you rely on their reporting.
- (c) **Usually yes.** Short quotation for news reporting or commentary is a classic fair-use / fair-dealing purpose, though there is no "safe" word count.
- (d) **Not without the owner's permission** (News Manual). In cases of doubt, get permission (Copyright Office).

**Q19. (SPJ ethics)** A source offers leaked exam papers for 500,000 soums and wants to stay anonymous. Which SPJ principles apply, and what should you do?
*Model answer:*
- **Act Independently:** "Do not pay sources in exchange for interviews or information."
- **Seek Truth:** anonymity is "a last resort", only for sources with information of substantial public interest who face danger, retribution or other harm. Consider their motives and explain the decision to the audience.
- **Minimize Harm:** weigh the harm to students and staff.
- **What to do:**
  - refuse to pay;
  - try to verify through open methods (documents, other sources);
  - grant anonymity only if the criteria are met, and say why.

**Q20. (Uzbekistan and jurisdictions)** What protects free expression in Uzbekistan's Constitution, what liability exists for slander and insult, and how does this differ from the US?
*Model answer:*
- **Constitution (2023):**
  - Art. 33: thought, speech and information. Restrictions are allowed only by law, for the listed aims.
  - Arts. 81–82: the media are free; censorship is not permitted; the media are responsible for the reliability of what they publish.
  - Art. 31: privacy, honour, dignity and personal data.
- **Liability:**
  - Administrative fines for slander (CAR Art. 40) and insult (Art. 41).
  - Criminal liability under CC Arts. 139 and 140 after a prior administrative penalty. Penalties are higher when the slander or insult is published in the media or online.
  - A separate crime of publicly insulting or slandering the President (Art. 158(3)).
  - Civil refutation and damages (Civil Code Art. 100), with the burden of proving truth on the defendant.
- **US contrast:** defamation is handled as a civil claim. The plaintiff must prove falsity and fault, and public figures must prove actual malice.
- **Context:** RSF ranked Uzbekistan 147/180 in 2026.

#### Exam strategy reminders
- **"Write a lead" questions:**
  1. List the facts.
  2. Rank them by news value.
  3. Write 30 words or fewer, strongest W first.
  4. Check against the lead checklist (Section 3).
  5. Name the lead type if asked.
- **Law questions:**
  1. Definition.
  2. Elements.
  3. Apply them to the facts.
  4. Defences.
  5. A note on how the jurisdiction changes the answer.
- **Angle questions:** always end with a *story question* and a *so what*.
- **Ethics questions:** name the SPJ principle, quote or paraphrase the relevant line, then state the action.
