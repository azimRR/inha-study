## 1. Paraphrasing: how to do it, and what separates an acceptable paraphrase from patchwriting or plagiarism

### Takeaway
An acceptable paraphrase meets four conditions:
- It restates the source's idea in **your own words and your own sentence structure**.
- It is accurate and keeps roughly the original level of detail.
- Any distinctive phrase you keep goes in quotation marks.
- It is **cited**.

Two failures count as plagiarism:
- **Too close to the source.** Swapping synonyms or reshuffling the author's own words is plagiarism *even with a citation*. Harvard calls this "inadequate paraphrase" or "mosaic plagiarism"; it is commonly called patchwriting.
- **No citation.** A well-worded paraphrase without a citation is also plagiarism. Harvard calls this "uncited paraphrase".

### Cited Findings

#### Definitions you can quote
- Purdue OWL defines a paraphrase as follows. It is "your own rendition of essential information and ideas expressed by someone else, presented in a new form". It is "one legitimate way (when accompanied by accurate documentation) to borrow from a source". It is also "a more detailed restatement than a summary, which focuses concisely on a single main idea." — [Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)
- Purdue gives three reasons why paraphrasing is valuable:
  - "It is better than quoting information from an undistinguished passage."
  - "It helps you control the temptation to quote too much."
  - "The mental process required for successful paraphrasing helps you to grasp the full meaning of the original."
  — [Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)
- Harvard: "When you paraphrase from a source, you restate the source's ideas in your own words. Whereas a summary provides your readers with a condensed overview of a source (or part of a source), a paraphrase of a source offers your readers the same level of detail provided in the original source. Therefore, while a summary will be shorter than the original source material, a paraphrase will generally be about the same length as the original source material." — [Harvard Guide to Using Sources: Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)
- **Sources disagree on paraphrase length.** Purdue: "Paraphrased material is usually shorter than the original passage, taking a somewhat broader segment of the source and condensing it slightly." — [Purdue OWL: Quoting, Paraphrasing, and Summarizing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/index.html). Harvard says "about the same length" — [Harvard: SPQ](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0). Both agree that a paraphrase keeps much more detail than a summary.
- APA: "A paraphrase restates another's idea (or your own previously published idea) in your own words." It adds: "Published authors paraphrase their sources most of the time, rather than directly quoting the sources; student authors should emulate this practice by paraphrasing more than directly quoting." — [APA Style: Paraphrases](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing)
- UNC: "Paraphrasing does NOT mean changing a word or two in someone else's sentence, changing the sentence structure while maintaining the original words, or changing a few words to synonyms. If you are tempted to rearrange a sentence in any of these ways, you are writing too close to the original. That's plagiarizing, not paraphrasing." It also asks: "do you need to cite when you paraphrase? Yes, you do!" — [UNC Writing Center: Plagiarism](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)

#### Step-by-step methods given by the sources
- Purdue's "6 Steps to Effective Paraphrasing" (verbatim):
  1. "Reread the original passage until you understand its full meaning."
  2. "Set the original aside, and write your paraphrase on a note card."
  3. "Jot down a few words below your paraphrase to remind you later how you envision using this material. At the top of the note card, write a key word or phrase to indicate the subject of your paraphrase."
  4. "Check your rendition with the original to make sure that your version accurately expresses all the essential information in a new form."
  5. "Use quotation marks to identify any unique term or phraseology you have borrowed exactly from the source."
  6. "Record the source (including the page) on your note card so that you can credit it easily if you decide to incorporate the material into your paper."
  — [Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)
- Harvard's method: "it's a good idea to put away the source material while you write your paraphrase of it... Once you have done this, you should look back at the original and make sure that you have represented the source's ideas accurately and that you have not used the same words or sentence structure. If you do want to use some of the author's words for emphasis or clarity, you must put those words in quotation marks and provide a citation." — [Harvard: What Constitutes Plagiarism?](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- Harvard's note-taking discipline:
  - Paste the actual quotation into your notes, not into your draft. Paraphrase it there, mark it as a paraphrase, and mention the author's name.
  - Avoid "gradually rewording the paraphrased material from draft to draft until you lose sight of the fact that it's still a paraphrase."
  - Avoid papers that are just a string of paraphrases.
  - "Whenever you paraphrase, make sure you indicate, at each logical progression, that the ideas are taken from an authored source."
  - "Never paraphrase or quote from a source without immediately adding a citation."

  — [Harvard: How to Avoid Plagiarism](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/how-avoid-plagiarism-0)
- UNC: "take notes from your reading with the book closed. Doing so will make it easier to put the ideas in your own words." — [UNC: Plagiarism](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)
- APA on long paraphrases:
  - Cite the work on first mention. You need not repeat the citation while it remains clear that the same work is still being paraphrased.
  - "If the paraphrase continues into a new paragraph, reintroduce the citation. If the paraphrase incorporates multiple sources or switches among sources, repeat the citation so the source is clear."
  - A page number is not required, but you may add one for long works.
  - Translating a passage yourself "is considered a paraphrase, not a direct quotation."

  — [APA Style: Paraphrases](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing)

#### What counts as plagiarism (Harvard's categories double as grader vocabulary)
- General rule: "it is considered plagiarism to draw any idea or any language from someone else without adequately crediting that source in your paper... Taking credit for anyone else's work is stealing, and it is unacceptable in all academic situations, whether you do it intentionally or by accident." This also covers sources with no visible author: "if you consult a website that has no clear authorship, you are still responsible for citing the website as a source for your paper." — [Harvard: What Constitutes Plagiarism?](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Verbatim plagiarism:** "If you copy language word for word from another source and use that language in your paper, you are plagiarizing verbatim." — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Mosaic plagiarism:** "If you copy bits and pieces from a source (or several sources), changing a few words here and there without either adequately paraphrasing or quoting directly, the result is mosaic plagiarism." It often results from "careless note-taking and confusion over where your source's ideas end and your own ideas begin." — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Inadequate paraphrase:** "It's not enough to change a few words here and there and leave the rest; instead, you must completely restate the ideas in the passage in your own words. If your own language is too close to the original, then you are plagiarizing, even if you do provide a citation." — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Uncited paraphrase:** "it's not enough to paraphrase the source material responsibly; you also need to cite the source, even if you have changed the wording significantly." — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Uncited quotation:** quotation marks alone are not enough. "You must also credit the author of that material and provide a trail for your reader to follow back to the original document... Citations should always go directly after quotations." — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- **Material from another student's work:** even where discussion is collaborative, "the writing or calculations that you do must be your own" unless group authorship is permitted. Ideas your professor offers in lecture should always be cited. — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- Purdue: "Plagiarism is using someone else's ideas or words without giving them proper credit". It "can range from unintentional (forgetting to include a source in a bibliography) to intentional (buying a paper online, using another writer's ideas as your own to make your work sound smarter)." — [Purdue OWL: Plagiarism Overview](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/avoiding_plagiarism/index.html)
- UNC treats accidental plagiarism like deliberate plagiarism: "If you fail to give proper credit to someone else's ideas because you didn't know you were supposed to or because you didn't know how to do so, you face the same consequences as if you intentionally stole someone else's work."
  - Its checklist says you must still cite even if you "changed the words used by the author into synonyms," "completely paraphrased the ideas to which you referred," or "mention the author's name in the sentence."
  - Its moral: "When in doubt, give a citation."

  — [UNC: Plagiarism](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)
- Common knowledge is the exception to the citation rule.
  - Purdue: "Generally, common knowledge is information that someone finds undocumented in at least five credible sources." Its "golden rule" is "When in doubt, just cite." — [Purdue OWL: Common Knowledge & Attribution](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/avoiding_plagiarism/common-knowledge_attribution.html)
  - UNC's test is two questions: "Did I know this information before I took this course?" and "Did this information/idea come from my own brain?" A "no" to either means you cite. — [UNC: Plagiarism](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)

#### Worked examples from the sources (original → unacceptable → acceptable)

**Example 1: Purdue OWL (MLA in-text style).** — [Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)
- *Original* (Lester, *Writing Research Papers*, 2nd ed., 1976, pp. 46–47): "Students frequently overuse direct quotation in taking notes, and as a result they overuse quotations in the final [research] paper. Probably only about 10% of your final manuscript should appear as directly quoted matter. Therefore, you should strive to limit the amount of exact transcribing of source materials while taking notes."
- *Plagiarized:* "Students often use too many direct quotations when they take notes, resulting in too many of them in the final research paper. In fact, probably only about 10% of the final copy should consist of directly quoted material. So it is important to limit the amount of source material copied while taking notes."
  - Purdue classes this as plagiarism "in part, because of its failure to deploy any citation."
  - It also mirrors the original sentence for sentence (an inference).
- *Legitimate paraphrase:* "In research papers, students often quote excessively, failing to keep quoted material down to a desirable level. Since the problem usually originates during note taking, it is essential to minimize the material recorded verbatim (Lester 46-47)."
- *Acceptable summary* (for comparison): "Students should take just a few notes in direct quotation from sources to help minimize the amount of quoted material in a research paper (Lester 46-47)."

**Example 2: Harvard, inadequate paraphrase** (Michael Sandel, "The Case Against Perfection," *The Atlantic*, April 2004). — [Harvard: What Constitutes Plagiarism?](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- *Original:* "Though there is much to be said for this argument, I do not think the main problem with enhancement and genetic engineering is that they undermine effort and erode human agency. The deeper danger is that they represent a kind of hyperagency—a Promethean aspiration to remake nature, including human nature, to serve our purposes and satisfy our desires. The problem is not the drift to mechanism but the drive to mastery. And what the drive to mastery misses and may even destroy is an appreciation of the gifted character of human powers and achievements."
- *Inadequate paraphrase (plagiarism even though it is cited):* "The main problem with enhancement and genetic engineering is not that they undermine effort and erode human agency. The bigger problem is that they represent a kind of hyperagency—a Promethean desire to remake nature, including human nature, to serve our purposes and satisfy our desires. The problem is not the drift to mechanism but the drive to mastery. And what the drive to mastery misses and may even destroy is an appreciation of the gifts that make our achievements possible (Sandel)."
  - Harvard's diagnosis: the student "has only cut or replaced a few words".
  - The swaps: "I do not think the main problem" became "the main problem is not"; "deeper danger" became "bigger problem"; "aspiration" became "desire"; "the gifted character of human powers and achievements" became "the gifts that make our achievements possible".
- *Adequate paraphrase:* "Michael Sandel disagrees with the argument that genetic engineering is a problem because it replaces the need for humans to work hard and make their own choices. Instead, he argues that we should be more concerned that the decision to use genetic enhancement is motivated by a desire to take control of nature and bend it to our will instead of appreciating its gifts."
  - Sandel is named in the signal phrase, and the online article has no page numbers, so no parenthetical citation is needed.

**Example 3: Harvard, verbatim plagiarism** (Ellora Derenoncourt, 2022, *The American Economic Review*, 112(2), 369–408). — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- *Plagiarized:* the student wrote one sentence of their own ("But things changed mid-century."). They then copied three sentences of Derenoncourt's paragraph word for word, leaving out two.
- *Acceptable paraphrase with citation:* "But things changed mid-century. In fact, as Derenoncourt (2022) explains, the wartime increase in jobs in both defense and naval shipyards marked the first time during the Great Migration that Black southerners went to California and other west coast states. After the war, the increase in jobs in the car industry led to Black southerners choosing cities in the midwest, including Detroit and Cleveland."
- *Acceptable mixed version:* quotes one clause, followed by "(p. 379)", and paraphrases the rest.
- Harvard's note for STEM students: "Social scientists and STEM scholars rarely quote in their writing, paraphrasing their sources instead."

**Example 4: Harvard, uncited paraphrase** (C. Thi Nguyen, 2020, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles," *Episteme*). — [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)
- *Original (excerpt):* "Epistemic bubbles can easily form accidentally. But the most plausible explanation for the particular features of echo chambers is something more malicious. Echo chambers are excellent tools to maintain, reinforce, and expand power through epistemic control."
- *Plagiarized (well worded but uncited):* "Echo chambers and epistemic bubbles have different origins. While epistemic bubbles can be created organically, it's more likely that echo chambers will be formed by those who wish to keep or even grow their control over the information that people hear and understand."
- *Acceptable:* the same text, but opened with "According to Nguyen (2020), …" and continued with "Nguyen argues that …". The signal phrases remove any doubt about whose idea it is.

**Example 5: Harvard, model paraphrase** (Stanley Milgram, 1974, "The Perils of Obedience," p. 737). — [Harvard: SPQ](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)
- *Original:* "The problem of obedience is not wholly psychological. The form and shape of society and the way it is developing have much to do with it. There was a time, perhaps, when people were able to give a fully human response to any situation because they were fully absorbed in it as human beings. But as soon as there was a division of labor things changed."
- *Paraphrase:* "Milgram (1974) claims that people's willingness to obey authority figures cannot be explained by psychological factors alone. In an earlier era, people may have had the ability to invest in social situations to a greater extent. However, as society has become increasingly structured by a division of labor, people have become more alienated from situations over which they do not have control (p.737)."
  - It keeps the hedge ("perhaps" became "may have"). Note that "division of labor" is kept as a standard term.

### Inferences

#### Patchwriting, plagiarism and paraphrase: one classification table
"Patchwriting" is the usual informal name for the near-copy that Harvard calls **mosaic plagiarism** (patched together from bits of one or more sources) or **inadequate paraphrase** (one source with a few words swapped). In an exam answer, treat these labels as the same fault, and use Harvard's term when the question refers to Harvard.

| Student version | Words | Sentence structure | Citation | Verdict (Harvard category) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copied text without quotation marks | source's | source's | with or without | Plagiarism (verbatim) |
| Synonym swap or reshuffle (patchwriting) | mostly source's | source's | with or without | Plagiarism (inadequate paraphrase / mosaic) |
| Genuinely new wording and structure | yours | yours | none | Plagiarism (uncited paraphrase) |
| Genuinely new wording and structure | yours (any unique phrase in "quotes") | yours | yes | **Acceptable paraphrase** |
| Exact words in quotation marks | source's | source's | none | Plagiarism (uncited quotation) |
| Exact words in quotation marks | source's | source's | yes, with page | **Acceptable quotation** |

#### A 7-step exam method (synthesised from Purdue's 6 steps, Harvard and UNC)
1. Read the passage twice. Mark its main claim and the logical link it uses (cause, contrast, condition, sequence).
2. Note 3–5 single keywords, not phrases.
3. **Cover the original** (Purdue step 2; Harvard; UNC's "book closed").
4. Rebuild the idea with a **new structure**:
   - start from a different element (for example, the effect instead of the cause);
   - change the sentence type (two simple sentences into one complex sentence, or the reverse);
   - change parts of speech (a verb becomes a noun, or the reverse).
5. Uncover the original and check three things:
   - (a) the meaning is complete and accurate, including hedges such as "probably" (Purdue step 4);
   - (b) any copied run of three or more ordinary words gets rewritten or quoted (a personal heuristic, not a published rule);
   - (c) the clause order is not the original's (Harvard's "same words or sentence structure" test).
6. Put quotation marks around any distinctive phrase you keep (Purdue step 5). Standard technical terms that have no real synonym, such as "thermal runaway" or "tensile strength", are normally kept without quotation marks. That point is my inference, so confirm the instructor's expectation.
7. Attribute with a signal phrase and a citation. In APA give author and year (page optional for paraphrase). In MLA give author and page.

#### How graders spot a bad paraphrase (inferred from the sources' own diagnoses)
- **Side-by-side check:** the same number of sentences in the same order, the same clause order, and synonyms dropped into the original's slots. This is exactly Harvard's diagnosis of the Sandel example.
- **Distinctive words kept without quotation marks.** In the Sandel example these are "hyperagency", "Promethean" and "drive to mastery".
- **Borrowed facts or statistics with no signal phrase or citation.** See the Lester "10%" example and the Nguyen example.
- **One citation placed mid-paragraph,** so that later borrowed sentences read as the student's own. This is Harvard's mosaic-plagiarism paragraph #1.
- **Meaning drift:** the student's opinion is added, or the author's qualifiers are dropped.

**Example 6: reinforced concrete.** *Original* (Rahimova, 2024, p. 12): "Because concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension, engineers embed steel reinforcing bars in the regions of a beam that will be stretched under load. The steel carries the tensile forces, while the concrete resists compression and protects the steel from corrosion."
- *Patchwritten (unacceptable even though it is cited):* "Since concrete is strong when compressed but weak when pulled, engineers put steel reinforcing bars in the areas of a beam that will be stretched by loads. The steel takes the tensile forces, while the concrete resists compression and shields the steel from rust (Rahimova, 2024)."

- *Acceptable:* "Rahimova (2024) explains that a reinforced-concrete beam combines two materials with opposite strengths: concrete handles the squeezing forces, while steel bars placed where the beam lengthens under load take the pulling forces, and the concrete around them also keeps the steel from corroding (p. 12)."
  - Why it works: the structure is new (a colon-led explanation), the order of ideas is different, the technical terms are kept, and it has a narrative citation with a page number.

**Example 7: battery safety.** *Original* (Lee, 2023, p. 4): "Most lithium-ion battery fires begin when an internal short circuit causes a cell to overheat, which can trigger a self-sustaining reaction known as thermal runaway. Once one cell fails, the heat can spread to neighbouring cells within seconds."
- *Unacceptable (uncited paraphrase):* "A short circuit inside a lithium-ion cell can heat it until a chain reaction called thermal runaway starts, and the failure can jump to adjacent cells almost immediately." The wording is new, but there is no attribution, so this is Harvard's "uncited paraphrase".
- *Acceptable:* "According to Lee (2023), an internal short circuit is the usual starting point of a lithium-ion battery fire: the overheated cell can enter thermal runaway, a reaction that keeps feeding itself, and its heat may reach the cells next to it almost immediately (p. 4)."

**Example 8: specifications.** *Original* (Tursunov, 2022, p. 5): "Engineers who write clearly save their organizations time and money, because readers who misunderstand a specification may build the wrong component."
- *Unacceptable (near-verbatim, despite the citation):* "Engineers who write clearly save their companies time and money because readers who misread a specification might build the wrong part (Tursunov, 2022)."
- *Acceptable:* "Tursunov (2022) argues that clarity in engineering documents pays off financially: a misread specification can lead a team to manufacture a part that does not match the design (p. 5)."

## 2. Summary vs paraphrase vs quotation: when to use each, signal phrases, APA/MLA basics, and how to write a 3–5-sentence article summary

### Takeaway
Choose the technique by what the reader needs:
- **Quote** rarely, and only when the exact wording matters: a definition, a memorable phrase, or wording you will analyse or dispute.
- **Paraphrase** when you need a passage's detail.
- **Summarize** when you need only the main idea and key points, in far fewer words.

All three need a signal phrase and a citation. APA uses author–date, and a quotation needs a page number. MLA uses author–page, with no comma and no "p.".

A 3–5-sentence summary:
- opens with the author, the title and the thesis in your own words;
- groups the key supporting points by importance, not in the source's sentence order;
- ends with the author's conclusion;
- leaves out your opinion and is cited.

### Cited Findings

#### The three techniques compared
- Purdue:
  - Quotations: "Quotations must be identical to the original, using a narrow segment of the source. They must match the source document word for word and must be attributed to the original author."
  - Summaries: "Summarizing involves putting the main idea(s) into your own words, including only the main point(s)... Summaries are significantly shorter than the original and take a broad overview of the source material."
  - Practice steps: "Read the entire text, noting the key points and main ideas. Summarize in your own words what the single main idea of the essay is. Paraphrase important supporting points that come up in the essay. Consider any words, phrases, or brief passages that you believe should be quoted directly."
  - On quoting: "quoting should be done only sparingly".

  — [Purdue OWL: Quoting, Paraphrasing, and Summarizing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/index.html)
- Purdue lists why writers use all three:
  - "Provide support for claims or add credibility to your writing";
  - "Refer to work that leads up to the work you are now doing";
  - "Give examples of several points of view on a subject";
  - "Call attention to a position that you wish to agree or disagree with";
  - "Highlight a particularly striking phrase, sentence, or passage by quoting the original";
  - "Distance yourself from the original by quoting it in order to cue readers that the words are not your own";
  - "Expand the breadth or depth of your writing."

  It adds that writers "frequently intertwine summaries, paraphrases, and quotations." — [Purdue OWL: QPS](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/index.html)
- Harvard: "Scholars in the humanities tend to summarize, paraphrase, and quote texts; social scientists and natural scientists rely primarily on summary and paraphrase." Also: "Unless you have a good reason to quote directly from the source, you should paraphrase the source." — [Harvard: Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)

#### When to quote (the rules graders apply)
- APA: "It is best to paraphrase sources rather than directly quoting them because paraphrasing allows you to fit material to the context of your paper and writing style." Quote instead "when reproducing an exact definition", "when an author has said something memorably or succinctly", or "when you want to respond to exact wording (e.g., something someone said)." — [APA Style: Quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations)
- Harvard's four situations for quoting:
  1. "When you plan to discuss the actual language of a text."
  2. "When you are discussing an author's position or theory, and you plan to discuss the wording of a core assertion or kernel of the argument in your paper."
  3. "When you risk losing the essence of the author's ideas in the translation from their words to your own."
  4. "When you want to appeal to the authority of the author and using their words will emphasize that authority."

  Harvard also warns against block quotations unless you will "do something substantive with a long quotation". — [Harvard: SPQ](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)
- Purdue:
  - Quote when the source's language is "particularly striking or notable", or when the author "has coined a term unique to her or his research".
  - Use an indirect quotation (a paraphrase) "when you merely need to summarize key incidents or details of the text."
  - Indirect quotations need no quotation marks but "still require proper citations".

  — [Purdue OWL: Quotation Marks](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/index.html)
- UNC: "The majority of your paper should still be your original ideas in your own words." On how much to quote: "As few words as possible." — [UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)
- Lester, as quoted by Purdue: "Probably only about 10% of your final manuscript should appear as directly quoted matter." — [Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)

#### Signal phrases
- Harvard's definition: "A signal phrase is an introductory clause that signals to the reader a shift in point of view from you to your source."
  - The verb carries meaning: "the phrase 'Sandel argues' signals that Sandel is making a claim, while the phrase 'Sandel notes' signals a more neutral reporting of information."
  - Harvard's examples: "Allen argues", "Sandel notes", "Lewis confirms", "Gates emphasizes", "Wilson contends", "Patterson acknowledges", "Mukherjee observes", "Carpio suggests", "Banaji claims".

  — [Harvard: The Nuts & Bolts of Integrating](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)
- Purdue:
  - Signal (lead-in) phrases usually contain a verb such as "indicate" or "argue". Some contain no verb at all ("According to [author],").
  - **APA recommends past tense**: Doe asserted / acknowledged / claimed / pointed out / stated; "The work of Doe indicated"; "Doe has drawn attention to".
  - **MLA and Chicago allow present tense**: declares, argues, believes, shows, indicates, implies, suggests, thinks, addresses.
  - For disagreement: rejected or denied (APA); refutes or disputes (MLA).
  - "Be sure each signal phrase verb matches your intention for the in-text citation."

  — [Purdue OWL: Signal and Lead-in Phrases](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/signal_and_lead_in_phrases.html)
- Purdue on APA tense: the APA manual "suggests that authors of research papers should use the past tense or present perfect tense for signal phrases that occur in the literature review and procedure descriptions (for example, Jones (1998) found or Jones (1998) has found...)". — [Purdue OWL: APA In-Text Citations: The Basics](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/in_text_citations_the_basics.html)
- Last's engineering textbook:
  - Avoid "says (too vague)", "writes (too vague)" and "talks about (too informal)". Choose a verb that describes "what the author is DOING".
  - On tense: "summaries of ideas are generally given in the present tense, while results and findings are often given in the past tense" (e.g., "Dombrowski explains …" vs "Hollander's study found that …").

  — [Last, *Technical Writing Essentials*, Appendix B: Writing a Summary](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/)
- UNC's alternatives to the "they said" rut: add, remark, exclaim, announce, reply, state, comment, respond, estimate, write, point out, predict, argue, suggest, propose, declare, criticize, proclaim, note, complain, opine, observe, think. UNC adds that "Different reporting verbs are preferred by different disciplines". — [UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)
- Purdue: attribution has three parts: "signal phrase (also called 'signposting'), in-text citation (also called 'parenthetical citation'), bibliographic citation". "You need all three to effectively attribute ideas and words to their proper sources of origin." — [Purdue OWL: Common Knowledge & Attribution](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/avoiding_plagiarism/common-knowledge_attribution.html)

#### Framing and integrating source material
- Harvard on framing:
  - Start each paragraph with "a topic sentence that sets up the point of that paragraph in your own words rather than beginning with a quotation or an idea from a source".
  - "Your paper should never contain a paragraph that is solely based on a source without any commentary from you."
  - "Every quotation you use in your paper should be introduced with a sentence of your own", and you should follow it with your own discussion. In Harvard's words: "Don't float the quotation!"

  — [Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)
- UNC's four steps: "1. Provide context for each quotation. 2. Attribute each quotation to its source. 3. Explain the significance of the quotation. 4. Provide a citation for the quotation."
  - Four ways to embed a quotation: lead in with a colon; attribute it with a verb plus a comma; interrupt the quotation with the attribution (commas on both sides); or make the quoted words part of your own grammar.
  - On that last method: "when you use 'that' after the verb that introduces the quote, you no longer need a comma."

  — [UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)
- Harvard on grammar: square brackets can make a quotation fit your sentence (for example, "haircut [that] was"), but "Be careful not to overuse brackets in your paper." — [Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)

#### APA 7 citation basics
- **The system:**
  - "each work used in a paper has two parts: an in-text citation and a corresponding reference list entry."
  - In-text citations "may be parenthetical or narrative."
  - Use "&" in parenthetical citations and spell out "and" in narrative citations.
  - **One or two authors:** name them in every citation. **Three or more:** first author plus "et al." in every citation, including the first.
  - Examples: (Luna, 2020) / Luna (2020); (Salas & D'Agostino, 2020) / Salas and D'Agostino (2020); (Martin et al., 2020) / Martin et al. (2020).
  - Give only the year in-text, and use "n.d." when there is no date.

  — [APA Style: Author–Date Citation System](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/basic-principles/author-date)
- **Quotations:**
  - "always include a full citation (parenthetical or narrative) in the same sentence as the quotation, including the page number (or other location information, e.g., paragraph number)."
  - Place a parenthetical citation "either immediately after the quotation or at the end of the sentence."
  - "If the citation appears at the end of a sentence, put the end punctuation after the closing parenthesis for the citation."
  - APA's example: Effective teams can be difficult to describe because "high performance along one domain does not translate to high performance along another" (Ervin et al., 2018, p. 470).

  — [APA Style: Quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations)
- **Page numbers and paraphrase:**
  - Use "p." for one page and "pp." for a range, with an en dash, e.g., (Jones, 1998, pp. 199–201).
  - For a paraphrase, the page may be omitted, but "APA guidelines, however, do encourage including a page range for a summary or paraphrase when it will help the reader find the information in a longer work."
  - Sources without pages use another locator, e.g., (paras. 4–5).

  — [Purdue OWL: APA In-Text Citations: The Basics](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/in_text_citations_the_basics.html)
- **Titles in APA body text:** a title that is italic in the reference list stays italic, in title case. A title that is not italic there (an article or chapter) goes in double quotation marks, in title case. — [Purdue OWL: APA In-Text Basics](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/in_text_citations_the_basics.html)

#### MLA 9 citation basics
- **Author–page method:** "the author's last name and the page number(s) from which the quotation or paraphrase is taken must appear in the text, and a complete reference should appear on your Works Cited page."
  - Examples: Wordsworth stated that Romantic poetry was marked by a "spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" (263). / Romantic poetry is characterized by the "spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" (Wordsworth 263).
  - **No comma and no "p."** between author and page.
  - Two authors: (Best and Marcus 9). Three or more authors: (Franck et al. 327).
  - With no known author, use a shortened title: in quotation marks for a short work, in italics for a long work.
  - The signal word must match the first item of the Works Cited entry.

  — [Purdue OWL: MLA In-Text Citations: The Basics](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_in_text_citations_the_basics.html)
- **Works Cited:** "Each entry in the list of works cited is composed of facts common to most works—the MLA core elements. They are assembled in a specific order." On containers: "When the source being documented forms part of a larger whole, the larger whole can be thought of as a container that holds the source." — [MLA Style Center: Works Cited: A Quick Guide](https://style.mla.org/works-cited/works-cited-a-quick-guide/)
- A model Works Cited entry: Sipher, Roger. "So That Nobody Has to Go to School If They Don't Want To." *The New York Times*, 19 Dec. 1977, p. 31. — [Purdue OWL: Sample Essay](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing_sample_essay.html)

#### Engineering note: IEEE
- Last's technical-writing textbook uses numbered IEEE citations ("Cite and document your source using an IEEE note [1]"). Its reference example: [1] P.M. Dombrowski. "Can ethics be technologized? Lessons from Challenger, philosophy, and rhetoric." In IEEE Transactions of Professional Communication, vol. 38.3, Sept. 1995, pp. 146-50. — [Last: Appendix B](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/)
- The book has a full part titled "Citing and Documenting Sources in IEEE Style". — [Last: Part 6](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/part/citing/)

#### How to write a summary: rules from the sources
- Harvard's guidelines (verbatim):
  - "Identify the author and the source."
  - "Represent the original source accurately."
  - "Present the source's central claim clearly."
  - "Don't summarize each point in the same order as the original source; focus on giving your reader the most important parts of the source."
  - "Use your own words. Don't provide a long quotation in the summary unless the actual language from the source is going to be important for your reader to see."
- Harvard's model is a 7-sentence APA summary of Milgram's 12-page essay. It opens: "Stanley Milgram (1974) reports that ordinarily compassionate people will be cruel to each other if they are commanded to be by an authority figure." Harvard adds: "Depending on your assignment, your summary might be even shorter."

  — [Harvard: SPQ](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)
- Last's three-stage process:
  - **Pre-writing:** read actively; identify the thesis and main points; explain it aloud; break the text into "stages of thought".
  - **Writing:**
    - give the author and the title;
    - paraphrase the thesis;
    - describe neutrally how the thesis is developed, keeping key points and leaving out "minor details and examples";
    - don't quote, or limit quotation to one key word or phrase ("Padding your summary with quotations does not effectively condense and summarize, so will lower your mark");
    - use signal phrases, watch your tense, and cite.
  - **Rewriting:** check content and organization, then flow, then proofread.
  - An academic summary "does NOT analyze, evaluate, or critique".
  - Last's image for getting the level right: describe "the forest as a whole" without getting "bogged down in the minor details".

  — [Last: Appendix B](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/)
- **Conflict on order:** Harvard says not to summarize in the source's order, while Last's revision checklist asks "Does it follow the order of the original text?" (Sources as above.)
- UNC's warning-sign openers for unnecessary summary: "[This essay] is about…", "[This book] is the story of…", "[This author] writes about…", "[This movie] is set in…". In argument papers, keep summary to "a few sentences". — [UNC: Summary: Using it Wisely](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/summary-using-it-wisely/)
- Purdue's 3-sentence model summary of Sipher's essay: "Roger Sipher makes his case for getting rid of compulsory-attendance laws in primary and secondary schools with six arguments. These fall into three groups—first that education is for those who want to learn and by including those that don't want to learn, everyone suffers. Second, that grades would be reflective of effort and elementary school teachers wouldn't feel compelled to pass failing students. Third, that schools would both save money and save face with the elimination of compulsory-attendance laws."
  - The same page also gives a model paraphrase of the conclusion, ending "(par. 17)".
  - It gives a model quotation that uses a bracket: "[a]bolish compulsory-attendance laws…" (par. 3).
  - The full essay is on the page, so it is free SPQ practice material.

  — [Purdue OWL: Sample Essay for Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing_sample_essay.html)

### Inferences

#### Decision table (synthesis of APA, Harvard and Purdue)
| What the reader needs | Technique | Length relative to source | Quotation marks | Citation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The exact wording: a definition, a standard's requirement, a memorable line, words you will analyse or dispute | **Quote** | identical, narrow | yes (or a block quote) | always, **with page** |
| The detail or reasoning of one passage | **Paraphrase** | about the same (Harvard) or slightly shorter (Purdue) | only around unique phrases | always (page optional in APA; MLA gives page if paginated) |
| The main idea of an article or section | **Summary** | much shorter | rarely | always |

For an engineering audience, default to paraphrase and summary, and quote mainly exact definitions and specification wording. This follows APA's and Harvard's STEM advice.

The Purdue Sipher page models the whole SPQ assignment: one summary, one paraphrase and one quotation from the same source.

#### 3–5-sentence summary template (synthesis of Harvard, Last and Purdue)
1. **S1:** signal phrase, author, title, and the thesis in your own words, e.g. "In '[Title],' [Author] argues that…". Use present tense for ideas (MLA), or past tense in APA style.
2. **S2–S3 (or S4):** the key supporting points, grouped and paraphrased. Leave out examples and numbers unless they are central.
3. **Last sentence:** the author's conclusion or recommendation, not your opinion.
4. **Citation:** a narrative citation in S1 plus a page or locator where needed. Add the reference-list or Works Cited entry.

#### Worked example: a 4-sentence summary of Sipher's essay (the full text is free on the Purdue page above)
- **MLA version:** In "So That Nobody Has to Go to School If They Don't Want To," Roger Sipher argues that American public education would improve if attendance laws stopped forcing unwilling students into classrooms. He contends that these students disrupt the learning of motivated classmates, and he cites economists who found that the laws barely raised enrollment and were never properly enforced. Sipher predicts that repeal would signal that school is for learning, let grades reflect real progress, raise public regard for schools, and save the money now spent on enforcement. He concludes that schools have failed by trying to meet every social need and should return to their original job of educating (31).
- **APA version** (past-tense signal verbs): Sipher (1977) argued … He contended … He predicted … He concluded … (p. 31).
- **Why it scores well:**
  - It names the author and title, and its first sentence states the thesis.
  - Six arguments are compressed into one sentence, and it uses no quotations.
  - It stays neutral.
  - The signal phrases ("He contends", "Sipher predicts", "He concludes") keep the attribution clear throughout, which matches APA's rule for long paraphrases.

#### Common SPQ errors and how graders spot them
- **Opinion inside the summary** ("I think", "brilliantly", "obviously"). This breaks the objectivity Last requires.
- **Following the source paragraph by paragraph, or too much detail.** Last calls this missing the forest for the trees; Harvard says to lead with the most important parts.
- **Summary padded with quotations.** Last says this "will lower your mark".
- **The UNC giveaway opener** "This article is about…". Better: name what the author *argues*, *proposes* or *finds*.
- **A signal verb that overstates the source,** such as "proves" when the source only "suggests". Purdue: the verb must match your intention.
- **APA slips:** a quotation with no page number; "and" inside parentheses (it should be "&"); a full date in the in-text citation; a period placed before the citation.
- **MLA slips:** a comma or "p." between author and page, as in (Rao, p. 45). It should be (Rao 45).
- **A floating quotation:** a quoted sentence with no lead-in and no follow-up (Harvard; UNC).

## 3. Sentence patterns: the four sentence types, basic clause patterns, fixing run-ons, comma splices and fragments, and sentence variety

### Takeaway
Every sentence needs at least one **independent clause**: a subject, a verb and a complete thought. The four sentence types (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex) differ only in how many independent and dependent clauses they contain.

The common errors are all clause errors:
- **Run-on / fused sentence:** two independent clauses joined with no punctuation.
- **Comma splice:** two independent clauses joined with only a comma.
- **Fragment:** a dependent clause or phrase punctuated as if it were a sentence.

There are four standard fixes:
1. a period;
2. a semicolon (with or without a transition such as "however,");
3. a comma plus a FANBOYS conjunction;
4. subordination, which makes one clause dependent.

For a fragment, attach it to a neighbouring sentence or add the missing subject or verb. Variety then comes from mixing sentence lengths, openings and types. Technical writing leans on simple sentences and averages roughly 20–30 words per sentence.

### Cited Findings

#### Clause basics
- **Independent clause:** "a group of words that contains a subject and verb and expresses a complete thought. An independent clause is a sentence."
- **Dependent clause:** "a group of words that contains a subject and verb but does not express a complete thought. A dependent clause cannot be a sentence. Often a dependent clause is marked by a dependent marker word."
  - The markers: after, although, as, as if, because, before, even if, even though, if, in order to, since, though, unless, until, whatever, when, whenever, whether, while.
- **Connecting two independent clauses** works in one of two ways:
  - With a **coordinating conjunction** (and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet), "a comma is needed before the coordinating conjunction". Example: "Jim studied in the Sweet Shop for his chemistry quiz, but it was hard to concentrate because of the noise."
  - With an **independent marker word** (also, consequently, furthermore, however, moreover, nevertheless, therefore), "a semicolon is needed before the independent marker word". Example: "Jim studied in the Sweet Shop for his chemistry quiz; however, it was hard to concentrate because of the noise."
- **Complex sentences** combine an independent clause with a dependent one, and "Either clause can come first". "The clauses are separated with a comma when the dependent clause comes first": "Whenever a substitute taught the class, the students acted differently."

— [Purdue OWL: Independent and Dependent Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html)

#### The four sentence types
- Purdue: "Structurally, English sentences can be classified four different ways... The classifications are based on the number of independent and dependent clauses a sentence contains."
  - **Simple**: "A sentence with one independent clause and no dependent clauses." Example: "China's Han Dynasty marked an official recognition of Confucianism."
  - **Compound**: "A sentence with multiple independent clauses but no dependent clauses." Example: "The clown frightened the little girl, and she ran off screaming."
  - **Complex**: "A sentence with one independent clause and at least one dependent clause." Example: "After Mary added up all the sales, she discovered that the lemonade stand was 32 cents short."
  - **Compound-complex** (Purdue labels it "Complex-Compound"): "A sentence with multiple independent clauses and at least one dependent clause." Example: "Catch-22 is widely regarded as Joseph Heller's best novel, and because Heller served in World War II, which the novel satirizes, the zany but savage wit of the novel packs an extra punch."

  — [Purdue OWL: Sentence Types](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/sentence_types.html)
- Last (engineering textbook):
  - A simple sentence has "one main clause (one subject + one verb) and any number of phrases".
  - Compound sentences join main clauses "by coordinating conjunctions (CC) such as and, but, for, yet, nor, or, so (FANBOYS)", or by a semicolon or colon. "By coordinating the ideas, you are giving them roughly equal weight and importance."
  - Complex sentences "express complex and usually unequal relationships between ideas". "Often, it is stylistically effective to begin your sentence with the dependent clause, and place the main clause at the end for emphasis."
  - A compound-complex sentence is "usually quite long, you must be careful that it makes sense".
  - Key takeaways: "Simple sentences are great for emphasis. The[y] make great topic sentences"; "Compound sentences balance ideas"; complex sentences "show complicated relationships between ideas by subordinating one idea to another."

  — [Last, *Technical Writing Essentials*, Appendix E: Sentence Structure](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixe-sentencestructure/)
- **A compound verb does not make a compound sentence.** "A compound predicate consists of two (or more) such predicates connected", e.g., "The glacier melted, broke apart, and slipped into the sea." That is still one clause. — [Guide to Grammar and Writing: Predicates, Objects, Complements](https://guidetogrammar.org/grammar/objects.htm)

#### Basic clause (subject–verb) patterns
- *Writing for Success* (1st Canadian ed.) says "Six basic subject-verb patterns can enhance your writing":
  1. Subject → Verb
  2. Subject → Linking Verb → Noun
  3. Subject → Linking Verb → Adjective
  4. Subject → Verb → Adverb
  5. Subject → Verb → Direct Object
  6. Subject → Verb → Indirect Object → Direct Object

  It adds two notes. A direct object "receives the action of the verb". An indirect object "explains to whom or to what the action is being done... and it comes before the direct object in a sentence". It also notes that "If you use the six basic sentence patterns when you write, you should be able to avoid these errors and thus avoid writing fragments." — [Writing for Success, 1st Canadian Ed., Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)
- The Guide to Grammar fills in the remaining terms:
  - **Linking verb + subject complement:** a "predicate adjective" ("Ramonita is beautiful.") or a "predicate nominative" ("Dr. Couchworthy is acting president of the university.").
  - **Direct object:** "He hit the ball."
  - **Indirect object:** "The instructor gave his students A's."
  - **Object complement** (the S-V-DO-OC pattern): "They named their daughter Natasha", where "'daughter' is the direct object and 'Natasha' is the object complement, which renames or describes the direct object."

  — [Guide to Grammar and Writing: Predicates, Objects, Complements](https://guidetogrammar.org/grammar/objects.htm)

#### Run-ons (fused sentences) and comma splices
- **Comma splice:** "the use of a comma between two independent clauses."
  - Fixes: change the comma to a period, change it to a semicolon, or insert a dependent marker.
  - "Incorrect: I like this class, it is very interesting." Corrected: "I like this class. It is very interesting." / "I like this class; it is very interesting." / "I like this class, and it is very interesting." / "I like this class because it is very interesting." / "Because it is very interesting, I like this class."
- **Fused sentence:** "two independent clauses not separated by any form of punctuation. This error is also known as a run-on sentence."
  - "Incorrect: My professor is intelligent I've learned a lot from her." Corrected: "My professor is intelligent; moreover, I've learned a lot from her."

  — [Purdue OWL: Independent and Dependent Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html)
- Purdue's run-ons page: "Run-ons, comma splices, and fused sentences are all names given to compound sentences that are not punctuated correctly." Its two rules:
  1. "Join the two independent clauses with one of the coordinating conjunctions (and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet), and use a comma before the connecting word."
  2. With no connecting word, or with a different one, use a semicolon, e.g., "…reruns; however, she preferred to read instead."

  Example: "INCORRECT: They weren't dangerous criminals they were detectives in disguise. CORRECT: They weren't dangerous criminals; they were detectives in disguise." — [Purdue OWL: Run-ons, Comma Splices, Fused Sentences](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/runonsentences.html)
- UNC adds:
  - The em dash as a fifth fix: "My favorite Mediterranean spread is hummus—it is very garlicky."
  - "You CANNOT simply add a comma between the two sentences, or you'll end up with what's called a 'comma splice.'"
  - Two diagnostic tests (from Noguchi): "Turn your sentences into yes/no questions" and "Turn your sentences into tag questions". A run-on fails both, because only each separate clause can be turned into a question.
  - "Really long sentences can be tiring but not necessarily wrong".
  - Instructors' margin codes: "frag," "S.F.," "R.O.," or "run-on".

  — [UNC: Fragments and Run-ons](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/fragments-and-run-ons/)

#### Fragments
- UNC: a complete sentence needs "a subject (the actor in the sentence)", "a predicate (the verb or action)" and "a complete thought (it can stand alone and make sense—it's independent)".
  - Most student fragments are dependent clauses that begin with a subordinator.
  - UNC's subordinators, grouped by the relationship they show:
    - **Cause/effect:** because, since, so that.
    - **Comparison/contrast:** although, even though, though, whereas, while.
    - **Place and manner:** how, however, where, wherever.
    - **Possibility/conditions:** if, whether, unless.
    - **Relation:** that, which, who.
    - **Time:** after, as, before, since, when, whenever, while, until.
  - Fix: "every dependent clause needs to be attached to an independent clause". Example: "They took the bus because their car was in the shop."

  — [UNC: Fragments and Run-ons](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/fragments-and-run-ons/)
- Purdue: "Usually, fragments are pieces of sentences that have become disconnected from the main clause. One of the easiest ways to correct them is to remove the period between the fragment and the main clause."
  - An engineering example: "Purdue offers many majors in engineering. Such as electrical, chemical, and industrial engineering." Revised: "Purdue offers many majors in engineering, such as electrical, chemical, and industrial engineering."
  - **Fragments with no verb:** "Toys of all kinds thrown everywhere." Revised: "Toys of all kinds were thrown everywhere."
  - **Fragments with no subject** ("mixed constructions"): "By paying too much attention to polls can make a political leader unwilling to propose innovative policies." Fix by removing the preposition: "Paying too much attention to polls can make…"
  - Journalistic fragments are a newspaper convention; "For academic writing and other more formal writing situations, however, you should avoid such journalistic fragment sentences."

  — [Purdue OWL: Sentence Fragments](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/sentence_fragments.html)
- *Writing for Success* notes that fragments often start with a preposition, a dependent word, a gerund (an -ing word) or an infinitive. Examples:
  - "Incorrect: Taking deep breaths. Saul prepared for his presentation." Corrected: "Taking deep breaths, Saul prepared for his presentation."
  - "Incorrect: We needed to make 300 more paper cranes. To reach the one thousand mark." Corrected: "We needed to make 300 more paper cranes to reach the one thousand mark."

  — [Writing for Success, 1st Canadian Ed., Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)

#### Sentence variety
- Purdue's strategies (example sources are listed after the bullets):
  - "Too many sentences with the same structure and length can grow monotonous for readers."
  - "1. Vary the rhythm by alternating short and long sentences."
  - "2. Vary sentence openings." Avoid starting too many sentences with "The, It, This, or I". Purdue rewrites one sentence with 16 different openings, e.g., "Coincidentally, …", "By sheer coincidence, …", "What are the odds that…?".
  - For choppy writing, combine sentences with conjunctions, or "Link Sentences Through Subordination": "The campus parking problem is getting worse because the university is not building any new garages."
  - For repetitive patterns, start with a dependent marker ("When the TV newscaster reported the story of the earthquake, the room fell silent."). Or open with a transitional word: "…high-fat combination meals. Meanwhile, the American population faces a growing epidemic of obesity."

  — [Purdue OWL: Strategies for Variation](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html); [Purdue OWL: For Short, Choppy Sentences](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/for_short_choppy_sentences.html); [Purdue OWL: Similar Sentence Patterns or Rhythms](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/similar_sentence_patterns_or_rhythms.html)
- Last on technical writing:
  - "Technical writing tends to use simple sentence structures more often than the other types. That said, simple does not necessarily mean 'simplistic,' short, or lacking in density."
  - "The goal is to aim for an average of around 20 to 30 words per sentence. Reserve the short sentences for main points and use longer sentences for supporting points that clarify or explain cause and effect relationships. If you feel the sentence is too long, break it into two sentences."

  — [Last, 2.2 Communicating with Precision](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)

#### Identify the sentence type in 10 seconds: count clauses
- Underline each subject–verb pair and mark it IC (independent clause) or DC (dependent clause; it starts with a subordinator or a relative pronoun).

#### Worked rewrites [constructed]
1. **Comma splice:** "The sensor failed, the system shut down." Any of five fixes:
   - "The sensor failed. The system shut down."
   - "The sensor failed; the system shut down."
   - "The sensor failed, so the system shut down."
   - "Because the sensor failed, the system shut down."
   - "The sensor failed—the system shut down."
2. **"However" splice** (very common): "The test passed, however the report was late." Becomes "The test passed; however, the report was late." Or subordinate instead: "Although the test passed, the report was late."
3. **Fused sentence:** "The beam deflected too much it needed a deeper section." Becomes "The beam deflected too much; it needed a deeper section." Or: "Because the beam deflected too much, it needed a deeper section."
4. **Dependent-clause fragment:** "We rejected the batch. Because the tolerance was exceeded." Becomes "We rejected the batch because the tolerance was exceeded."
5. **"Such as" fragment:** "We tested three alloys. Such as aluminium, steel, and titanium." Becomes "We tested three alloys, such as…". Better, since all three are listed: "We tested three alloys: aluminium, steel, and titanium."
6. **-ing fragment:** "Using a finite-element model. The team predicted the peak stress." Becomes "Using a finite-element model, the team predicted the peak stress."
7. **"Which" fragment:** "The pump was oversized. Which wasted energy." Becomes "The pump was oversized, so it wasted energy." Or more concisely: "The oversized pump wasted energy."
8. **Choppy to varied:** "The bridge was built in 1965. It carries heavy traffic. Engineers inspected it last month. They found fatigue cracks." Becomes "Built in 1965, the bridge now carries heavy traffic, and when engineers inspected it last month, they found fatigue cracks." This is compound-complex, with a participial opening.
9. **Monotonous openings** (every sentence starts with "The"): "The robot picks up the part. The robot scans the barcode. The robot places the part on the conveyor." Becomes "After picking up the part, the robot scans its barcode. Once the scan is confirmed, it places the part on the conveyor."
10. **Overlong sentence** (more than 40 words, so split it, as Last advises): "The report, which was prepared by the design team after three months of testing in both laboratory and field conditions and which includes data from over 200 samples, recommends that the coating be changed because corrosion appeared earlier than predicted." Becomes "The design team prepared the report after three months of laboratory and field testing on more than 200 samples. Because corrosion appeared earlier than predicted, the report recommends a different coating."

#### Common sentence errors and how graders spot them
- **Comma splices:** a comma followed directly by a new subject pronoun (", it…", ", they…", ", this…"), or ", however," joining two clauses. Graders apply UNC's yes/no-question test, or simply look for a second subject–verb pair after the comma.
- **Fragments:** a "sentence" that begins with Because, Although, Which, Such as, For example + noun, To + verb, or an -ing word, with no main clause. Margin codes: "frag" / "S.F." (UNC).
- **Run-ons:** two complete thoughts with no punctuation between them. Margin code: "R.O." (UNC).
- **A missing comma after an introductory dependent clause** ("When the alarm sounded the operators left"). Purdue's rule: use a comma when the dependent clause comes first.
- **A comma after "that"** before a quotation or reported speech ("He said that, the test failed"). UNC: no comma after "that".
- **Monotony:** every sentence in the same S-V-O order of similar length, or every sentence starting "The"/"It"/"This" (Purdue).

## 4. Paragraph development: topic sentence, support, unity, coherence, transitions and closing; Langan's "four bases"; methods of development; model paragraphs

### Takeaway
A well-developed paragraph has six parts:
1. A **topic sentence**: topic + controlling idea, written in your own words.
2. **Specific support**: reasons, facts, statistics, examples, quotations, each one *explained*.
3. **Unity**: every sentence serves that one idea.
4. **Coherence**: a logical order made visible with transitions, repeated key terms and pronouns.
5. A **closing sentence** that ties back to the controlling idea without adding a new one.
6. Clean, correct sentences.

These line up with the course's Langan "four bases": **unity, support, coherence and sentence skills**. The *method of development* (examples, narration, description, process, cause/effect, comparison/contrast, definition, division/classification, argument) follows from the paragraph's purpose. It decides both the order of the support and which transitions fit.

### Cited Findings

#### What a paragraph is and must contain
- Purdue's definition: "A paragraph is a collection of related sentences dealing with a single topic." "The basic rule of thumb with paragraphing is to keep one idea to one paragraph." A paragraph "should contain each of the following: Unity, Coherence, A Topic Sentence, and Adequate Development."
  - **Unity:** "The entire paragraph should concern itself with a single focus. If it begins with one focus or major point of discussion, it should not end with another or wander within different ideas."
  - **Coherence** "makes the paragraph easily understandable to a reader". It comes from **logical bridges** ("The same idea of a topic is carried over from sentence to sentence"; "Successive sentences can be constructed in parallel form"). It also comes from **verbal bridges**: repeated key words, synonyms, "Pronouns can refer to nouns in previous sentences", and "Transition words can be used to link ideas from different sentences".
  - **Topic sentence:** "indicates in a general way what idea or thesis the paragraph is going to deal with". It can appear anywhere, but putting it near the beginning is "a good general rule for less experienced writers". Either way, "you should be able to easily summarize what the paragraph is about."
  - **Adequate development:** "writers should be wary of paragraphs that only have two or three sentences."
  - **When to start a new paragraph:** when you begin a new idea; to contrast information or ideas; when readers need a pause; and when ending the introduction or starting the conclusion.

  — [Purdue OWL: On Paragraphs](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- UNC: a paragraph is "a group of sentences or a single sentence that forms a unit" (quoting Lunsford and Connors). Its main idea is the "controlling idea". Every paragraph should be:
  - "Unified: All of the sentences in a single paragraph should be related to a single controlling idea (often expressed in the topic sentence of the paragraph)";
  - "Clearly related to the thesis";
  - "Coherent: The sentences should be arranged in a logical manner and should follow a definite plan for development";
  - "Well-developed: Every idea discussed in the paragraph should be adequately explained and supported through evidence and details".

  — [UNC Writing Center: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)
- *Writing for Success*:
  - **Three parts:** "A strong paragraph contains three distinct components: Topic sentence. The topic sentence is the main idea of the paragraph. Body. The body is composed of the supporting sentences that develop the main point. Conclusion. The conclusion is the final sentence that summarizes the main point."
  - **Length:** "try to keep the paragraphs longer than one sentence but shorter than two-thirds of a page of double spaced text, or roughly 75 to 200 words in length."

  — [Writing for Success, 1st Canadian Ed., Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)

#### Topic sentences
- *Writing for Success* lists "Five characteristics" of a good topic sentence (verbatim headings, with its weak/stronger examples):
  1. "A good topic sentence provides an accurate indication of what will follow in the rest of the paragraph." Weak: "People rarely give firefighters the credit they deserve…", which is too general for a paragraph about one incident. Stronger: "During the October riots, Unit 3B went beyond the call of duty."
  2. "A good topic sentence contains both a topic and a controlling idea or opinion." Weak: "In this paper, I am going to discuss the rising suicide rate among young professionals." Stronger: "The rising suicide rate among young professionals is a cause for immediate concern."
  3. "A good topic sentence is clear and easy to follow." Stronger: "Most forms of writing can be improved by first creating an outline."
  4. "A good topic sentence does not include supporting details." Weak: "Salaries should be capped in baseball for many reasons, most importantly so we don't allow the same team to win year after year." Stronger: "Introducing a salary cap would improve the game of baseball for many reasons."
  5. "A good topic sentence engages the reader by using interesting vocabulary."

  — [Writing for Success, Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)
- UNC's sandwich: "the top slice of bread (the first sentence of the paragraph) explains the topic (or controlling idea) of the paragraph. And, the bottom slice (the last sentence of the paragraph) tells the reader how the paragraph relates to the broader argument." UNC's repair of a paragraph with no topic sentence adds one at the start: "Although most people consider piranhas to be quite dangerous, they are, for the most part, entirely harmless." — [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)
- Harvard: when you use sources, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence "in your own words rather than beginning with a quotation or an idea from a source". — [Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)

#### Support and development
- *Writing for Success*: "Most paragraphs contain three to six supporting sentences depending on the audience and purpose for writing. A supporting sentence usually offers one of the following: Reason; Fact; Statistic; Quotation; Example."
  - For persuasion, "you should rely on facts, statistics, and concrete examples, rather than personal opinions."
  - Its labelled model is the hybrid-car paragraph. Topic sentence: "There are numerous advantages to owning a hybrid car." It is supported by a statistic, a fact, a reason, an example and a quotation, then closes with a concluding sentence.

  — [Writing for Success, Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)
- UNC's "5-step process" for an illustration paragraph:
  1. Decide on a controlling idea and create a topic sentence.
  2. Elaborate on the controlling idea.
  3. Give an example (or several).
  4. Explain each example. "NONE of your examples should be left unexplained."
  5. "Complete the paragraph's idea or transition into the next paragraph."

  Steps 3–4 repeat as needed. The finished model is UNC's piranha paragraph. — [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)
- Purdue's ways to develop a paragraph:
  - "Use examples and illustrations"
  - "Cite data (facts, statistics, evidence, details, and others)"
  - "Examine testimony (what other people say such as quotes and paraphrases)"
  - "Use an anecdote or story"
  - "Define terms in the paragraph"
  - "Compare and contrast"
  - "Evaluate causes and reasons"
  - "Examine effects and consequences"
  - "Analyze the topic"
  - "Describe the topic"
  - "Offer a chronology of an event (time segments)"

  — [Purdue OWL: On Paragraphs](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)

#### Methods of organization named in the sources
- UNC's methods:
  - "Narration: Tell a story. Go chronologically, from start to finish."
  - "Description: Provide specific details about what something looks, smells, tastes, sounds, or feels like. Organize spatially, in order of appearance, or by topic."
  - "Process: Explain how something works, step by step. Perhaps follow a sequence—first, second, third."
  - "Classification: Separate into groups or explain the various parts of a topic."
  - "Illustration: Give examples and explain how those examples support your point."

  — [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)
- For professional (technical) argument, Last's book includes "2.3 Writing to Persuade: Understand how to use rhetoric in a professional context, avoiding logical fallacies and inappropriate marketing language." — [Last: Part 2, Professional Style](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/part/style/)

#### Unity problems
- UNC: "If a paragraph has more than one main idea, consider eliminating sentences that relate to the second idea, or split the paragraph into two or more paragraphs, each with only one main idea." UNC suggests reverse outlining to test unity. Its example cuts two sentences about cooking piranhas from a paragraph about piranhas being harmless. — [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)

#### Coherence and transitions
- UNC on what transitions do:
  - "transitions tell readers what to do with the information you present to them".
  - "Transitions cannot substitute for good organization, but they can make your organization clearer and easier to follow."
  - Kinds: transitions between sections, between paragraphs ("a word or two (however, for example, similarly), a phrase, or a sentence") and within paragraphs.
  - Signs you need them: comments such as "choppy," "jumpy," "abrupt," "flow," "need signposts," or "how is this related?".
  - UNC's table, by relationship:
    - **Similarity:** also, in the same way, just as … so too, likewise, similarly.
    - **Exception/Contrast:** but, however, in spite of, on the one hand … on the other hand, nevertheless, nonetheless, notwithstanding, in contrast, on the contrary, still, yet.
    - **Sequence/Order:** first, second, third, … next, then, finally.
    - **Time:** after, afterward, at last, before, currently, during, earlier, immediately, later, meanwhile, now, recently, simultaneously, subsequently, then.

  — [UNC: Transitions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/transitions/)
- UNC's piranha paragraph shows within-paragraph transitions: "But there are two situations in which a piranha bite is likely. The first is when… The second is when…". UNC notes that "the phrases 'the first' and 'the second' help the reader follow the organization". — [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)
- Purdue's transitional devices by purpose (excerpt):
  - **To add:** furthermore, moreover, in addition, first (second, etc.).
  - **To compare:** whereas, on the other hand, however, by comparison, in contrast, conversely.
  - **To prove:** because, for, since, evidently, indeed, in fact.
  - **To show exception:** yet, still, however, nevertheless, despite.
  - **To show time:** immediately, thereafter, soon, then, later, previously.
  - **To repeat:** in brief, as has been noted.
  - **To emphasize:** definitely, in fact, indeed, without a doubt.
  - **To show sequence:** first, second, next, then, following this, subsequently, consequently, thus, therefore, hence.
  - **To give an example:** for example, for instance, in this case, to illustrate.
  - **To summarize or conclude:** in brief, on the whole, in conclusion, as a result, consequently, thus.

  — [Purdue OWL: Transitional Devices](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/transitions_and_transitional_devices/transitional_devices.html)
- *Writing for Success* gives three kinds of transition:
  - "First" and "second" show sequence.
  - "because" is a transition "of consequence" (others: "as a result, so that, since, or for this reason").
  - A summarizing transition opens the concluding sentence ("In conclusion, …").

  — [Writing for Success, Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)
- Last's 7 C's (technical writing):
  - Clear writing: "ensure that each sentence conveys one idea, and that each paragraph thoroughly develops one unified concept."
  - Coherent writing uses "transitional words and phrases, structural markers, planned repetition, sentences with clear subjects, headings that are clear, and effective and parallel lists". Without this, writing "often sounds 'choppy'".

  — [Last, 2.2 Communicating with Precision](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)

#### Closing (concluding) sentences
- *Writing for Success*: "An effective concluding sentence draws together all the ideas you have raised in your paragraph. It reminds readers of the main point—the topic sentence—without restating it in exactly the same words."
  - In the hybrid model, "advantages" in the topic sentence becomes "benefits" in the conclusion.
  - "You should avoid introducing any new ideas into your concluding sentence."

  — [Writing for Success, Ch. 3](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)

#### Model paragraphs [constructed]
Labels: **TS** = topic sentence; **S** = support; *italics* = transitions; **CS** = closing sentence.

**(A) Examples method.**
- **TS:** Engineers protect critical systems by building in redundancy, so that one failed part does not stop the whole system.
- **S1:** *For example*, large passenger aircraft are designed with more than one independent hydraulic system, so pilots can still move the control surfaces if one system loses pressure.
- **S2:** *Similarly*, hospitals keep backup generators that start automatically when mains power fails, which keeps life-support equipment running.
- **S3:** *In computing*, data centres store copies of the same data on separate drives or servers; *as a result*, a single disk failure does not erase anyone's files.
- **Explanation:** *In each case*, the extra component adds cost and weight, but it buys reliability exactly where a failure would be dangerous or expensive.
- **CS:** Redundancy, *in short*, is a deliberate trade of efficiency for safety.

Why it works:
- Unity: every example illustrates redundancy.
- Support: three specific examples, each followed by its effect, plus an explaining sentence (UNC steps 3–4).
- Coherence: the transitions mark each new example.
- Closing: rewords the controlling idea without adding a new one.

**(B) Process method.**
- **TS:** Measuring the resistance of a component with a digital multimeter is a simple four-step process.
- **Step 1:** *First*, switch off the circuit and, ideally, disconnect one leg of the resistor, because other components in the circuit can distort the reading.
- **Step 2:** *Next*, turn the dial to the resistance (Ω) setting and plug the probes into the correct sockets.
- **Step 3:** *Then* touch the probes to the two leads of the resistor and wait for the display to settle.
- **Step 4:** *Finally*, compare the reading with the value marked on the component; a result within the stated tolerance, such as ±5 percent, means the part is good.
- **CS:** Following these steps in order prevents both false readings and damage to the meter.

Why it works: the order is chronological, and each step has one action plus a reason where needed. The sequence transitions come from Purdue's "To Show Sequence" list.

**(C) Cause/effect method.**
- **TS:** De-icing salt is a major cause of damage to concrete bridge decks in cold climates.
- **Cause chain:**
  - When salt is spread on roads in winter, chloride ions dissolve in the meltwater and gradually soak into the concrete.
  - *Once* enough chloride reaches the steel reinforcing bars, it breaks down the protective layer around the steel, and the bars begin to rust.
  - *Because* rust takes up more space than the original steel, it pushes outward and causes the surrounding concrete to crack and flake off.
- **Effect loop:** *As a result*, even more water and salt can enter, *so* the damage speeds up over time.
- **CS:** *In short*, a chemical that keeps roads safe in winter slowly weakens the structures that carry those roads.

Why it works: the chain runs cause → mechanism → effect → feedback, linked by "consequence" transitions (*Writing for Success*; Purdue's "To Prove" and "To Summarize" lists). The closing restates the idea without bringing in a new topic such as remedies.

**(D) Comparison/contrast method** (point by point; bonus).
- **TS:** Bolted and welded steel connections each suit different situations.
- **Point 1 (strength and continuity):** A welded joint is continuous and rigid, and it needs no holes that weaken the members; *however*, it requires skilled welders and careful inspection, and welding on site is slowed by weather and awkward access.
- **Point 2 (installation and maintenance):** Bolted joints, *in contrast*, are quick to install on site, easy to inspect, and can be taken apart later, *although* the bolt holes reduce the cross-section of the connected parts.
- **CS:** *Consequently*, many structures use welded connections made in the workshop and bolted connections made on site, combining the strengths of both.

#### Common paragraph errors and how graders spot them
- **An "announcement" topic sentence** ("In this paragraph I will discuss…"). *Writing for Success* lists this as weak because it has no controlling idea.
- **A topic sentence that is too broad, too narrow, or stuffed with supporting detail** (*Writing for Success*, characteristics 1 and 4).
- **A quotation as the first sentence** (Harvard).
- **Unexplained examples:** a list of facts with no "this shows that…". UNC: "NONE of your examples should be left unexplained".
- **Unity breaks:** a sentence about a side topic, or two controlling ideas (UNC). Graders find these by reverse-outlining your paragraph.
- **Choppy writing with no transitions,** or transitions used wrongly ("however" where no contrast exists). Margin comments: "choppy," "jumpy," "flow" (UNC).
- **A new idea in the closing sentence** (*Writing for Success*).
- **A two-or-three-sentence paragraph,** which is probably underdeveloped (Purdue).

## 5. Quotation marks: US vs UK rules, punctuation inside or outside, quotations within quotations, titles, block quotations, common errors, and drills

### Takeaway
American usage, followed by Purdue, APA, MLA and Harvard, has five core rules:
1. Use **double** marks, and **single** marks for a quotation inside a quotation.
2. **Periods and commas go inside** the closing mark. The one exception: when a parenthetical citation follows, the period moves to after the citation.
3. **Colons and semicolons go outside.**
4. **Question marks and exclamation points go inside only if they belong to the quoted words.**
5. Titles of short works (articles, chapters, songs, episodes) take quotation marks; long works take italics.

British usage (Cambridge; GOV.UK) often puts single marks first and double marks inside them.

Long quotations become indented **block quotations with no quotation marks**: 40 words or more in APA, more than four lines of prose in MLA.

#### Core US rules (Purdue OWL)
- **Purpose and basics:**

#### APA 7
- "Place periods and commas within closing single or double quotation marks. Place other punctuation marks inside quotation marks only when they are part of the quoted material."

#### Harvard Guide
- **The US system:** "In the system of punctuation used in the United States, periods and commas go inside quotation marks except when you use in-text citations. In those cases, periods and commas go outside the quotation marks after the parenthetical citation."
  - Example: Batkin argues that "what is needed to shore up essential protections for low-income people is a principle behind wealth-based equal process" (2021, p.1553).
  - With a footnote instead of an in-text citation, the period stays inside the quotation marks.
- **Question marks** go inside when part of the quotation: … saying, "And I still have that feeling a little bit. Which of my works have done the most good?" (Gross). They go outside when part of your sentence: But what are we to make of Harding's admonition to "write while you can no matter the sacrifice"?
- **Colons and semicolons** "go outside quotation marks unless they are part of the quotation". Example: Sandel argues that "the problem is not the drift to mechanism but the drive to mastery"; in other words, …
- **Single vs double:** "For MLA, APA, and Chicago, you should use double quotation marks when you quote material from a source." Use single marks for material that was already quoted in your source. Example: Silber writes that "Didion begins in her familiarly sardonic tone, observing that 'the wives had pretty dresses, and everyone liked Las Vegas.'"

- **Block quotations by style:** MLA blocks are for "any quotation that is more than four typed lines of prose or three lines of poetry". APA blocks are for "any quotation that is 40 or more words". In Chicago, "you can decide whether to set off your block quotations or to run them into the body of your essay". Always introduce and follow a block with your own words ("Don't float the quotation!").

- **Ellipses:** "You don't need to use the ellipsis at the beginning or the end of a quotation".

#### UNC
- **Citations and note numbers:** "Place the parenthetical reference or footnote/endnote number after—not within—the closed quotation mark." Examples: Roosevelt declared, "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" (Roosevelt, Public Papers, 11). / Roosevelt declared, "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself."1
- **Periods and commas** go inside, except before an internal citation. "Place all other punctuation marks (colons, semicolons, exclamation marks, question marks) outside the quotation marks, except when they were part of the original quotation." Examples: "…started charging $15 per hour for parking"! (the writer's own exclamation) vs. The coach yelled, "Run!"
- **Nesting:** single marks inside double; in the rare third level, use double again. Example: "'But the Emperor has nothing on at all!' cried a little child."
- **Ellipses:**
  - Write the ellipsis as ". . ." with spaces.
  - Don't change the meaning by what you omit.
  - Don't put an ellipsis at the start or end unless the reader needs to know the quotation was cut.
  - Keep punctuation that survives the cut: ". . . ." at the end of a sentence; ". . . ," before a clause.
- **Brackets:** use them for changed pronouns or tenses, added context, and [*sic*]. "Always italicize 'sic' and enclose it in brackets."
  - **Note the conflict:** Harvard says MLA does *not* italicize *sic*.
  - Do not bracket a capitalization change at the start of a quotation.
- **Block quotations:** use one "If that passage exceeds four lines (some sources say five)". Set it up with your own words and a colon, indent it, use no quotation marks, and follow it with your own words.

— [UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)

#### Placement cheat-sheet (US)
- **, and .** go **inside**: … "on Monday," he said. / … "fear itself." The citation exception: … "outdated" (Chen, 2021, p. 8).
- **: and ;** go **outside**: … "three critical gaps": training … / … "a definitive step forward"; others …
- **? and !** go where they **belong**:
  - Asked by the speaker: inside, e.g., She asked, "Is it safe?"
  - Asked by you: outside, e.g., Did he say "safe"?
  - Never doubled: "Is it safe?". (wrong)
- **Citation** goes **after** the closing mark, and the end punctuation goes after the citation (APA, MLA, Harvard, UNC). **Block quotations** are the exception: there the citation comes after the final period, with no period after it.

#### Common quotation-mark errors and how graders spot them
1. A period or comma outside the closing mark with no citation following (US): "outdated".
2. A period before the parenthetical citation: "humidity." (45). It should be "humidity" (45).
3. A semicolon or colon placed inside the closing mark.
4. A question mark in the wrong place, or doubled punctuation (?". or ?").
5. Capitalizing a quoted fragment, or capitalizing the second half of an interrupted quotation.
6. Quotation marks around a paraphrase or indirect quotation. Purdue: none are needed, but the citation still is.
7. Quotation marks around a block quotation, or a block quotation with no lead-in or follow-up.
8. Single marks for first-level quotations in a US-style paper, or mixing the two systems.
9. Unclosed quotation marks (Purdue: "always come in pairs").
10. Quotation marks used for emphasis rather than irony (We sell "fresh" bread). Purdue allows them only for irony or "reservation".
11. Changing quoted words without brackets or an ellipsis, or using an ellipsis at the start or end of a quotation.
12. A comma after "that" before a quotation ("He said that, 'the…'"). UNC: no comma after "that".

4. *Did the professor really call our design "elegant?"*
   - **Answer:** Did the professor really call our design "elegant"?

## 6. Writing in the "four C's" (clear, concise, coherent, complete) and the wider "7 C's" of professional communication

### Takeaway
The open engineering textbook *Technical Writing Essentials* (Suzan Last) lists seven C's of professional writing: **clear, coherent, concise, concrete, correct, complete and courteous**. The course's four C's (clear, concise, coherent, complete) are a subset of that list.

The C's can pull against each other. A very concise text can become incomplete or curt, and Last's rule is to **always give priority to clarity**.

Two published methods supply most of the before/after technique:
- **Concision (Purdue, UNC):** cut redundancy and filler; turn nominalizations into verbs and passive into active; replace roundabout phrases ("circumlocutions") with single words.
- **Coherence:** link ideas with transitions, repeated key terms and a logical order.

### Cited Findings

#### The 7 C's (Last, *Technical Writing Essentials*, §2.2)
- "The 7 C's are simply seven words that begin with C that characterize strong professional style." They produce writing that is "Clear, Coherent, Concise, Concrete, Correct, Complete, Courteous." Last's definitions:
  - **CLEAR:** "CLEAR writing involves knowing what you want to say before you say it because often a lack of clarity comes from unclear thinking or poor planning… Clear writing conveys the purpose of the document immediately to the reader; it matches vocabulary to the audience, avoiding jargon and unnecessary technical or obscure language while at the same time being precise. In clarifying your ideas, ensure that each sentence conveys one idea, and that each paragraph thoroughly develops one unified concept."
  - **COHERENT:** "COHERENT writing ensures that the reader can easily follow your ideas and your train of thought. One idea should lead logically into the next through the use of transitional words and phrases, structural markers, planned repetition, sentences with clear subjects, headings that are clear, and effective and parallel lists. Writing that lacks coherence often sounds 'choppy' and ideas seem disconnected or incomplete."
  - **CONCISE:** "CONCISE writing uses the least words possible to convey the most meaning while still maintaining clarity. Avoid unnecessary padding, awkward phrasing, overuse of 'to be' forms (is, are, was, were, am, be, being), long preposition strings, vagueness, unnecessary repetition and redundancy. Use active verbs whenever possible…"
  - **CONCRETE:** writing "using specific, precise language to paint a picture for your readers… Use measurable descriptors whenever possible; avoid vague terms like 'big' or 'good.'"
  - **CORRECT:** "uses standard English punctuation, sentence structure, usage, and grammar. Being correct also means providing accurate information, as well as using the right document type and form for the task."
  - **COMPLETE:** "includes all requested information and answers all relevant questions. The more concrete and specific you are, the more likely your document will be complete as well."
  - **COURTEOUS:** "entails designing a reader-friendly, easy-to-read document; using tactful language and appropriate modes of addressing the audience; and avoiding potentially offensive terminology, usage, and tone."
- **Trade-offs:** "what if being too concise results in a tone that sounds terse, or an idea that seems incomplete?… Be mindful of the tradeoffs, and always give priority to being clear: writing that lacks clarity cannot be understood and therefore cannot achieve its purpose. Writing that adheres to the 7 C's helps to establish your credibility as a technical professional."
- **Last's one-line priority list** (Figure 2.2.1 description):
  - "Clear: Plan ahead! Know your purpose and convey your ideas in a unified manner."
  - "Coherent: Organize your thoughts in a logical, structured progression."
  - "Concise: Budget your words wisely; ensure your writing contains only what's necessary."
  - "Concrete: Use specific and precise language, use measurable descriptors and avoid vague language."
  - "Correct: Adhere to proper grammar, punctuation, and document structure."
  - "Complete: Give all the important information and answer all relevant questions."
  - "Courteous: Format so that the document is easy to read. Use appropriate and tactful language."
- **Precise wording rules** with Last's examples:
  1. "Replace abstract nouns with verbs": acquisition→acquire, analysis→analyze, recommendation→recommend, assessment→assess.
  2. "Prefer short words to long words and phrases": utilize→use, commence→begin, "at this point in time"→now, "due to the fact that"→because, "has the ability to"→can. "Write to express, not impress."
  3. "Avoid clichés": "ballpark figure"→about, "needless to say"→of course.
  4. "Avoid cluttered constructions": redundancies such as "join together", "refer back", "plan ahead", "end result", "positive benefits", "mutual agreement".
  5. "Use accurate wording": use consistent terminology, separate fact from opinion ("we recommend"), and avoid absolutes and overused intensifiers or qualifiers ("very", "really", "basically", "kind of").
  6. "Prefer the active voice": "The outfielder throws the ball" vs "The ball was thrown (by the outfielder)".
- **Sentence length:** aim for "an average of around 20 to 30 words per sentence".
- **Practice exercise:** "Exercise 2.5 Revise for clarity" asks students to rewrite an 80-word "garbled memo" (counting the bracketed numerals) so that it follows the 7 C's. A model rewrite appears under Inferences below.

— [Last, *Technical Writing Essentials*, 2.2 Communicating with Precision (CC BY 4.0)](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)

#### Concision techniques with before/after pairs (Purdue OWL)
- **Definition:** "The goal of concise writing is to use the most effective words. Concise writing does not always have the fewest words, but it always uses the strongest ones… Like bad employees, words that don't accomplish enough should be fired."
- **Replace vague phrases with specific words:**
  - "The politician talked about several of the merits of after-school programs in his speech (14 words)" becomes "The politician touted after-school programs in his speech. (8 words)".
  - "Our Web site has made available many of the things you can use for making a decision on the best dentist. (20 words)" becomes "Our website presents criteria for determining the best dentist. (9 words)".
- **Interrogate every word:** "The teacher demonstrated some of the various ways and methods for cutting words from my essay that I had written for class. (22 words)" becomes "The teacher demonstrated methods for cutting words from my essay. (10 words)".
- **Combine sentences:** "The supposed crash of a UFO in Roswell, New Mexico aroused interest in extraterrestrial life. This crash is rumored to have occurred in 1947. (24 words)" becomes "The supposed 1947 crash of a UFO in Roswell, New Mexico aroused interest in extraterrestrial life. (16 words)".

— [Purdue OWL: Concision](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html)
- **Eliminating words:**
  - Explain only what readers need: "I received your inquiry that you wrote about tennis rackets yesterday, and read it thoroughly." becomes "I received your inquiry about tennis rackets yesterday."
  - Cut filler such as "kind of, sort of, type of, really, basically, for all intents and purposes, definitely, actually, generally, individual, specific, particular".
  - Cut redundant pairs: "past memories, various differences, each individual, basic fundamentals, true facts, important essentials, future plans, terrible tragedy, end result, final outcome, free gift, past history, unexpected surprise, sudden crisis". Also "very unique" is illogical.
  - Cut redundant categories: "large in size, often times, of a bright color, heavy in weight, period in time, round in shape…". Example: "During that time period, many car buyers preferred cars that were pink in color and shiny in appearance. (18 words)" becomes "During that period, many car buyers preferred pink, shiny cars. (10 words)".

  — [Purdue OWL: Eliminating Words](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/eliminating_words.html)
- **Changing phrases:**
  - Phrases become single words or adjectives: "The employee with ambition" becomes "The ambitious employee".
  - Clauses become phrases: "The report, which was released recently" becomes "The recently released report".
  - Passive becomes active: "Your figures were checked by the research department." becomes "The research department checked your figures."
  - Nominalizations become verb phrases.

  — [Purdue OWL: Changing Phrases](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/changing_phrases.html)
- **Common pitfalls:**
  - Expletive openings: "It is the governor who signs or vetoes bills." becomes "The governor signs or vetoes bills."; "There are four rules that should be observed" becomes "Four rules should be observed".
  - Nominalizations: "The function of this department is the collection of accounts. (10 words)" becomes "This department collects accounts. (4 words)".
  - Unnecessary infinitives: "The duty of a clerk is to check all incoming mail and to record it." becomes "A clerk checks and records all incoming mail."
  - Circumlocutions:
    - "the reason for / due to the fact that / in light of the fact that" become "because", "since" or "why";
    - "it is crucial that / it is necessary that" become "must" or "should";
    - "has the ability to" becomes "can";
    - "it is possible that / there is a chance that" become "may", "might" or "could".

  — [Purdue OWL: Avoid Common Pitfalls](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/avoid_common_pitfalls.html)

#### Concision and wordiness (UNC)
- Instructors' margin words that signal wordiness: "wordy," "passive voice," "filler" or "irrelevant".
- **Six strategies,** each with a before/after:
  1. "Eliminate redundant pairs": "For each and every book you purchase…" becomes "For every book you purchase…".
  2. "Delete unnecessary qualifiers" (actually, really, basically, probably, very, definitely…).
  3. "Identify and reduce prepositional phrases": a 43-word sentence about a basketball loss becomes "UNC's basketball team lost the Final Four game against Kansas because it could not consistently rebound the ball."
  4. "Locate and delete unnecessary modifiers": "anticipate in advance" becomes "anticipate".
  5. "Replace a phrase with a word": "In the event that going out for the purpose of eating with them cannot be avoided, it is necessary that we first go to the ATM, in light of the fact that I am out of cash." becomes "If we must go out to eat with them, we should first go to the ATM because I am out of cash."
  6. "Identify negatives and change them to affirmatives".
- **Passive voice:** "The 1780 constitution of Massachusetts was written by John Adams." becomes "John Adams wrote the 1780 Massachusetts Constitution." UNC adds that passive voice "is not a grammatical error, and it can be useful, especially in scientific writing."
- **At paper level:** "Every paragraph in your essay must have a purpose."

— [UNC Writing Center: Writing Concisely](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conciseness-handout/)

#### Coherence before/after (UNC)
- **Before:** a piranha paragraph listing facts with no organizing signals.
- **After:** the same content organized as "except in two main situations… But there are two situations in which a piranha bite is likely. The first is when… The second is when…". UNC notes that "the phrases 'the first' and 'the second' help the reader follow the organization of the ideas in the paragraph."

— [UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)

### Inferences

#### The course's four C's: definitions, from Last, with graders' checks
| C | One-line definition (after Last) | What a grader checks |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | The purpose is obvious at once; words fit the reader; one idea per sentence, one unified concept per paragraph | Can I state the main point after one reading? Is the jargon necessary or explained? Are there ambiguous words such as "it", "this", "things", "issues"? |
| **Concise** | The fewest words that keep the full meaning | Redundant pairs, filler qualifiers, "there is/it is… that", nominalizations, unnecessary passives, circumlocutions |
| **Coherent** | Ideas follow logically, with visible links | Transitions, consistent key terms, a logical order (time, importance, cause to effect), parallel lists, no "choppy" jumps |
| **Complete** | All the information the reader needs or asked for | Who, what, when, where, how much, what action is needed and by when; numbers with units; attachments mentioned |

#### Before/after rewrites [constructed unless a source is named]
- **Clear** (and concrete):
  - *Before:* "The thing with the sensor might have caused some issues with the readings at some point."
  - *After:* "The temperature sensor on Line 2 read 4 °C too high from 10:00 to 14:00 on 14 October."
  - Changes: a vague noun replaced by a named part; "issues" replaced by the specific error; time and size added.
- **Concise:**
  - *Before:* "Due to the fact that the pump was in a state of malfunction, it was decided by the team that a replacement should be made." (25 words)
  - *After:* "Because the pump malfunctioned, the team replaced it." (8 words)
  - Techniques: a circumlocution replaced, a nominalization turned into a verb, passive turned into active (Purdue; UNC).
- **Concise** (Purdue's own pair): "The teacher demonstrated some of the various ways and methods for cutting words from my essay that I had written for class." becomes "The teacher demonstrated methods for cutting words from my essay." — [Purdue OWL: Concision](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html)
- **Coherent:**
  - *Before:* "The new valve reduced leaks. Maintenance costs fell. Installation took longer than planned. Operators needed training."
  - *After:* "The new valve reduced leaks; as a result, maintenance costs fell. However, installation took longer than planned, partly because operators first needed training on the new design."
  - Changes: cause and contrast transitions added; the training sentence now explains the delay.
- **Complete** (also courteous):
  - *Before:* "hi, the test failed. pls fix asap."
  - *After:*
    > Dear Ms. Karimova,
    > The tensile test on sample B-3 failed this morning: it fractured at 410 MPa, below the 450 MPa specification. I have attached the stress–strain curve and the test log. Could you check the heat-treatment record for batch B and let me know by Thursday, 23 October, whether we should retest? Thank you,
    > Timur Rakhimov, Materials Lab
  - The "after" version answers what, where, how far off, what evidence, what action and by when. It is also courteous, correct and concrete.
- **Concrete:** "The new design is much better." becomes "The new bracket is 18% lighter and passed 10⁶ load cycles without cracking."
- **Correct:** "The data shows that, the beam fail at 12 kN." becomes "The data show that the beam fails at 12 kN." Note that some style guides accept "data shows"; follow the course style.
- **Courteous:** "You obviously didn't read the spec." becomes "Section 3.2 of the specification may answer this; I have highlighted the relevant line."

#### Model answer to Last's Exercise 2.5 (the "garbled memo")
- **Original (about 80 words):** "When workloads increase to a level requiring hours in excess of an employee's regular duty assignment, and when such work is estimated to require a full shift of eight (8) hours or more on two (2) or more consecutive days, even though unscheduled days intervene, an employee's tour of duty shall be altered so as to include the hours when such work must be done, unless an adverse impact would result from such employee's absence from his previously scheduled assignment." — [Last, 2.2](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)
- **Model rewrite** (mine, about 70 words, but split into scannable parts):
  > **Schedule changes for extra work**
  > We will move an employee's shift to cover extra work when both of these apply:
  > 1. the work needs more hours than the employee's regular assignment; and
  > 2. it is expected to take at least one full 8-hour shift on two or more consecutive days (days off in between do not break the sequence).
  >
  > We will not move the shift if the employee's absence would harm their regular assignment.
- **Why it is better:**
  - Clear: one idea per sentence, with the conditions listed.
  - Coherent: a parallel numbered list.
  - Concise: "tour of duty shall be altered" becomes "move an employee's shift".
  - Complete: every condition is kept.
  - Courteous: the plain "we" address, and the gender-neutral "their" replaces "his".

#### How graders spot violations of the C's
- **Not concise:**
  - padding phrases such as "It is important to note that", "At this point in time", "Due to the fact that";
  - redundant pairs ("each and every", "end result");
  - stacked nouns ending in -tion or -ment followed by "of";
  - "There is/are … that" openings.
- **Not clear or concrete:** words such as "thing", "stuff", "issues", "big", "good", "a lot"; pronouns ("it", "this") with no clear antecedent; unexplained acronyms.
- **Not coherent:** no transitions; key terms that keep changing (the "sensor" becomes the "device", then the "unit"); a jumbled order. Graders write "choppy", "flow?" or "how is this related?" (UNC).
- **Not complete:** missing numbers, units, dates, owners or deadlines; a question asked in the prompt but left unanswered (Last: "answers all relevant questions").

#### 20 practice items with model answers
These items are constructed. Fictional authors and dates only show citation format; the rule each answer applies is named in brackets.

**A. Plagiarism and paraphrase**

1. **Classify.** A student copies two sentences from a journal article, changes "significant" to "major" and "demonstrates" to "shows," and adds "(Kim, 2021)".
   - **Model answer:** This is an inadequate paraphrase, also called mosaic plagiarism or patchwriting, and it is still plagiarism. The language and structure stay too close to the original, and Harvard says it is plagiarism "even if you do provide a citation". Either quote the sentences exactly, with quotation marks and a page number, or genuinely restate the idea. [Harvard]
2. **Classify.** A student writes an excellent paraphrase of an idea from a company website that lists no author. They give no citation "because there was no author."
   - **Model answer:** This is an uncited paraphrase, which is plagiarism. Harvard says that with "a website that has no clear authorship, you are still responsible for citing the website". In APA, use the organization as a group author, or the title and year if there is no author. [Harvard; APA author–date]
3. **Cite or not?**
   - (a) Water boils at 100 °C at sea level.
   - (b) "57% of first-year engineering students reported…" taken from a survey.
   - (c) An idea from your professor's lecture.
   - (d) Your own lab measurements.
   - **Model answer:**
     - (a) This is generally common knowledge, so no citation is needed (Purdue's and UNC's tests). Cite it if in doubt.
     - (b) Yes, because a specific statistic needs a source.
     - (c) Yes. Harvard: ideas "your professor offers in lecture (you should always cite these)".
     - (d) No citation is needed for your own data, but describe how you obtained it. (That last point is my inference.)
4. **Paraphrase in APA.** The original (Park, 2022, p. 33): "Additive manufacturing allows engineers to produce complex geometries, such as internal cooling channels, that would be difficult or impossible to machine. However, printed metal parts often contain small pores that reduce their fatigue strength unless they are post-processed."
   - **Model answer:** Park (2022) notes that 3D printing lets designers build shapes that conventional machining cannot easily make, internal cooling passages being one example; the drawback is that printed metal components tend to contain tiny voids, which weaken them under repeated loading unless they receive further treatment after printing (p. 33).
   - Why it works: it has a new structure, keeps the hedge ("often" became "tend to"), keeps the technical meaning, and is cited. [Purdue 6 steps; Harvard; APA]
5. **Diagnose.** The original: "Regular calibration of measuring instruments is essential, since even small errors can accumulate across a production run and result in parts that do not fit together." The student wrote: "Regular calibration of measuring tools is vital, because even minor errors can add up across a production run and cause parts that do not fit together."
   - **Model answer:** There are four problems.
     1. The structure and clause order are identical.
     2. The synonyms are swapped one for one (instruments→tools, essential→vital, small→minor, accumulate→add up).
     3. Whole strings are copied: "Regular calibration of", "across a production run", "parts that do not fit together".
     4. There is no citation.

     Together these make an inadequate paraphrase that is also uncited. A better version: "Because tiny measurement errors compound over a long production run until components no longer assemble properly, instruments must be checked against a standard at regular intervals (Author, Year)." [Harvard; UNC]

**B. Summary, quotation and citation**

6. **Quote, paraphrase or summarize?**
   - (a) Your report's glossary needs a design code's exact definition of "factor of safety".
   - (b) Your introduction needs the main argument of a 20-page report.
   - (c) You need a study's detailed explanation of why a sensor drifted.
   - (d) You will analyse a CEO's controversial wording in a press release.
   - **Model answer:**
     - (a) Quote, because it is an exact definition (APA).
     - (b) Summarize.
     - (c) Paraphrase, because you need the detail of one passage.
     - (d) Quote: you will discuss the actual language and respond to exact wording (Harvard; APA).
7. **Summarize in three sentences (APA).** The article is "Why Small Bridges Fail" (Aliyeva, 2025; fictional):

   > "Most bridge failures do not involve dramatic collapses of famous structures. Instead, they happen on small, ageing bridges that receive little attention. Three causes recur. First, scour—the erosion of riverbed material around foundations during floods—can undermine supports without any visible warning above the water. Second, corrosion of steel slowly reduces load capacity, especially where drainage is poor. Third, many older bridges now carry heavier vehicles than they were designed for. Because inspection budgets favour large, high-profile bridges, local authorities should adopt low-cost sensors and schedule inspections according to risk rather than reputation."

   - **Model answer:** In "Why Small Bridges Fail," Aliyeva (2025) argues that bridge failures mostly occur on minor, neglected structures rather than on well-known ones. She identifies three repeated causes: floods washing away the soil that supports foundations, gradual rusting that lowers how much weight a bridge can bear, and modern vehicles that exceed original design loads. Since funding tends to go to prominent bridges, she urges local governments to install inexpensive monitoring devices and to prioritise inspections by risk.
   - Why it works: the thesis comes first, the causes are grouped, it is neutral, and it has no quotations. [Harvard; Last]
8. **Fix the APA citations.**
   - (a) According to Chen (2020), "students rarely check units" (Chen, 2020).
   - (b) (Chen and Wu, 2020)
   - (c) The first citation of a three-author work: (Lopez, Patel, and Ruiz, 2019)
   - (d) "…grew rapidly." (Ng, 2018, p. 4)
   - **Model answer:**
     - (a) According to Chen (2020), "students rarely check units" (p. 12). A quotation needs a page number, and the author and year should not be repeated.
     - (b) (Chen & Wu, 2020).
     - (c) (Lopez et al., 2019). Use "et al." even in the first citation.
     - (d) "…grew rapidly" (Ng, 2018, p. 4). The period goes after the citation.

     [APA author–date; APA quotations]
9. **Fix the MLA citations.**
   - (a) (Rao, p. 45)
   - (b) "…humidity." (Rao 45)
   - (c) Rao states that humidity matters (Rao 45).
   - **Model answer:**
     - (a) (Rao 45).
     - (b) "…humidity" (Rao 45).
     - (c) Rao states that humidity matters (45). When the author is named in the sentence, give only the page.

     [Purdue MLA in-text]

**C. Sentences**

10. **Identify the sentence type.**
    - (a) The turbine stopped.
    - (b) The turbine stopped, and the alarm sounded.
    - (c) When the wind exceeded 25 m/s, the turbine stopped.
    - (d) When the wind exceeded 25 m/s, the turbine stopped, and the operators logged the event.
    - (e) The engineers inspected and photographed the blades.
    - **Model answer:** (a) simple; (b) compound; (c) complex; (d) compound-complex; (e) simple, because a compound verb still makes one clause. [Purdue; Guide to Grammar]
11. **Name the clause pattern.**
    - (a) The circuit overheated.
    - (b) Copper is a good conductor.
    - (c) The solution looks cloudy.
    - (d) The technician replaced the fuse.
    - (e) The manager sent the client a revised quote.
    - (f) The panel declared the design acceptable.
    - (g) The train arrived late.
    - **Model answer:** (a) S-V; (b) S-LV-N; (c) S-LV-Adj; (d) S-V-DO; (e) S-V-IO-DO; (f) S-V-DO-OC; (g) S-V-Adv. [*Writing for Success*; Guide to Grammar]
12. **Fix the comma splice three ways.** "The prototype is lighter, it costs more to produce."
    - **Model answer:**
      1. "The prototype is lighter, but it costs more to produce."
      2. "The prototype is lighter; however, it costs more to produce."
      3. "Although the prototype is lighter, it costs more to produce."

      [Purdue: clauses and run-ons]
13. **Fix the run-on.** "Check the pressure gauge before you start the compressor the relief valve may be stuck."
    - **Model answer:** "Check the pressure gauge before you start the compressor; the relief valve may be stuck." Or: "…before you start the compressor because the relief valve may be stuck." [Purdue; UNC]
14. **Fix the fragments.** "Because the readings were inconsistent. We repeated the experiment. Using a new sensor."
    - **Model answer:** "Because the readings were inconsistent, we repeated the experiment using a new sensor." [UNC on dependent-clause fragments; *Writing for Success* on -ing fragments]
15. **Combine the choppy sentences.** "The drone has four rotors. It weighs 1.2 kg. It can fly for 25 minutes. It carries a thermal camera."
    - **Model answer:** "Weighing only 1.2 kg, the four-rotor drone carries a thermal camera and can stay airborne for 25 minutes." [Purdue: choppy sentences and varied openings]

**D. Paragraphs**

16. **Write a topic sentence for this support.** Aluminium is about one-third as dense as steel; it resists corrosion; it costs more per kilogram; it is highly recyclable.
    - **Model answer:** "Aluminium offers car makers clear advantages over steel, but its higher cost limits how widely it is used."
    - Why it works: it has a topic plus a controlling idea, holds no supporting detail, and previews both the advantages and the drawback. [*Writing for Success*: five characteristics]
17. **Find the sentence that breaks unity.**
    - (1) Solar panels lose efficiency as they get hotter.
    - (2) For most crystalline-silicon panels, output falls by roughly 0.3–0.5 percent for each degree Celsius above 25 °C.
    - (3) The first practical silicon solar cell was demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1954.
    - (4) For this reason, installers leave an air gap behind panels so that they can cool.
    - (5) In hot climates, this heat loss can noticeably reduce a system's annual energy output.

#### Exam-day checklist
This list is my synthesis of the cited rules.
- **SPQ:**
  - Every borrowed idea gets a signal phrase and a citation.
  - Quotations carry page numbers.
  - A paraphrase changes both the words and the structure.
  - A summary is the thesis plus the key points, with no opinion.
- **Sentences:**
  - Each sentence has at least one independent clause.
  - Scan for ", however" between two clauses, and for fragments starting "Because…", "Which…", "Such as…" or with an -ing word.
- **Paragraphs:**
  - The topic sentence has a controlling idea.
  - Each piece of support is explained.
  - Transitions show the relationships.
  - The closing sentence adds no new idea.
- **Quotation marks (US):**
  - , and . go inside; ; and : go outside; ? and ! go where they belong.
  - (Citation) comes before the final period.
  - Nested quotations take 'single' marks.
  - Block quotations: 40+ words in APA, more than 4 lines in MLA.
- **Four C's:**
  - Put the purpose first.
  - Cut padding.
  - Link ideas.
  - Include who, what, when, how much and what action is needed.
