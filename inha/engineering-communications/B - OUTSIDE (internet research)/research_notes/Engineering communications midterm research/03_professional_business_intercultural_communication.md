## 1. The communication process model, channel choice (rich vs lean media), and digital/social media norms

### Takeaway
Communication is "the process of understanding and sharing meaning." A sender encodes a message and sends it through a channel. The receiver decodes it, feedback flows back, and noise can distort any step. The three classic models build on each other. The transmission model is linear and one-way. The interaction model adds feedback and context. The transaction model has both people sending and receiving at the same time, inside social, relational and cultural contexts. Choose the channel by how rich the message needs to be. Sensitive, complex or emotional messages belong in rich channels (in person or video). Routine, factual messages that need a record belong in lean written channels (email, memo).

### Cited Findings

#### Definitions and the eight components
- Communication "is defined as the process of understanding and sharing meaning" (Pearson & Nelson, 2000). The Latin root *communicare* means "to share, or to make common" — [Business Communication for Success §1.2 (Saylor open mirror)](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)
- The eight essential components are source, message, channel, receiver, feedback, environment, context and interference — [Business Communication for Success §1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)
  - **Source:** "imagines, creates, and sends the message." The source first decides on the message, then **encodes** it ("choosing just the right order or the perfect words"), then sends it, then watches the reaction.
  - **Message:** "the stimulus or meaning produced by the source for the receiver or audience." It includes tone, body language, writing style, punctuation, headings and formatting.
  - **Channel:** "the way in which a message or messages travel between source and receiver." Spoken channels include face-to-face talk, phone, voicemail and VoIP. Written channels include letters, memos, email, texts and tweets.
  - **Receiver:** "receives the message from the source, analyzing and interpreting the message in ways both intended and unintended by the source."
  - **Feedback:** "composed of messages the receiver sends back to the source." Also: "As the amount of feedback increases, the accuracy of communication also increases" (Leavitt & Mueller, 1951). The book's example is a manager on a conference call who should ask, "Does this make sense? Do any of you have any questions?"
  - **Environment:** "the atmosphere, physical and psychological, where you send and receive messages."
  - **Context:** "the setting, scene, and expectations of the individuals involved." Also: "In a business meeting, who speaks first? That probably has some relation to the position and role each person has outside the meeting. Context plays a very important role in communication, particularly across cultures."
  - **Interference (noise):** "anything that blocks or changes the source's intended meaning of the message." Also: "Interference, also called noise, can come from any source."
- Psychological noise "is what happens when your thoughts occupy your attention while you are hearing, or reading, a message." In the book's example, a boss emails at 4:45 p.m. asking for three things. The employee, thinking ahead to the reply, sends only two and misses the five-year figures. Hunger and a "hot and stuffy" office are also listed as interference — [Business Communication for Success §1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)
- "Encoding is the process of turning thoughts into communication… Decoding is the process of turning communication into thoughts." Messages travel through "a channel, or a sensory route on which a message travels" — [Communication in the Real World §1.2 (LibreTexts)](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)

#### Three (plus one) models of communication
- **Transmission model:** "a linear, one-way process in which a sender intentionally transmits a message to a receiver (Ellis & McClintock, 1990)." It extends Aristotle's speaker–message–hearer model and was shaped by telegraphy and radio (Shannon & Weaver, 1949). The model is sender-focused, so "responsibility is put on the sender." It is "well suited for describing the act of text messaging" — [Communication in the Real World §1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)
- Noise in the transmission model: "Noise is anything that interferes with a message being sent between participants." **Environmental noise** is "any physical noise present in a communication encounter." **Semantic noise** "occurs in the encoding and decoding process when participants do not understand a symbol," such as an unknown abbreviation or a phone autocorrect error — [Communication in the Real World §1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)
- **Interaction model** (Schramm, 1997): "participants alternate positions as sender and receiver and generate meaning by sending messages and receiving feedback within physical and psychological contexts." Physical context means size, layout, temperature and lighting. Psychological context "includes the mental and emotional factors," such as stress, anxiety and emotions — [Communication in the Real World §1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)
- **Transaction model** (Barnlund, 1970): "communicators generate social realities within social, relational, and cultural contexts." The model holds that "we are simultaneously senders and receivers," so we adapt a message while we are still sending it. Social context means "stated rules or unstated norms." Relational context means "previous interpersonal history and type of relationship." Cultural context means identities such as race, gender, nationality, ethnicity, class and ability — [Communication in the Real World §1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)
- **Constructivist model:** "we focus on the negotiated meaning, or common ground." In the book's example, a waiter in Atlanta asks "what kind?" after a customer orders a "Coke," because locally "Coke" means any soft drink — [Business Communication for Success §1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)

#### Channel choice: rich vs lean
- "Many professionals make the mistake of sending an email when another channel (e.g., a verbal rather than a written one) would be more appropriate." For inappropriate workplace behaviour, "the right thing to do is discuss it in person with all involved," then follow up with an email summarising the agreed action plan — [Communication at Work §2.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/2-3-selecting-appropriate-channels/)
- "Addressing sensitive situations exclusively by email (or, even worse, by text message) tends to intensify conflict. Stripped of the richness of in-person verbal and nonverbal information, email or instant messaging can't possibly address the emotional complexity of a tense situation" — [Communication at Work §2.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/2-3-selecting-appropriate-channels/)
- From the book's table of channel pros and cons — [Communication at Work §2.3, Table 2.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/2-3-selecting-appropriate-channels/):
  - In-person conversation or meeting is "the most information-rich channel combining words and nonverbal messages."
  - Text/IM "can be misinterpreted for tone when used to communicate complex ideas."
  - A microblog "forces conciseness with character limits, making it inappropriate to communicate complex ideas."
  - Video conferencing "enables face-to-face one-on-one or group meetings for people physically distant from each other."
  - Email rule: "Netiquette: be as kind as you should be in person; don't write emails angrily (flaming)."
- Media richness theory comes from Daft & Lengel (1986), "Organizational information requirements, media richness and structural design," *Management Science* 32(5), 554–571 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.32.5.554). It is very widely cited (10,093 citations on Semantic Scholar on 2026-10-19) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4343f12d34fbeefe96054ae909dd05f296f0a9ad). *(The paper is closed-access, so its full text was not read.)*
- Your voice carries enthusiasm better than text: "if you are congratulating one employee on receiving an industry award, your voice as the channel carries your enthusiasm." A general information message to all employees, by contrast, suits email — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)

#### Digital and social media professional norms
- Netiquette means "etiquette, or protocols and norms for communication, on the Internet." Texting "is useful for short exchanges… not useful for long or complicated messages." Also: "Contacting someone too frequently can border on harassment" — [Business Communication for Success §9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html)
- Business email "requires attention to detail, awareness that your e-mail reflects you and your company, and a professional tone so that it may be forwarded to any third party." Also: "Never write or send anything that you wouldn't want read in public or in front of your company president" — [Business Communication for Success §9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html)
- Virginia Shea's 10 Core Rules of Netiquette (1994) — [Albion.com, Core Rules of Netiquette](http://www.albion.com/netiquette/corerules.html):
  1. Remember the human.
  2. Adhere to the same standards of behavior online that you follow in real life.
  3. Know where you are in cyberspace.
  4. Respect other people's time and bandwidth.
  5. Make yourself look good online.
  6. Share expert knowledge.
  7. Help keep flame wars under control.
  8. Respect other people's privacy.
  9. Don't abuse your power.
  10. Be forgiving of other people's mistakes.
- Social media cautions — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html):
  - What you post "may become public and can remain online forever."
  - "Always consider what you access and what you post, and how it represents you and your employer, even if you think others cannot know where you work or who you are."
  - Business computer use "can and often is subject to inspection."

### Inferences

#### Exam-ready model diagram (synthesised from the sources above)
```
 SENDER --encode--> MESSAGE --via CHANNEL--> --decode--> RECEIVER
   ^                                                        |
   |<------------------------ FEEDBACK ----------------------|
 NOISE can strike at any point: environmental/physical, semantic, psychological
 CONTEXT surrounds everything: physical, psychological, social, relational, cultural
```
- A useful exam habit is to diagnose any miscommunication by naming the component that failed. Typical examples:
  - Jargon or an unexplained acronym is **semantic noise**.
  - Skim-reading while thinking about your reply is **psychological noise**.
  - Bad news sent by text is a **channel** error.
  - No confirmation that the message was understood is a **missing feedback loop**.
  - A missing attachment or missing data is an **incomplete message**.
- Many textbooks also call hunger, fatigue or illness *physiological* noise. The open sources above simply list these as examples of interference.

#### Which model fits which situation
| Situation | Best-fitting model | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mass email, text message, recorded announcement | Transmission | One-way; the sender cannot see whether the message was decoded |
| Email thread, Q&A after a presentation | Interaction | Turn-taking with feedback |
| Live design review, negotiation, interview | Transaction | Both sides send and receive at once (words plus nonverbal reactions); the relationship and culture shape meaning |

#### Rich-to-lean channel ladder (synthesis consistent with CAW Table 2.3 and media richness theory)
In-person meeting → video call → phone/VoIP → voicemail → instant message/text → email → memo/letter → report/formal document → mass bulletin/post
- **Use richer channels for:**
  - bad news, criticism, conflict and performance problems;
  - negotiations;
  - ambiguous or novel problems;
  - anything needing quick back-and-forth or emotional reading.
- **Use leaner channels for:**
  - routine information;
  - confirmations;
  - detailed data people must refer back to;
  - anything that needs a record ("talk in person, then confirm in writing").

#### Common mistakes (exam "ineffective vs effective")
- Delivering criticism, layoffs or conflict by email or text.
- Writing vague subject lines.
- Replying when angry.
- Using "Reply All" for a one-person answer.
- Writing in ALL CAPS.
- Using texting abbreviations with external readers.
- Not asking for feedback ("Any questions?") after a complex instruction.
- Posting about your employer or clients on personal social media.

## 2. Audience adaptation: audience analysis, the "you" attitude, positive tone, plain language, bias-free language, conversational but professional style

### Takeaway
Start every message by analysing the audience: who they are, how many, what they already know, what they need and how they will react. Then adapt the message:
- Write from the reader's side (the "you" view), and lead with the reader's benefit.
- Use positive wording.
- Write in plain language: active voice, present tense, short sentences, no hidden verbs or jargon.
- Use bias-free terms.
- Match the level of formality to the audience. External messages are usually more formal than internal ones.

### Cited Findings

#### Audience analysis
- Questions to ask about an audience — [Business Communication for Success §3.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s07-04-getting-to-know-your-audience.html):
  - "How big is the audience?"
  - "What are their backgrounds, gender, age, jobs, education, and/or interests?"
  - "Do they already know about your topic? If so, how much?"
  - "Will other materials be presented or available?"
  - "How much time is allotted for your presentation, or how much space do you have for your written document?"
- The same section describes fairness, or **reciprocity**, toward an audience. It has four parts: mutuality, nonjudgmentalism, honesty and respect (Tyler, 1978) — [Business Communication for Success §3.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s07-04-getting-to-know-your-audience.html)
- Thill & Bovée's 12-item planning checklist includes these items — [Business Communication for Success §5.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s09-02-a-planning-checklist-for-busin.html):
  - Determine the general purpose ("inform, persuade, entertain, facilitate interaction, or motivate").
  - Determine the specific purpose (the desired outcome).
  - Determine audience size, composition, and knowledge of the topic.
  - "Anticipate probable responses."
  - "Select the correct channel."
  - Make sure information is "accurate, ethical, and pertinent."
- Purdue OWL describes a "complex audience" across a document's life cycle — [Purdue OWL, Audience Analysis Overview](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/audience_analysis/index.html):
  - **Development stage:** primary author, secondary authors such as technical or budget experts, and a gatekeeper (your supervisor).
  - **Reading stage:** primary audience (decision maker), secondary audience (technical expert) and **shadow audience** ("others who may read your communication").
  - **Action stage:** stakeholders affected by the decisions.
- Communication at Work's audience-analysis chapter covers audiences of various sizes, secondary and other audiences, your relationship to the audience and their position, their level of knowledge, and demographics (from the table of contents) — [Communication at Work §2.2](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/2-2-analyzing-your-audience-2/)

#### Tone, the "you" attitude and positive emphasis
- "Tone in writing refers to the writer's attitude toward the reader and the subject of the message" (Ober). The target tone is "confident, courteous, and sincere; that uses emphasis and subordination appropriately; that contains nondiscriminatory language; that stresses the 'you' attitude; and that is written at an appropriate level of difficulty" — [Purdue OWL, Tone in Business Writing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/tone_in_business_writing.html)
- OWL examples — [Purdue OWL, Tone in Business Writing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/tone_in_business_writing.html):
  - **Courtesy.** "Not: You didn't read the instructions carefully, thus your system has shut down. But: The system may automatically shut down if any installation errors occur."
  - **Confidence without arrogance.** "Not: You must agree that I am qualified… But: My qualifications in the areas of accounting and customer service meet your job requirements."
  - **Emphasis by position.** Ideas in the first paragraph get the most emphasis and middle paragraphs the least.
- Positive emphasis — [Purdue OWL, Business Letters: Accentuating the Positives](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/accentuating_the_positives.html):
  - Avoid words that "deny" (no, do not, refuse, stop) or carry unpleasant associations (unfortunately, unable to, cannot, mistake, problem).
  - "Stress what something is rather than what it is not."
  - "Open with action rather than apology or explanation."
  - Put good news in high-emphasis positions (beginnings and endings) and bad news in the middle. Give more space to good news.
  - Example: "Negative: We cannot ship in lots of less than 12. Positive: To keep down packaging costs and to help customers save on shipping costs, we ship in lots of 12 or more."
- The "You" view — [Communication at Work §4.5.2](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/4-5-standard-business-style/):
  - "Audience-oriented messages that address the reader directly using the pronouns *you* and *your* have a much greater impact when the message is positive or even neutral."
  - Sender-oriented messages that use I/me/my/we/our "don't register as well with readers."
  - Its before/after: "You forgot to sign and date our contract, so you've got to do that and send it to me a.s.a.p. because I can't process it till I receive it signed." → "For your contract to be processed and services initiated, please sign, date, and return it as soon as possible."
  - The three principles drawn from this example: **use the "You" view, prioritize audience benefits** (lead with the result, then the instruction), and **focus on future positive outcomes**.
  - In negative situations where you must accept responsibility, use "I/we" as needed.

#### Plain language
- The Plain Writing Act of 2010 defines plain writing as "writing that is clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience" — [Public Law 111-274 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ274/html/PLAW-111publ274.htm)
- plainlanguage.gov/guidelines now permanently redirects (HTTP 301) to Digital.gov's plain-language guide — [Digital.gov plain language guide](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language). That guide says:
  - "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience."
  - Plain language does not mean "dumb down." "Don't write for an 8th-grade class if your readers are PhD candidates…"
  - Key practices: have a topic sentence, use active voice, organize ("Summarize lengthy documents up-front"), use tables, use lists — [Digital.gov, Principles of plain language](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles)
- Digital.gov's writing guidance — [Digital.gov, Writing for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing):
  - Content is easier to understand with "shorter words, short sections, active voice, present tense."
  - Active voice: "Not 'It must be done,' but 'You must do it.'"
  - Spotting passive voice: a form of "to be" plus a past participle. Example: "The lake was polluted by the company" → "The company polluted the lake."
  - Hidden verbs (nominalizations) end in -ment, -tion, -sion or -ance, or pair with verbs like achieve, effect, give, have, make, reach, take. Examples: "We conduct an analysis of the data" → "We analyze data." "We are responsible for management of the program" → "We manage the program."
- The original Federal Plain Language Guidelines are archived in GSA's GitHub repository (`_pages/guidelines`) — [GSA/plainlanguage.gov on GitHub](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov). The guideline files are:
  - **audience:** do your research; address the user; address separate audiences separately.
  - **organize:** have a topic sentence; add useful headings; place the main idea before exceptions and conditions; use lists; use transition words; make it easy to follow.
  - **concise:** write short sentences, paragraphs and sections; keep subject, verb and object close together; use positive language.
  - **conversational:** use active voice; use the present tense; use contractions; use examples; use "must" to indicate requirements; "shall and must"; don't use slashes.
  - **words:** avoid hidden verbs, jargon and noun strings; minimize abbreviations and definitions; use simple words and phrases; use the same terms consistently; place words carefully.
  - **design:** highlight important concepts; use tables; consider visuals; minimize cross-references.
  - **test:** paraphrase testing; usability testing; controlled comparative studies.

#### Bias-free language
- APA Style bias-free principle, Guideline 1, "Describe at the appropriate level of specificity" — [APA Style, General Principles for Reducing Bias](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/bias-free-language/general-principles):
  - "using 'man' to refer to all human beings is not as accurate or inclusive as using the terms 'individuals,' 'people,' or 'persons.'"
  - "Be mindful to describe only relevant characteristics."
- Purdue OWL's non-discriminatory language rules — [Purdue OWL, Tone in Business Writing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/tone_in_business_writing.html):
  - Use neutral job titles (Chairman → Chairperson).
  - Avoid demeaning terms ("the girls in the office").
  - Avoid needless gendering ("Executives and their wives" → "spouses").
  - Omit irrelevant group membership ("performed the job well for her age" → "performed the job well").
  - Use a non-sexist salutation ("Dear Gentlemen" → "To Whom it May Concern").
  - Avoid generic "he" ("Each student must provide his own lab jacket" → "Students must provide their own lab jackets").

#### Conversational but professional style
- Three styles: colloquial, casual and formal. "As a general rule, external communications tend to be more formal." Also: "colloquial style is not an excuse for using expressions that are sexist, racist, profane, or otherwise offensive" — [Business Communication for Success §4.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s08-04-style-in-written-communication.html)
- An informal reply to a supervisor still needs "an informative subject line, a salutation ('Hi [supervisor's name]' is typical in e-mails), a word of thanks…, and an indication that you stand ready to help further." Check grammar and spelling before sending — [Business Communication for Success §4.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s08-04-style-in-written-communication.html)
- Communication at Work describes a "formality spectrum" (formal, casual, slang) and has a section on emojis in professional writing. It treats contractions and direct address as acceptable in standard business style. The federal guidelines likewise list "use contractions" under *conversational* — [Communication at Work §4.5](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/4-5-standard-business-style/); [GSA/plainlanguage.gov](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov)

### Inferences

#### Audience-analysis checklist (synthesis for exam answers)
1. **Who** are they? Note the primary, secondary and shadow readers, and the gatekeeper.
2. **How many**, and are they internal or external? This sets the formality.
3. **What do they already know?** This sets how much jargon and background to include.
4. **What do they need or want** ("What's in it for me?")? This tells you which benefit to lead with.
5. **How will they react?** Positive or neutral news calls for a direct opening. Negative or persuasive news calls for a softer opening that builds context first.
6. **Culture and language:** are they English learners or from a high-context culture? Avoid idioms and be explicit about actions.
7. **Channel:** what will they expect, and what needs a record?

#### Seven before/after rewrites (each one labelled with the principle it applies)
1. **"You" attitude plus reader benefit first**
   - *Before:* "We have implemented a new VPN system and we need all staff to install the client by Friday."
   - *After:* "To keep your remote access to the design servers after Friday, please install the new VPN client (about 10 minutes; instructions attached) by 5:00 p.m. on Friday, 23 October."
   - *Why:* this follows CAW's audience-benefit-first rule plus clarity (exact date and time).
2. **Positive tone instead of blame**
   - *Before:* "We cannot process your reimbursement because you failed to attach the receipts."
   - *After:* "As soon as we receive your receipts, we will process your reimbursement, usually within three working days. You can attach them to this email thread."
   - *Why:* this applies OWL positive emphasis and CAW's future-positive outcomes.
3. **Plain language (hidden verbs, passive voice, wordiness)**
   - *Before:* "It has been determined that an evaluation of the feasibility of the implementation of the proposed modifications will be conducted by the committee."
   - *After:* "The committee will evaluate whether we can make the proposed changes."
   - *Why:* this follows Digital.gov's rules on active voice and hidden verbs.
4. **Bias-free language**
   - *Before:* "Each engineer must submit his timesheet; the girls in the front office will check them."
   - *After:* "Engineers must submit their timesheets; the office administrators will check them."
   - *Why:* these are Purdue OWL's non-discriminatory language rules.
5. **Adapting technical content for a non-specialist manager**
   - *Before:* "PLC scan-cycle latency exceeded the 10 ms SLA owing to I/O bus saturation, necessitating a firmware rollback."
   - *After:* "On Tuesday the production line's control system slowed down because too much data was passing through it. We undid Monday's software update, and the line is running normally again. We will test the update offline before reinstalling it."
   - *Why:* audience knowledge level, plain words and the main point first.
6. **Too casual, rewritten as conversational but professional**
   - *Before:* "hey cant make the mtg tmrw can u send the slides thx"
   - *After:* "Hello Professor Lee, I am unable to attend tomorrow's 10:00 meeting because of a lab exam. Could you please share the slides? I will review them before Thursday's class. Thank you, [Name]"
   - *Why:* BCS §4.4 and §9.1 (salutation, no texting abbreviations, specific request, thanks).
7. **Clarity and concreteness**
   - *Before:* "Let's meet sometime next week to discuss the budget change."
   - *After:* "Could we meet to discuss the 10% budget cut? I am free 9:00–10:00 on Tuesday 27 October or 14:00–15:00 on Wednesday 28 October, in Room B-204. Please reply with the time that suits you."
   - *Why:* CAW §4.5.2 says to replace "a change in the budget" with "a 10% budget cut" and to offer specific time options.

#### Common mistakes
- **Writer-centred openings.** Openings like "I am writing to inform you…" or "We are pleased to…" put the writer first; open with the point or benefit instead.
- **Negative or blaming words.** Avoid "you failed," "you forgot," "unfortunately."
- **Passive voice that hides who acts.** For example, "It was decided…"
- **Hidden verbs.** For example, "make a decision" instead of "decide."
- **Jargon and acronyms unexplained** for mixed audiences.
- **Gendered generics.**
- **Emojis or slang with external readers.**
- **Over-formal stiffness.** For example, "Please be advised that…" sounds cold; plain and conversational is still professional.

## 3. The writing process (e.g., Guffey's 3-x-3) and the "C's" of effective communication

### Takeaway
Free, credible open textbooks describe business writing as three phases that loop back on each other:
1. **Pre-writing/planning:** purpose, audience, research, organising.
2. **Drafting.**
3. **Revising/editing/proofreading.**

Professionals spend most of their time planning and revising rather than drafting. Guffey's "3-x-3" is a branded version of the same three phases, but no free, credible summary could be retrieved, so treat its sub-steps as "check against your textbook." Lists of "C's" vary by source: the course uses 4 (clear, concise, coherent, complete), Communication at Work uses 6, and MindTools-style lists use 7. Learn the course's four C's and be able to map them onto the longer lists.

### Cited Findings

#### The writing process
- "The writing process is made up of three main parts": a **pre-writing** stage (reading the prompt, researching, outlining, brainstorming), a **writing or drafting** stage, and a revising stage ("After you've finished writing, it's time to rethink your piece"). Revising "is a lot more than" grammar. "Expert writers often spend most of their composition time on revision." Also: "your writing process won't be a straight line" — [Business Writing For Everyone (KPU), "What is the Writing Process?"](https://kpu.pressbooks.pub/businesswriting/chapter/what-is-the-writing-process/)
- A Toronto consulting firm's study found that "writers in the workplace spend 40% of their time pre-writing or planning, 30% of their time revising and only 20% of their time writing." By contrast, "some studies have found that students only spend 3-5% of their composition time revising." The source does not identify the study further — [Business Writing For Everyone (KPU), "The Writing Process in the Workplace"](https://kpu.pressbooks.pub/businesswriting/chapter/the-writing-process-in-the-workplace/)
- "Preparation for the writing process involves purpose, research and investigation, reading and analyzing, and adaptation." Every document has a general purpose (inform, persuade, entertain, motivate) and "at least one specific purpose, which is the intended outcome" — [Business Communication for Success §5.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s09-02-a-planning-checklist-for-busin.html)
- Communication at Work orders its chapters as a process: purpose → audience → channel (ch. 2), research (ch. 3), drafting (organizational patterns, outlining, sentences, paragraphs, style, design; ch. 4), then revising and proofreading (ch. 5) — [Communication at Work (table of contents)](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/)
- Guffey's textbooks are a standard reference in this area. Open texts cite Guffey's *Essentials of Business Communication* (2007/2008) for email and meeting guidance — [Business Communication for Success §9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html); [Communication at Work §11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/)

#### The C's
- Communication at Work's **"6 Cs of Style"** — [Communication at Work §4.5.2](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/4-5-standard-business-style/):
  - The list is clear, concise, coherent (and complete), correct, courteous and convincing. Six-C writing "fulfills the author's purpose and meets the needs of the audience."
  - **Clarity:** "the words on the page are like a perfectly transparent window to the author's meaning." Use "hard facts, precise values, and concrete, visualizable images." For example, write "a 10% budget cut," not "a change in the budget."
  - **Clarity (dates):** "Always spell out months," because "4/5/27" could be April 5th or May 4th.
  - **Clarity (meetings):** offer specific meeting-time options instead of "Let's meet to discuss."
  - **Clarity (pronouns):** fix vague pronouns, such as an unclear "their."
  - **Conciseness:** "communicate ideas in as few words as possible without compromising clarity." Making "simple things sound complicated" is "a rookie mistake."
  - **Coherence and completeness:** "your writing flows logically and makes sense because it says everything it needs to say to meet your audience's needs." Organizational patterns, outlines, paragraph structure, pronouns and transitions all support it.
  - **Correctness:** spelling, grammar and mechanics matter "at the end of the editing stage." Don't proofread while drafting.
  - **Courtesy:** "writing courteously so that your reader feels respected." Sub-rules are the "You" view, audience benefits first, and future positive outcomes.
  - **Convincing and confident.**
- Clear, specific, complete instructions matter in practice. In the "4:45 p.m. boss email" example, the reply was incomplete because the writer did not read the whole request — [Business Communication for Success §1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)

### Inferences

#### Three-phase writing process: what to do in each phase (synthesis of KPU, BCS §5.2, CAW; consistent with how Guffey's 3-x-3 is usually taught)
| Phase | Key tasks | Exam keywords |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-writing / planning (about 40% of the time) | Define general and specific purpose; analyse the audience (primary/secondary/shadow); anticipate their reaction; choose the channel; gather facts; choose direct vs indirect order; outline | purpose, audience, anticipate, adapt, channel, research, organise |

#### Effective vs ineffective in one example (four C's)

## 4. Collaboration and teams: Tuckman's stages, team roles, managing conflict, productive meetings (agenda, roles, minutes)

### Takeaway
Teams typically move through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning (Tuckman, 1965; Tuckman & Jensen, 1977). The stages are not strictly linear, and teams fall back to earlier stages when members or roles change. Two role frameworks are useful:
- **Task / maintenance / negative roles** (Benne & Sheats tradition), from Communication in the Real World.
- **Belbin's nine team roles.**

Conflict styles sit on two axes, concern for self and concern for others: competing, avoiding, accommodating, compromising, collaborating. These styles carry a Western bias, so read them through culture. Productive meetings need four things:
- a purpose;
- an agenda sent in advance;
- clear roles (chair, recorder/minute-taker, timekeeper or expediter);
- **minutes that record decisions and action items (who, what, by when)**, approved at the next meeting.

### Cited Findings

#### Tuckman's stages of group development
- Original sources: Tuckman, B. W. (1965), "Developmental sequence in small groups," *Psychological Bulletin* 63(6), 384–399 — [DOI 10.1037/h0022100](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022100). Tuckman, B. W. & Jensen, M. A. C. (1977), "Stages of Small-Group Development Revisited," *Group & Organization Studies* 2(4), 419–427 — [DOI 10.1177/105960117700200404](https://doi.org/10.1177/105960117700200404). Bibliographic details were checked via the Crossref/Semantic Scholar metadata APIs.
- Each stage, from [Communication in the Real World §13.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/13%3A_Small_Group_Communication/13.02%3A_Small_Group_Development):
  - **Forming:** members "begin to reduce uncertainty associated with new relationships and/or new tasks through initial interactions." Groups "return to the forming stage as group members come and go."
  - **Storming:** "conflict emerges as people begin to perform their various roles, have their ideas heard, and negotiate where they fit in the group's structure." Also: "conflict can be positive and productive… a group that gets stuck at the storming stage will likely not have much success."
  - **Norming:** "the practices and expectations of the group are solidified, which leads to more stability, productivity, and cohesion." Norms are unstated. Rules are explicit, for example in "employee handbooks, membership policies, or codes of conduct."
  - **Performing:** "group members work relatively smoothly toward the completion of a task." Synergy develops. "Glitches… can lead the group back to previous stages."
  - **Adjourning:** the group dissolves. "It is important that there be some guided and purposeful reflection."
  - **Not linear:** "they are not always experienced in a linear fashion… some groups don't experience all five stages, may experience stages multiple times, or may experience more than one stage at a time."
- "A meeting is a short-term group." Expect a "forming" stage, and "if roles are not clear, there may be a bit of 'storming'" — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)

#### Team roles
- **Task roles** "contribute directly to the group's completion of a task" — [Communication in the Real World §14.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/14%3A_Leadership_Roles_and_Problem_Solving_in_Groups/14.02%3A_Group_Member_Roles). They are:
  - task leader (substantive "idea person" vs procedural guide);
  - **expediter** ("keep the group on track… by managing the agenda");
  - information provider;
  - information seeker;
  - **gatekeeper** ("manages the flow of conversation… so that all group members get to participate");
  - **recorder** ("takes notes on the discussion").
- **Maintenance roles** "create and maintain social cohesion": social-emotional leader, supporter, tension releaser, **harmonizer** (manages conflict) and **interpreter** (bridges cultural differences) — [Communication in the Real World §14.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/14%3A_Leadership_Roles_and_Problem_Solving_in_Groups/14.02%3A_Group_Member_Roles)
- **Negative roles** (Benne & Sheats, 1948; Cragan & Wright) — [Communication in the Real World §14.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/14%3A_Leadership_Roles_and_Problem_Solving_in_Groups/14.02%3A_Group_Member_Roles):
  - **Self-centred:** central negative, **monopolizer**, self-confessor, insecure compliment seeker, joker.
  - **Unproductive:** **blocker**, **withdrawer** (more likely "when groups exceed five members"), aggressor, doormat.
- On recording minutes: "The recorder is the only role that is essentially limited to one person at a time… at formal meetings there is almost always a person who records meeting minutes, which are an overview of what occurred at the meeting." Because recording "is often regarded as a low-status position… it may be desirable to have the role of recorder rotate among members" — [Communication in the Real World §14.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/14%3A_Leadership_Roles_and_Problem_Solving_in_Groups/14.02%3A_Group_Member_Roles)
- **Belbin's nine Team Roles** — [Belbin, The Nine Belbin Team Roles](https://www.belbin.com/about/belbin-team-roles):
  - A Team Role is "one of nine clusters of behavioural attributes identified by his research at Henley as being effective in order to facilitate team progress."
  - **Social roles:** Resource Investigator, Teamworker, Co-ordinator.
  - **Thinking roles:** Plant (creative problem solver), Monitor Evaluator (impartial judge), Specialist.
  - **Task roles:** Shaper (drive), Implementer (turns ideas into action), Completer Finisher (polishes and checks for errors).
  - Each role has "allowable weaknesses." "Most people will have two or three Belbin Team Roles that they are most comfortable with." A team does not need nine people.

#### Conflict management
- There are five styles — [Communication in the Real World §6.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/06%3A_Interpersonal_Communication_Processes/6.02%3A_Conflict_and_Interpersonal_Communication):
  - **Competing:** high concern for self, low for other ("win").
  - **Avoiding:** low/low, though see the culture note below.
  - **Accommodating:** low self, high other. It is "more likely to occur when there are time restraints."
  - **Compromising:** moderate/moderate. It is "not a win/win solution; it is a partial win/lose."
  - **Collaborating:** high/high. It "takes the most work in terms of communication competence" but "ultimately leads to a win/win situation."
- **Culture caveat:** the five styles "were formulated with a Western bias (Oetzel, Garcia, & Ting-Toomey, 2008)." Participants in the US, Germany, China and Japan "all viewed avoiding strategies as demonstrating a concern for the other." Members of collectivistic cultures "were more likely to use the avoiding style" — [Communication in the Real World §6.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/06%3A_Interpersonal_Communication_Processes/6.02%3A_Conflict_and_Interpersonal_Communication)
- **Negotiation steps:**
  - **Prenegotiation:** preview the topic so the other person isn't "blindsided."
  - **Opening:** set a cooperative tone, "establish common ground… and using 'we' language."
  - **Validation:** use back-channel cues such as eye contact, "mmm-hmm" and nodding. It de-escalates conflict.

  Source: [Communication in the Real World §6.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/06%3A_Interpersonal_Communication_Processes/6.02%3A_Conflict_and_Interpersonal_Communication)
- **Feedback without blame: Situation–Behavior–Impact (SBI)** — [Center for Creative Leadership, SBI / SBII (published 19 Aug 2025)](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/):
  - "Clarify the Situation, Describe the specific Behaviors observed, and Explain the Impact that behavior had on you."
  - Behavior example: "You interrupted me while I was telling the team about the monthly budget," instead of "You were rude."
  - Add an inquiry about **intent** to make it two-way (SBII).
  - CCL reports that its research shows "SBI reduces anxiety around giving feedback, as well as the defensiveness of the recipient."

#### Productive meetings
- A meeting is "a group communication in action around a defined agenda, at a set time, for an established duration" — [Business Communication for Success §15.3](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-03-meetings.html)
- **Agenda elements** — [Business Communication for Success §15.3, Table 15.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-03-meetings.html):
  - title header (time, date, location, contacts);
  - participants;
  - subject line or purpose statement;
  - call to order;
  - introductions ("If even one person is new, everyone should briefly introduce themselves");
  - roll call;
  - reading of the minutes;
  - old business ("tabled" items);
  - new business;
  - reports;
  - good of the order;
  - adjournment (including the next meeting date).
- **Strategies** — [Business Communication for Success §15.3](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-03-meetings.html):
  - Send the last minutes and the current agenda "at least one week in advance."
  - Send reminders.
  - "Start and end your meetings on time."
  - Make sure participants know their roles.
  - Follow Robert's Rules of Order "when applicable."
  - "Make sure notes taken at the meeting are legible and can be converted to minutes."
- **Purpose first:** "getting together just to get together is called a party, not a meeting." Avoid meetings held only "because you met at the same time last month" — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)
- **How to build the agenda** — [Communication at Work §11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/):
  - List new business "in order of priority so that the most important issues can be dealt with first."
  - Show "the expected length of time… for each item, with contentious items getting extra time."
  - List any preparatory work.
  - Send the agenda "a week in advance."
- **Minutes** — [Communication at Work §11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/):
  - Minutes are "a permanent record of the meeting's discussion" that "summarizes major points raised, decisions taken, and actions assigned."
  - The recorder "adds bullet-point notes under each agenda item."
  - "Action items should be highlighted since they are the main takeaways (e.g., 'ACTION: Melinda to investigate new software…')."
  - Minutes are emailed to all participants and can be forwarded to non-attendees.
  - They are "usually considered tentative until officially approved at the next meeting, where it's usually the first point on the agenda."
- More on minutes — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html):
  - Minutes "often appear as the agenda with notes in relation to actions taken during the meeting or specific indications of who is responsible for what before the next meeting."
  - "Normally minutes are sent within a week of the meeting if it is a monthly event."
  - Even without official minutes, compare your notes with others' "to make sure you understood what was discussed."
- **Participant checklist** (adapted from Guffey, 2007) — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html); [Communication at Work §11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/):
  - Be prepared.
  - Arrive on time and stay until adjournment.
  - Turn off phones.
  - Follow the turn-taking protocol.
  - Respect time limits.
  - Avoid tangents and side discussions.
  - Stay engaged.
  - Be professional in verbal and nonverbal behaviour.
- **The chair's job:** facilitate, redirect to the agenda, manage conflict and transitions, and close by "summarizing what has been discussed or decided, and what actions the group members are to take" — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)
- **Meeting load:** executives "spend an average of nearly 23 hours a week in [meetings], up from less than 10 hours in the 1960s" — [Perlow, Hadley & Eun, "Stop the Meeting Madness," HBR, July–Aug 2017](https://hbr.org/2017/07/stop-the-meeting-madness)

### Inferences

#### Leader actions by Tuckman stage (synthesis)
| Stage | What you observe | What a leader or member should do |
|---|---|---|
| Forming | Politeness, uncertainty, people looking to the leader | Clarify purpose, goals and roles; introductions; set ground rules |
| Storming | Disagreement over roles, ideas and procedures; cliques | Surface conflict, separate task conflict from personal conflict, use SBI, revisit goals, choose a conflict style deliberately |
| Norming | Agreed procedures, growing cohesion | Write the norms down (team charter); share leadership; watch for groupthink and conformity pressure |
| Performing | Smooth, self-managed work and synergy | Delegate, remove obstacles, set short-term wins; expect regression if membership changes |
| Adjourning | Task complete; group disbands | Debrief lessons learned, recognise contributions, hand over documentation |

#### Choosing a conflict style (synthesis)
- **Collaborate** when the issue is important to both sides and the relationship matters.
- **Compromise** under time pressure or when power is equal.
- **Accommodate** when the issue matters more to the other side, or to build goodwill.
- **Compete** in emergencies or for non-negotiable safety or ethics issues.
- **Avoid** for trivial issues or to let emotions cool. In collectivist settings, avoiding may be read as face-saving consideration, but don't avoid problems that will grow.

**What changed and why (exam talking points):**
- Added a full header: date, time, place, chair, recorder, attendance, apologies.
- Organised the notes under agenda items.
- Removed emotion and side-talk ("!!", "???").
- Wrote decisions in neutral, complete sentences.
- Turned every task into an **ACTION with owner + deadline**.

#### Common mistakes in meetings and minutes
- No agenda, or an agenda with no time limits.
- Inviting people "just in case."
- Writing a verbatim transcript instead of a summary.
- Recording personal opinions or emotional language.
- "It was agreed that something should be done" (passive voice, no owner, no deadline).
- Sending minutes weeks later.
- Not approving the minutes at the next meeting.
- The chair letting a monopolizer dominate.
- Online participants forgotten by the people in the room.

## 5. Interpersonal communication: active listening, nonverbal communication, workplace etiquette (email, phone, video meetings)

### Takeaway
Active listening pairs visible behaviours (eye contact, nodding, back-channel cues) with mental work (focusing, withholding judgment). You confirm understanding by paraphrasing, asking clarifying questions and summarising. Nonverbal communication comes through several channels: kinesics (movement), haptics (touch), vocalics (voice), proxemics (space), chronemics (time), and personal presentation and environment. All of them vary by culture. Etiquette is the practical form of audience awareness. Emails should be brief and well structured, with a clear subject line. Phone calls follow five stages. Video meetings need preparation, camera discipline and muting.

### Cited Findings

#### Active listening
- "Active listening refers to the process of pairing outwardly visible positive listening behaviors with positive cognitive listening practices." Active listeners "ask appropriate clarifying and follow-up questions and paraphrase information to check understanding" — [Communication in the Real World §5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence)
- Active listening shows in both verbal and nonverbal cues (Hargie, 2011) — [Communication in the Real World §5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence):
  - steady eye contact, smiling, slightly raised eyebrows;
  - upright posture, leaning in toward the speaker;
  - nonverbal back-channel cues such as head nods;
  - verbal back-channel cues such as "OK," "mmhum," or "oh";
  - no distracting mannerisms like doodling or fidgeting.

  Taking notes also signals listening.
- MindTools (updated 7 March 2025) gives "five steps to active listening… paying attention; showing that you're listening; providing feedback; deferring judgment; and responding appropriately." It adds two cautions: "active listening can give others the impression that you agree with them even if you don't," and avoid treating it "as a checklist" — [MindTools, Active Listening](https://www.mindtools.com/az4wxv7/active-listening)
- CCL's "6 Active Listening Techniques" are: pay attention, withhold judgment, reflect, clarify, summarize, share. Examples — [Center for Creative Leadership, Active Listening Techniques](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/coaching-others-use-active-listening-skills/):
  - **Reflect** (paraphrase): "So Emma's people skills are great, but accountability is a problem."
  - **Clarify:** "Let me see if I'm understanding. Are you talking about …?"
- Culture shapes listening. In some cultures indirectness is face-saving: "flatly turning down a business offer would be too direct, so a person might reply with a 'maybe' instead of a 'no'" — [Communication in the Real World §5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence)

#### Nonverbal communication
- The types of nonverbal communication are — [Communication in the Real World §4.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/04%3A_Nonverbal_Communication/4.02%3A_Types_of_Nonverbal_Communication):
  - **Kinesics:** "the study of hand, arm, body, and face movements." This covers gestures, head movements and posture, eye contact and facial expressions.
  - **Haptics:** "the study of communication by touch."
  - **Vocalics:** "the study of paralanguage, which includes the vocal qualities that go along with verbal messages, such as pitch, volume, rate, vocal quality, and verbal fillers."
  - **Proxemics:** "the study of how space and distance influence communication." The distances are public (12 feet or more), social (4–12 feet), personal (1.5–4 feet) and intimate.
  - **Chronemics:** "the study of how time affects communication."
  - **Personal presentation and environment.**
- On the phone you lack nonverbal context, so "make sure that your voice accurately communicates your message" — [Business Communication for Success §15.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-02-telephone-voip-communication.html)
- Voice tips — [Business Communication for Success §19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html):
  - A monotone "is often interpreted as being bored."
  - With English learners, "speaking louder (i.e., shouting) does not help."
  - "Avoid figures of speech that do not translate literally."

#### Email etiquette
- Tips for business email — [Business Communication for Success §9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html):
  - Proper salutation ("Dear Ms. X" externally, "Hi Barry" internally).
  - "Subject lines should be clear, brief, and specific."
  - A signature block.
  - Avoid abbreviations.
  - Be brief.
  - "A good e-mail should get to the point and conclude in three small paragraphs or less."
  - Reread before sending.
  - "Never reply in anger."
  - Reply "within twenty-four hours, even if only to say that you will provide the requested information in forty-eight or seventy-two hours."
  - Use "Reply All" sparingly.
  - Avoid all caps ("yelling").
  - Test links.
  - Warn before sending large attachments.
  - Follow up if there is no response in 24 hours.

#### Phone etiquette
- A phone call has five stages (Taylor & Terhune) — [Business Communication for Success §15.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-02-telephone-voip-communication.html):
  1. **Opening:** both parties identify themselves.
  2. **Feedforward:** purpose and tone are set, and the caller asks permission to continue (e.g., "do you have five minutes…?").

  4. **Feedback:** a signal that the business is concluded.

  - Be specific.

  - Silence phones in meetings.

#### Video and audio meetings

  - Lock out interruptions. The book cites the 2017 BBC interview where Prof. Robert Kelly's children walked in.

### Inferences

#### Active-listening phrase bank (synthesis)
- **Paraphrase:** "So what you're saying is that the test data is ready, but the analysis is delayed because of the missing licence — is that right?"
- **Clarify:** "When you say 'soon', do you mean before Friday's review?"
- **Reflect feeling:** "It sounds as if the last-minute change was frustrating."
- **Summarise:** "Let me recap the three issues: …"
- **Defer judgment:** let the speaker finish before you problem-solve. Don't prepare your rebuttal while they talk.

#### Professional email template (copy-ready)
```
Subject: [Action/Info/Request] – [specific topic] – [deadline if any]
   e.g., "Request: approve revised sensor order (+15%) by Thu 22 Oct"

Dear Dr./Mr./Ms. [Surname],            (external or senior)   |   Hi [First name],  (internal peer)

[Opening — 1–2 sentences: the main point or request first, framed for the reader.]
To keep the irrigation-controller demo on schedule for 20 November, I am requesting approval
to order the sensors from an alternative supplier.

[Body — the details the reader needs; bullets for facts, numbers and options.]
- Current supplier: delivery delayed by two weeks (now after the demo date).
- Alternative supplier: delivery in 5 working days; cost +15% (USD 84 extra; quote attached).
- Budget impact: remains within the approved project budget.

[Closing — specific action, deadline, offer of help.]
Could you please reply with your approval by 12:00 on Thursday, 22 October? I am happy to
discuss by phone (+998 XX XXX XX XX) if helpful.

Kind regards,
[Full name]
[Role, Team/Department, Organisation]
[Phone] | [Email]
```
- **Checklist:**
  - specific subject line;
  - correct salutation;
  - main point in the first two lines;
  - one topic per email;
  - three short paragraphs or bullets;
  - concrete dates with the month spelled out;
  - attachments mentioned *and* actually attached;
  - no all caps, emoji or slang for external readers;
  - proofread;
  - choose To/CC deliberately, and use Reply All only when everyone needs it.

#### Phone call script skeleton (five stages)
1. **Opening:** "Good morning, this is [name] from [team]."
2. **Feedforward:** "I'm calling about the sensor order — do you have three minutes?"
3. **Business:** give the facts, then the request.
4. **Feedback:** "So to confirm: you'll send the quote by 15:00 today."
5. **Closing:** "Thank you — goodbye."

#### Common etiquette mistakes
- Vague subject lines such as "Hi" or "Question."
- Burying the request at the end.
- Replying in anger.
- Overusing "Reply All."
- Texting a senior person in abbreviations.
- Taking calls in a shared open space.
- Camera off with no explanation in a camera-on culture.
- Not muting.
- Multitasking visibly.
- Looking at your own image instead of the camera.
- Forgetting time-zone differences when scheduling.

## 6. Intercultural communication: Hall's high- vs low-context cultures, Hofstede's dimensions (with Central Asia notes), monochronic vs polychronic time, ethnocentrism vs cultural relativism, and examples

### Takeaway
Four frameworks cover most exam questions:
- **Hall's context theory:** low-context cultures put meaning in explicit words; high-context cultures put it in nonverbal cues, relationships and silence.
- **Hall's time theory:** monochronic cultures do one thing at a time and treat schedules as firm; polychronic cultures handle many things at once and treat time as fluid.
- **Hofstede's six dimensions:** power distance, individualism, masculinity (now "Motivation towards Achievement and Success"), uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation and indulgence.
- **Ethnocentrism vs cultural relativism:** judging others by your own norms vs understanding them by their own standards.

Use all of these as tendencies, not stereotypes. Individuals vary more than countries do, and Hall's country rankings rest on weak evidence. **Uzbekistan has no official Hofstede scores.** The closest documented neighbour is Kazakhstan (The Culture Factor: PDI 88, IDV 20, MAS 50, UAI 88, LTO 85, IVR 22). Russia and South Korea, both relevant to Tashkent workplaces, have full scores.

### Cited Findings

#### Hall: high- vs low-context communication
- "A **low-context** communication style is one in which much of the meaning generated within an interaction comes from the verbal communication used rather than nonverbal or contextual cues. Conversely, much of the meaning generated by a **high-context** communication style comes from nonverbal and contextual cues (Lustig & Koester, 2006)." The same section adds — [Communication in the Real World §5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence):
  - "US Americans of European descent generally use a low-context communication style, while people in East Asian and Latin American cultures use a high-context communication style."
  - High-context cultures "value silence as a form of communication."
  - "People from low-context cultures may feel frustrated by the ambiguity of speakers from high-context cultures, while speakers from high-context cultures may feel overwhelmed or even insulted by the level of detail used by low-context communicators."
  - Low-context cultures "tend to have a monochronic orientation toward time, while high-context cultures have a polychronic time orientation."
- "The United States is said to be a low-context culture… Japan is said to be a high-context culture, which means they often look for nonverbal clues like tone, silence, or what is not said for meaning." The **cultural-individual dialectic** warns that individuals vary, so a direct-speaking Japanese person is not "from a low-context culture" — [Communication in the Real World §8.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/08%3A_Culture_and_Communication/8.03%3A_Intercultural_Communication)
- **Peer-reviewed critique:** Kittler, Rygl & Mackinnon (2011), "Beyond culture or beyond control? Reviewing the use of Hall's high-/low-context concept," *International Journal of Cross Cultural Management* 11(1), 63–82. In a systematic review they found that "most previous research that utilized HC/LC country classifications is based on seemingly less-than-adequate evidence," and that "mixed and often contradictory findings… show that they are flawed or, at best, very limited" — [DOI 10.1177/1470595811398797 (abstract via Crossref)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470595811398797)

#### Hall: monochronic vs polychronic time
- "Monochronic time-oriented cultures consider one thing at a time, whereas polychronic time-oriented cultures schedule many things at one time, and time is considered in a more fluid sense" (Hall & Hall, 1990) — [Communication at Work §10.4](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/10-4-intercultural-communication/):
  - In monochronic time, "interruptions are to be avoided." Examples given: Canada, the US, Germany, Switzerland.
  - In polychronic cultures such as Greece, Italy, Chile and Saudi Arabia, meetings "may be scheduled at a certain time, but when they actually begin may be another story." An 8 p.m. dinner "might as well say 9pm."
  - A polychronic visitor who arrives at 3:44 for a 3 p.m. meeting in a monochronic culture "will disappoint all who took pains to be there at 2:55pm."
  - Advice: "always ask before the event."
- Business Communication for Success describes the same idea (Hall & Hall, 1987) but misspells the terms as "monochromatic/polychromatic." The correct terms are **monochronic/polychronic** — [Business Communication for Success §18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html)

#### Hofstede's six dimensions
- Official definitions — [Geert Hofstede, 6-D model of national culture](https://geerthofstede.com/culture-geert-hofstede-gert-jan-hofstede/6d-model-of-national-culture/):
  - **Power Distance:** "the extent to which the less powerful members of organizations and institutions (like the family) accept and expect that power is distributed unequally."
  - **Individualism vs Collectivism:** "the extent to which people feel independent, as opposed to being interdependent as members of larger wholes."
  - **Uncertainty Avoidance:** "a society's tolerance for uncertainty and ambiguity."
  - **Long-term Orientation** "deals with change." It contrasts cultures where "preparing for the future is always needed" with those where "the past provides a moral compass."
  - **Indulgence vs Restraint** is "about the good things in life."
  - Each dimension was "derived by comparing many, but not all, countries."
- **Masculinity–femininity** in Hofstede's words: "the assertive pole has been called 'masculine' and the modest, caring pole 'feminine'" (Hofstede, 2009). Example: US business "has a masculine orientation"; Swedish business is "more attuned to modesty… and taking care of society's weaker members" — [Business Communication for Success §18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html). The Culture Factor now labels this dimension "Motivation towards Achievement and Success" — [The Culture Factor, Country comparison tool](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool?countries=kazakhstan%2Crussia%2Csouth+korea%2Cunited+states)
- **Power distance at work:** in high-power-distance cultures "you would probably be much less likely to challenge the decision." When working with them "you may need to take extra care to elicit feedback and involve them in the discussion… Unless you are sensitive to cultural orientation and power distance, you may lose valuable information" — [Business Communication for Success §18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html)
- Other contrasts from Business Communication for Success — [§18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html):
  - **Explicit-rule vs implicit-rule cultures** (Dodd). In explicit-rule cultures "the guidelines and agenda for a meeting are announced prior to the gathering." In implicit-rule cultures "there may be no agenda."
  - **Uncertainty:** "Whereas a U.S. business negotiator might enthusiastically agree to try a new procedure, the Egyptian counterpart would likely refuse to get involved until all the details are worked out."
  - **Direct vs indirect:** "In indirect cultures, such as those in Latin America, business conversations may start with discussions of the weather, or family."
  - **Short- vs long-term orientation:** in a short-term culture a prompt thank-you note the morning after a business dinner is appreciated. Long-term orientation "is often marked by persistence, thrift and frugality, and an order to relationships based on age and status."

#### Ethnocentrism vs cultural relativism
- From [OpenStax, *Introduction to Sociology 3e* §3.1 (CC BY)](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-sociology-3e/pages/3-1-what-is-culture):
  - **Ethnocentrism** means "to evaluate and judge another culture based on one's own cultural norms," a "belief or attitude that one's own culture is better than all others." Example: travellers disgusted by unfamiliar foods while not questioning their own diet.
  - **Cultural relativism** is "the practice of assessing a culture by its own standards rather than viewing it through the lens of one's own culture." Example: anthropologist Ken Barger learning to value the Inuit emphasis on effort and survival skills over winning.
  - **Culture shock** is the "disorientation and frustration" people feel in a new culture. Example: a Chinese exchange student finding constant classroom interruptions rude.
  - **Xenocentrism** is "the belief that another culture is superior to one's own."
- Being ethnocentric "in the sense of being fearful, intolerant, or even just avoidant" toward other cultures "limit[s] your opportunities." Media stereotypes "will similarly lead you into serious error." The advice: "forget the stereotypes and learn about their culture by both researching it and talking to them respectfully." The golden rule is not enough, because "cultural conventions and expectations for how people would like to be treated are relative" — [Communication at Work §10.4](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/10-4-intercultural-communication/)
- "Intercultural communication competence (ICC) is the ability to communicate effectively and appropriately in various cultural contexts." Key components are "motivation, self- and other knowledge, and tolerance for uncertainty" — [Communication in the Real World §8.4](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/08%3A_Culture_and_Communication/8.04%3A_Intercultural_Communication_Competence)
- "Perceptual strategies" for diverse audiences include: become an active perceiver; recognise each person's unique frame of reference; keep an open mind; check your perceptions. Understanding that our perceptions are not the only ones possible "can limit ethnocentrism" — [Business Communication for Success §3.4, Table 3.3](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s07-04-getting-to-know-your-audience.html)
- **Face and conflict:** facework means "the communicative strategies we employ to project, maintain, or repair our face." Collectivists tend toward avoiding styles (Oetzel & Ting-Toomey) — [Communication in the Real World §6.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/06%3A_Interpersonal_Communication_Processes/6.02%3A_Conflict_and_Interpersonal_Communication)

#### Communicating across cultures: practical strategies (synthesis)
1. **Research before you engage** on norms for greetings, titles, time and hierarchy. Then confirm by asking.
2. **Be explicit in writing, gracious in person.** Send agendas and decisions in writing, which helps low-context and monochronic readers. Allow relationship-building time, which helps high-context and polychronic partners.
3. **Avoid idioms, slang, sarcasm and humour that does not translate.** Speak clearly, not louder.
4. **Check understanding without causing loss of face.** Ask open questions ("How would your team approach the test?") rather than "Do you understand?" Confirm decisions by email.
5. **Invite input in ways that fit high power distance.** Use round-robin turns, written or anonymous input, and one-to-one follow-ups.
6. **Give critical feedback privately in collectivist settings.** Frame it around the shared goal, using SBI.
7. **Treat every framework as a hypothesis about a person, not a fact about them.** This is the cultural-individual dialectic.

#### Worked intercultural incidents (analysis model answers)
1. **Silent agreement.**
   - *Incident:* A visiting US project manager asks a Tashkent design team, "Any objections to the new schedule?" Nobody speaks. Two weeks later, engineers privately say the schedule was impossible.
   - *Analysis:* The manager assumed low-context norms, where silence means consent, and low power distance, where juniors challenge seniors openly. The team was using high-power-distance and face-saving norms, where you don't contradict a senior publicly.
   - *Fix:* Ask each lead for a risk estimate in writing. Hold one-to-one check-ins. Use specific questions ("Which task is most at risk?"). Confirm in the minutes.
2. **"That may be difficult."**
   - *Incident:* A supplier from a high-context culture replies, "That may be difficult, but we will study it." The low-context buyer reports the order as accepted.
   - *Analysis:* This is likely a polite "no." In high-context and collectivist communication, refusals are indirect, as in the "maybe instead of no" example from Communication in the Real World §5.3.
   - *Fix:* Ask clarifying, face-saving questions ("What would make it possible?") and confirm commitments in writing.
3. **Clock vs relationship.**
   - *Incident:* A German partner sends a minute-by-minute agenda and starts the call at exactly 10:00. The local team joins at 10:10 after a previous meeting ran over and wants to begin with personal greetings.
   - *Analysis:* Monochronic vs polychronic time, plus explicit-rule vs implicit-rule cultures.
   - *Fix:* Agree norms at the start ("We'll start on time; a 5-minute informal check-in is built in"), and send apologies early.
4. **Ethnocentric judgment.**
   - *Incident:* A new hire says, "Their first meeting was a waste: two hours of tea and family questions, and no business."
   - *Analysis:* This is ethnocentrism, judging by one's own norms. A cultural-relativist reading is that relationship-building is the business in relationship-oriented, high-context cultures. Trust comes before contract.
   - *Fix:* Plan for relationship time and treat it as part of the negotiation.
5. **Uncertainty avoidance.**
   - *Incident:* A high-UAI client refuses a "quick pilot" until full specifications, test standards and liability terms are written.
   - *Analysis:* High uncertainty avoidance (compare BCS's US vs Egyptian negotiator example).
   - *Fix:* Provide detailed documentation, standards and a risk plan up front.

## 7. Business conduct policies: what they contain and why they are written the way they are

### Takeaway
A business-conduct policy (code of conduct or code of ethics) turns a company's implicit norms into **explicit, enforceable written rules**. US securities regulation describes a code of ethics as written standards "reasonably designed to deter wrongdoing." Its five core elements are:
1. honest and ethical conduct, including conflicts of interest;
2. accurate disclosure;
3. legal compliance;
4. prompt internal reporting of violations;
5. accountability.

Codes are written in plain, directive, second-person language ("you must…") with examples and reporting channels. They have to be understood by every employee, applied to grey areas, and enforced fairly. They also serve as evidence that the company communicated its expectations.

### Cited Findings
- US SEC Regulation S-K Item 406 (17 CFR § 229.406) defines a code of ethics as "written standards that are reasonably designed to deter wrongdoing and to promote" — [17 CFR § 229.406 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/229.406):
  - "(1) Honest and ethical conduct, including the ethical handling of actual or apparent conflicts of interest between personal and professional relationships;"
  - "(2) Full, fair, accurate, timely, and understandable disclosure in reports and documents…;"
  - "(3) Compliance with applicable governmental laws, rules and regulations;"
  - "(4) The prompt internal reporting of violations of the code to an appropriate person or persons identified in the code; and"
  - "(5) Accountability for adherence to the code."
- Under the same rule, listed companies must disclose whether they have adopted such a code for their senior financial officers, and "if the registrant has not adopted such a code of ethics, explain why." They must file it, post it online, or provide it free on request. The code "may be a portion of a broader document that addresses additional topics or that applies to more persons" — [17 CFR § 229.406 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/229.406)
- Groups are guided by unstated **norms** and by explicit **rules**. Rules "may be communicated through verbal instructions, employee handbooks, membership policies, or codes of conduct (Hargie, 2011)." Violations of rules "typically result in more explicit punishments than do violations of norms" — [Communication in the Real World §13.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/13%3A_Small_Group_Communication/13.02%3A_Small_Group_Development)
- Cultures differ in whether rules are explicit or implicit. In implicit-rule cultures "rules are often understood and communicated nonverbally," and outsiders "communicate their 'otherness' by not knowing where to stand, when to sit." A written code makes the rules visible to newcomers and to a multicultural workforce — [Business Communication for Success §18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html)
- Plain-language practice for requirements. "Use 'must' to indicate requirements" and "shall and must" are federal plain-language guidelines — [GSA/plainlanguage.gov archive](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov). Active voice makes "clear who should do what… Not 'It must be done,' but 'You must do it'" — [Digital.gov, Writing for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing)
- Professional-conduct topics in a workplace communication textbook include the five qualities of a successful professional, civility, social and emotional intelligence, business etiquette (dining, dress), respectful workplaces and sexual harassment, and ethical persuasion (chapter headings) — [Communication at Work §10.2](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/10-2-professionalism-etiquette-and-ethical-behaviour/)
- Business email should be written so "that it may be forwarded to any third party if needed." Companies may inspect business computer use, which is why many conduct codes include acceptable-use and social-media clauses — [Business Communication for Success §9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html); [§19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)

### Inferences

#### Typical anatomy of a code of conduct (synthesis; compare with the textbook's sample policy)
1. **Purpose and scope.** A leadership letter or values statement; who the code covers (employees, managers, contractors) and where it applies.
2. **Core standards**, each with "what this means" and examples:
   - respect, anti-harassment and anti-discrimination;
   - health and safety;
   - conflicts of interest, gifts and hospitality;
   - anti-bribery;
   - confidentiality and data privacy;
   - protecting company assets, including IT acceptable use;
   - accurate records and reporting;
   - legal compliance;
   - social media and public statements.
3. **Decision aids.** Q&A scenarios and tests such as "Would I be comfortable if this appeared in the news?"
4. **How to raise concerns.** Manager, HR, ethics hotline, anonymous options, and a **non-retaliation** commitment. This links to Item 406 element (4), prompt internal reporting.
5. **Investigation and discipline.** This links to element (5), accountability.
6. **Waivers and amendments, plus contacts.**
7. **Acknowledgment.** Employees sign that they have read and understood it.

#### Why it is written that way (exam explanation)
- **Plain language and second person ("you must…").** The audience includes every employee, at every education level and possibly in translation. Ambiguity defeats enforcement. This is the plain-language rule "write for your audience" applied to a mass, mixed audience.
- **"Must / must not" for rules, "should" for guidance.** This separates mandatory requirements from advice, so discipline can be fair and consistent.
- **Examples and Q&A.** Real situations are grey. Examples turn abstract values into behaviour. This is concreteness, one of the C's.
- **Headings, lists, short sections.** These make the code scannable and easy to look up when a question arises (plain-language "organize" guidelines).
- **Positive, values-based framing plus clear consequences.** Building commitment is more effective than threats alone, but accountability must be explicit.
- **Reporting channels and non-retaliation.** People report problems only when it feels safe. This connects directly to responding to harassment or gaslighting (Section 8).
- **Signed acknowledgment and legal precision.** These document that expectations were communicated, which matters if the company must show it had an effective program (Item 406 disclosure).

#### Common exam question: "Identify the purpose of each clause"
Map each clause to a goal:
- deter wrongdoing;
- protect the company and its stakeholders;
- comply with law;
- enable reporting;
- ensure accountability;
- build trust and reputation.

## 8. Gaslighting at work: definitions, signs, how to respond and document, and how it differs from ordinary disagreement

### Takeaway
Gaslighting is manipulation that makes a person doubt their own perceptions, memory or judgment (APA). It is a **pattern** of behaviour (Cleveland Clinic: "specific patterns of behavior"; Merriam-Webster: "usually over an extended period of time"), often used to gain power. At work it shows up in four common ways:
- denying things that were said or agreed;
- rewriting events;
- blaming the target and calling them "too sensitive";
- erasing the target's contributions.

The best defence is **contemporaneous documentation**: written confirmation of conversations, saved emails, a dated log, trusted allies and formal channels. Ordinary disagreement is different. It is a good-faith difference of opinion that can be resolved with evidence, and it does not attack the other person's sanity or credibility.

### Cited Findings

#### Definitions
- **APA Dictionary of Psychology**, "gaslight" (updated 15 Nov 2023) — [APA Dictionary, "gaslight"](https://dictionary.apa.org/gaslight):
  - Definition: "to manipulate another person into doubting their perceptions, experiences, or understanding of events."
  - The term "once referred to manipulation so extreme as to induce mental illness… but is now used more generally."
  - "It is usually considered a colloquialism, though occasionally it is seen in clinical literature."
  - Origin: "from *Gaslight*, a 1938 stage play and two later film adaptations (1940, 1944) in which a wife is nearly driven to insanity by the deceptions of her husband."
  - Note: the dictionary entry is "gaslight"; "gaslighting" itself is not a headword.
- **Cleveland Clinic** (Chivonna Childs, PhD, psychologist; 14 July 2025): "Gaslighting is when someone uses specific patterns of behavior to get another person to question their sanity and their ability to make decisions." It "can occur in romantic relationships, as well as in the workplace" — [Cleveland Clinic Health Essentials, "What Is Gaslighting?"](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/gaslighting)
- **Psychology Today:** "Gaslighting is an insidious form of manipulation and psychological control. Victims of gaslighting are deliberately and systematically fed false information that leads them to question what they know to be true, often about themselves." On work: "Targets of workplace abuse are made to believe it's their mental weakness that results in maltreatment" — [Psychology Today, Gaslighting (Basics)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/gaslighting)
- **Merriam-Webster** — [Merriam-Webster, "gaslighting"](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/gaslighting):
  - Sense 1: "psychological manipulation of a person usually over an extended period of time that causes the victim to question the validity of their own thoughts, perception of reality, or memories and typically leads to confusion, loss of confidence and self-esteem, uncertainty of one's emotional or mental stability, and a dependency on the perpetrator."
  - Sense 2: "the act or practice of grossly misleading someone especially for one's own advantage."
  - First known use: 1961. It was Merriam-Webster's Word of the Year for 2022.
  - "Unlike lying, which tends to be between individuals, and fraud, which tends to involve organizations, gaslighting applies in both personal and political contexts."
- **Sociological view (peer-reviewed):** Sweet (2019), *American Sociological Review*. Gaslighting is "rooted in social inequalities… and executed in power-laden intimate relationships." Perpetrators "mobilize… structural and institutional inequalities against victims to manipulate their realities." This helps explain why workplace hierarchies (boss–subordinate) enable it — [Sweet, "The Sociology of Gaslighting," DOI 10.1177/0003122419874843](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419874843)
- **Dates of the play.** APA and Psychology Today date the play to 1938 (its premiere). A Psychology Today article citing the printed edition dates it to 1939 (Hamilton, Constable). The two dates are consistent: premiered 1938, published 1939 — [APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/gaslight); [Psychology Today (Knaus, June 2026)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/science-and-sensibility/202606/3-ways-to-stop-a-gaslighter-from-messing-with-your-mind)

#### Signs and tactics
- Behaviours Cleveland Clinic lists (paraphrased list) — [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/gaslighting):
  - accusing you of being overly emotional or sensitive;
  - blaming you for things you didn't do;
  - deflecting;
  - denying fault;
  - disagreeing with your version of events;
  - guilting, humiliating or belittling;
  - isolating you from support;
  - lying, subtly or overtly;
  - questioning your judgment;
  - shaming;
  - shifting blame;
  - trivialising your feelings;
  - **withholding information**.
- Gaslighters "distort your reality to gain power, often using blame, denial, and inversion tactics." Similar patterns "appear in politics, workplaces, families, and business." A "paradoxical defense" is a deliberate narrative flip: "They lie, then accuse you of lying." Research links these behaviours to Dark Tetrad traits (March et al., 2023) — [Psychology Today, Bill Knaus EdD (June 2026)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/science-and-sensibility/202606/3-ways-to-stop-a-gaslighter-from-messing-with-your-mind)
- **Workplace red flag** (opinion blog by abuse-culture researcher Jennifer Fraser, PhD, July 2026): the target's record is erased. "If you are being maltreated and gaslit, instead of your track record providing the evidence for an unfortunate work history, it will instead be erased. It is a red flag of maltreatment and manipulation." #### How to respond and document
- Cleveland Clinic's advice — [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/gaslighting):
  - **Identify and document interactions** with screenshots, messages and written records.
  - Call out unhealthy behaviours as they happen.
  - Practise self-care and grounding.
  - Work with a therapist.
  - Leave the relationship if the gaslighting continues despite being addressed.
- Protective strategies — [Psychology Today (Knaus)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/science-and-sensibility/202606/3-ways-to-stop-a-gaslighter-from-messing-with-your-mind):
  - "Objective observation is the first line of defense."
  - Pause and ask, "Is this about me or about their tactic?"
  - Don't base your self-worth on the gaslighter's approval.
  - Use "trusted allies and professionals."
- Factual, non-judgmental language for raising issues: "Describe the actual, observable behavior. Keep to the facts. Don't insert any opinions or judgements" — [CCL, SBI](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/)
- Codes of conduct typically require "prompt internal reporting of violations… to an appropriate person or persons identified in the code" — [17 CFR § 229.406](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/229.406)

### Inferences

#### Gaslighting vs ordinary disagreement vs honest mistake vs tough feedback (synthesis)
| | Ordinary disagreement | Honest mistake / memory lapse | Tough but fair feedback | Gaslighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | The issue or idea | A fact got wrong | Your work or behaviour | **Your perception, memory or sanity** |
| Evidence | Welcomes evidence; can be resolved by data | Corrected when shown the record | Specific, observable (SBI) | Denies or rewrites documented facts; "That never happened" |
| Pattern | One-off or topic-specific | Occasional | Linked to goals, with a path to improve | **Repeated pattern** over time |
| Effect on you | You may lose the argument but keep your confidence | Resolved | Uncomfortable but clarifying | Confusion, self-doubt, dependence, isolation |
| Power | Often between peers | Anyone | Usually manager to employee, with respect | Often exploits a power gap (Sweet, 2019) |
| Example | "I think option B is cheaper; here's my spreadsheet." | "Sorry, I thought the deadline was Friday; you're right, it's Thursday." | "In today's review your test plan missed the load case; please add it by Monday." | "I never asked you for that report. You always imagine things; maybe you're too stressed for this project." |

#### Workplace response and documentation plan (synthesis)
1. **Check the facts first.** Is it a pattern, or a single misunderstanding? Separate disagreement from manipulation.
2. **Create a written record at the time.** After verbal instructions, send a short confirmation: "To confirm today's 14:00 conversation: you asked me to deliver X by Friday 23 October. Please let me know if I've misunderstood."
3. **Keep a dated incident log, stored privately** and not on a work device you could lose access to (subject to company policy). For each incident, record:
   - date, time and place;
   - who was present (witnesses);
   - the exact words used (quotes);
   - which document or email contradicts them;
   - the impact on your work.
4. **Save the evidence:** emails, meeting minutes, chat messages, version histories, performance reviews. Minutes with named action items are protective evidence.
5. **Respond calmly and factually** using SBI: "In Monday's meeting [S], you said the design was approved [B]; today you said it was never approved. That leaves me unsure which instruction to follow [I]. Can we agree in writing?"
6. **Find allies and support:** a trusted colleague, mentor, HR, an employee-assistance programme or a counsellor.
7. **Escalate through formal channels** under the code of conduct (reporting and non-retaliation). Bring a factual summary and your log, not labels. "This pattern of contradictory instructions…" is more effective than "My boss is a gaslighter."
8. **Protect your wellbeing.** If nothing changes, consider your options, including transfer or exit. Cleveland Clinic advises leaving if the behaviour continues after being addressed.

#### Common mistakes (exam)
- Calling any disagreement "gaslighting." That dilutes the term and can itself be unfair.
- Confronting emotionally without evidence.
- Relying only on verbal agreements.
- Keeping no dated record.
- Discussing the issue in public channels or social media, which may breach the code of conduct.
- Waiting until the situation is severe before documenting.

- *Model answer:*
  - (a) The sender is the manager. The message is the request for three items. The channel is email. The receiver is you. The feedback is your incomplete reply, plus the manager's reaction the next morning.
  - (b) Psychological noise. You were thinking about your reply while reading, so you missed the third item (the BCS §1.2 example). Time pressure at the end of the day added to it.
  - (c) As receiver: read the whole message, list each request, and reply with a checklist ("1 ✓ 2 ✓ 3 — will send by 8:30"). As sender: number the requests and ask for confirmation, which closes the feedback loop.

**2. Choose the channel and justify it.**
- *Task:* Pick a channel for each: (a) telling a team member their contract will not be renewed; (b) announcing a room change; (c) negotiating a 15% price rise with an overseas supplier; (d) a new safety procedure; (e) "Free for lunch?"
- *Model answer:*
  - (a) In person, privately, then a written confirmation. Bad news is sensitive and emotional, and email "tends to intensify conflict" (CAW §2.3).
  - (b) Email or a calendar update. It is routine and lean, and it needs a record.
  - (c) A video call or meeting, then a written summary. Negotiation needs immediate feedback and relationship cues, especially with a high-context partner.
  - (d) A written procedure plus a briefing or training session. It needs both a permanent record and a check that people understood.
  - (e) Text or IM. It is brief, informal and low-stakes.

**3. Name the model.**
- *Task:* Which communication model fits: a recorded all-staff announcement; an email exchange; a live design review?
- *Model answer:*
  - Recorded announcement: **transmission.** It is linear and one-way, with no feedback.
  - Email exchange: **interaction.** People alternate as sender and receiver, with feedback.
  - Live design review: **transaction.** Speakers send and receive verbal and nonverbal messages at the same time, shaped by relational and cultural context.

**4. Rewrite using the "you" attitude and positive tone.**
- *Before:* "We regret to inform you that we cannot accept your application because you failed to include your transcript. We do not review incomplete applications."
- *Model answer:* "Your application is almost complete. As soon as we receive your transcript, we will send your application to the review panel. Please upload it at [link] by Friday, 30 October, so it is considered in this round."
- *Why it works:*
  - It leads with the reader's benefit.
  - It focuses on a future positive outcome.
  - It removes the words "regret," "cannot," "failed" and "do not."
  - It sets a concrete deadline (OWL; CAW §4.5.2).

**5. Plain-language rewrite.**
- *Before:* "Utilization of the new form is required by all personnel for the submission of travel reimbursement requests, and failure to comply with this requirement may result in a delay in the processing of said requests."
- *Model answer:* "You must use the new form to request travel reimbursement. If you use the old form, your payment may be delayed."
- *Why it works:* It uses active voice, "you," and "must" for a requirement. It removes hidden verbs ("utilization," "submission," "processing") and splits one long sentence into two short ones (Digital.gov; federal guidelines).

**6. Bias-free rewrite.**
- *Before:* "Every manager should make sure his team attends; elderly staff and the girls in accounting may need extra help with the new software."
- *Model answer:* "Managers should make sure their teams attend. Anyone who would like extra help with the new software can book a one-to-one session."
- *Why it works:*
  - It drops the generic "his."
  - It removes irrelevant age and gender labels (APA Guideline 1: describe only relevant characteristics).
  - It drops the demeaning word "girls."
  - It offers help to everyone (OWL).

**7. Audience analysis.**
- *Task:* You must recommend pedestrian-bridge repairs to the city council. Identify the audiences and list five analysis questions.
- *Model answer:*
  - **Audiences:**
    - Primary: council members, who are the decision makers and mostly not engineers.
    - Secondary: city engineers, who will check the technical content.
    - Shadow: residents, the media and future auditors.
    - Gatekeeper: your supervisor.
    - Stakeholders: pedestrians and nearby businesses.
  - **Questions:**
    1. What do they already know?
    2. What decision must they make, and by when?
    3. What will they care about most (safety, cost, disruption)?
    4. How will they react (budget resistance?)
    5. What length, format and visuals do they expect?
  - **Adaptation:** a one-page executive summary first, in plain language, with cost and risk options in a table and the technical detail in an appendix.

**8. Diagnose with the four C's, then rewrite.**
- *Before:* "Regarding the thing we discussed, I think we should maybe change some stuff soon, as per the issues, because of reasons mentioned before, ASAP."
- *Diagnosis:*
  - **Not clear:** "thing" and "stuff" are vague.
  - **Not complete:** no what, who or when.
  - **Not coherent:** no logic linking the problem to the action.
  - **Not concise:** filler such as "as per" and "because of reasons."
  - **Discourteous:** "ASAP" sounds demanding.
- *Model answer:* "Following Monday's design review, I recommend replacing the 6 mm bracket bolts with 8 mm bolts before the 2 November load test, because the review found stresses above the 6 mm bolts' rating. Could you approve the change by Wednesday, 28 October?"

**9. Writing process.**
- *Task:* You have 100 minutes to write a proposal email to a client. Plan your time.
- *Model answer:*
  - About 40 minutes **planning:** purpose, audience, the client's likely objections, gathering costs, outline, main point first.
  - About 20 minutes **drafting:** write quickly from the outline.
  - About 30 minutes **revising:** check the C's, "you" view, tone, numbers and attachments, then proofread.
  - About 10 minutes **buffer:** a colleague reads it.
  - Justification: workplace writers spend about 40% planning, 30% revising and 20% writing (KPU). The phases loop; they are not strictly linear.
  - *(If asked about Guffey's 3-x-3, name the three phases with their sub-steps as given in your textbook.)*

**10. Tuckman.**
- *Scenario:* Two weeks into the capstone, members argue about who leads the software work. One says the schedule is unrealistic, and meetings run over.
- *Model answer:* This is **storming**: conflict over roles, ideas and procedures (CRW §13.2). The leader should:
  - bring the conflict into the open and depersonalise it;
  - revisit goals and agree roles and decision rules in a written team charter, which moves the team toward norming;
  - use SBI for behaviour issues;
  - collaborate on a revised schedule.

  Storming is normal and can be productive. Getting stuck in it is the danger.

**11. Roles in meetings.**
- *Scenario:* Rustam talks 60% of the time and interrupts. Malika never speaks.
- *Model answer:* Rustam is a **monopolizer** (a self-centred role). Malika is showing **withdrawer** behaviour (an unproductive role; CRW §14.2). The chair or **gatekeeper** should:
  - use round-robin turns with time limits;
  - ask Malika a prepared question, or collect written input before the meeting;
  - give Rustam private SBI feedback.

  Belbin lens: Rustam's drive may be Shaper energy, so give him a task to own. Malika may be a Monitor Evaluator or Specialist, so ask for her analysis.

**12. Choosing a conflict style.**
- *Scenario:* Two engineers disagree on a test protocol three days before a safety certification, and the relationship matters.
- *Model answer:*
  - **Collaborate.** There is high concern for both sides. Use data and the shared goal (passing certification), and combine the best of both protocols.
  - If time runs out, **compromise on non-safety details** but **do not compromise on safety-critical requirements**. Competing is justified there.
  - For trivial issues, accommodate or avoid.
  - **Cultural caveat:** in collectivist settings avoiding can signal concern for the other person (Oetzel et al.). Hold the discussion privately so nobody loses face.

**13. Minutes from notes.**
- *Notes:* "Lab safety cttee 20/10 14:00 rm 112. Present: Dr Kim (chair), Aziz, Lola, Nodir. Absent: Bek. prev mins ok. fire extinguisher lab 3 expired!! Nodir call facilities by Fri. new students need induction – Lola 1-page checklist for next mtg. goggles: agreed buy 20 more, Aziz to order, budget ok. next mtg 3 Nov 14:00. end 14:40"
- *Model answer:*
```
LABORATORY SAFETY COMMITTEE — MINUTES
Date: Tuesday, 20 October 2026   Time: 14:00–14:40   Location: Room 112
Chair: Dr Kim   Recorder: [name]
Present: Dr Kim, Aziz, Lola, Nodir   Absent: Bek

   ACTION: Nodir to contact Facilities to replace it by Friday, 23 October.

   ACTION: Lola to draft a one-page safety induction checklist for review at the next meeting.
```

  The "!!" was removed. Every task has an owner and a deadline. Decisions are marked separately from actions.

**14. SBI feedback.**
- *Before:* "You're always late and you obviously don't care about this team."
- *Model answer:* "In this morning's 9:00 stand-up [**S**], you joined at 9:20 [**B**]. We had to repeat the status update, and the meeting ran over, so two people missed their next call [**I**]. Is something making the 9:00 start difficult?" The last question asks about intent (CCL SBI/SBII).
- *Why it works:* it avoids "always" and does not judge motives.

**15. Active listening.**
- *Colleague:* "I'm fed up. I spent all weekend fixing the simulation, and in the meeting nobody even mentioned it."
- *Model answer:*
  - **Reflect and paraphrase:** "That sounds really frustrating. You gave up your weekend to fix the simulation, and it wasn't recognised."
  - **Clarify:** "Is it mainly the lack of credit, or are you worried the fix won't be used?"
  - **Respond later:** "Would it help if we raised it with the team lead together?"
  - **Nonverbal:** eye contact, nodding, phone away.
  - **Avoid:** "Don't worry about it" (dismissive), jumping to your own story, or giving advice before you understand (MindTools; CCL).

**16. Intercultural incident analysis.**
- *Scenario:* A US manager emails a three-page specification to a Tashkent team and writes, "Push back if anything is unrealistic." No one replies, and the deadline is missed.
- *Model answer:*
  - **Hall:** a low-context, explicit email treated silence as agreement. In high-context communication, silence or a vague "OK" may hide concerns.
  - **Hofstede:** if the team works with higher power distance and collectivist face concerns, publicly "pushing back" against a senior manager is uncomfortable. Regional reference points: Kazakhstan PDI 88 and Russia PDI 93 vs the USA's 40. Uzbekistan has no official score, so treat this as a hypothesis.
  - **Channel:** email was too lean for a kickoff.
  - **Fixes:**
    - Hold a video kickoff and spend time building the relationship.
    - Ask named leads specific questions ("Which task is highest risk?").
    - Hold one-to-one check-ins.
    - Allow written or anonymous risk input.
    - Confirm commitments in minutes.
  - Avoid stereotyping. Check with individuals.

**17. Ethnocentrism or not?**
- *Task:* Classify each statement:
  - (a) "Their long lunches prove they're lazy."
  - (b) "In their context, lunches are where relationships and decisions happen. Let's schedule around them."
  - (c) "Their culture is superior to ours in every way."
  - (d) "I feel lost and irritated that nothing here starts on time."
- *Model answer:* (a) ethnocentrism; (b) cultural relativism; (c) xenocentrism; (d) culture shock (OpenStax §3.1).

**18. Interpret a code-of-conduct clause.**
- *Clause:* "You must report any suspected bribery to your manager or the Ethics Hotline. We will not tolerate retaliation against anyone who reports in good faith."
- *Model answer:* The clause serves Item 406 elements (4) prompt internal reporting, (1) honest and ethical conduct, (3) legal compliance (anti-bribery law) and (5) accountability. It is written this way for four reasons:
  - "You must" makes it a plain, enforceable requirement.
  - It names specific channels, so people know *how* to report.
  - It promises non-retaliation, which makes reporting feel safe.
  - "Suspected" and "good faith" set the threshold for reporting and protect honest reporters.

**19. Gaslighting or disagreement?**
- *Task:* Classify each case, then give documentation steps for case (b).
  - (a) A manager says: "I disagree with your estimate. Our last three projects took 20% longer; here's the data."
  - (b) A manager repeatedly denies instructions that are in your emails, tells you "you're imagining things," and tells others you're "unstable."
  - (c) A colleague misremembers a date and corrects it when shown the calendar.
  - (d) A supervisor says: "Your calculations had two unit errors in today's review. Please fix them by Monday."
- *Model answer:*
  - (a) Ordinary, evidence-based disagreement.
  - (b) Gaslighting. It is a repeated pattern of denying documented reality, attacking your perception and sanity, and isolating you (APA; Cleveland Clinic; Merriam-Webster).
  - (c) An honest mistake.
  - (d) Tough but fair feedback.
- *Steps for (b):*
  1. Confirm instructions by email after every conversation.
  2. Keep a private, dated log with exact quotes, witnesses and links to evidence.
  3. Save emails and minutes.
  4. Respond factually using SBI.
  5. Seek allies and HR under the code's reporting and non-retaliation provisions.
  6. Look after your wellbeing, and seek counselling if needed.

**20. Apply Hofstede scores.**
- *Task:* Using The Culture Factor scores (Kazakhstan PDI 88, IDV 20, UAI 88; USA IDV 60, UAI 46), predict two differences in how a project kickoff might run and how you would adapt. Then explain why you should not quote "Uzbekistan's Hofstede score."
- *Model answer:*
  - **High PDI:** participants expect seniors to decide, and juniors may not volunteer objections. Have the senior sponsor open the meeting, and collect input in structured ways.
  - **Strong collectivism:** harmony and face matter. Recognise the team, and give critical feedback privately.
  - **High UAI:** people want a detailed plan, rules and risk controls. Circulate a detailed agenda and specification in advance, and define roles clearly.
  - US participants tend to show more individual initiative, comfort with ambiguity and open debate.
  - **Uzbekistan:** it has no row in Hofstede's official 2015 dataset and is not in The Culture Factor's country list. Use neighbouring countries only as hypotheses, and remember that "differences among the values of individuals in one country tend to be bigger than the value differences between countries."

#### One-page recap to memorise
- **Process:** sender → encode → message → channel → decode → receiver → feedback, plus noise (environmental/physical, semantic, psychological) and context. Models: transmission → interaction → transaction.
- **Channel choice:** rich channels for sensitive, complex or negotiable messages; lean channels for routine messages and records. Talk in person, then confirm in writing.
- **Audience:** primary, secondary, shadow, gatekeeper and stakeholders; their knowledge, needs and likely reaction.
- **Adaptation:** "you" view, benefit first, positive tone, plain language (active voice, present tense, short sentences, no hidden verbs or jargon), bias-free, appropriate formality.
- **Process:** plan (about 40%) → draft (about 20%) → revise (about 30%). Course C's: clear, concise, coherent, complete.
- **Teams:** forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning (not linear); task, maintenance and negative roles; Belbin's 9 roles; five conflict styles (with a cultural caveat); SBI feedback.
- **Meetings:** purpose → agenda a week ahead → chair, recorder, timekeeper → minutes = decisions + ACTIONS (owner, deadline) → approved at the next meeting.
- **Listening:** attend, show you're listening, reflect or paraphrase, clarify, summarise, defer judgment, respond.
- **Nonverbal:** kinesics, haptics, vocalics, proxemics, chronemics, personal presentation and environment.
- **Culture:** high vs low context; monochronic vs polychronic; Hofstede PDI, IDV, MAS/Motivation, UAI, LTO, IVR; ethnocentrism vs relativism. No official Uzbekistan scores; Kazakhstan is the nearest proxy.
- **Codes:** written standards to deter wrongdoing (honesty and conflicts of interest, accurate disclosure, legal compliance, internal reporting, accountability), written as plain "you must" rules with examples and non-retaliation.
- **Gaslighting:** a pattern of manipulation that makes you doubt your perception. Document in writing, respond factually, escalate through formal channels. Disagreement alone is not gaslighting.
