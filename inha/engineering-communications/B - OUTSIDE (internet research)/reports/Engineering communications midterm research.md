# Lead with the point, cite everything else

A top grade on the NTS3020 midterm depends less on memorising fifteen separate topics than on four habits. The outside authorities consulted (Purdue OWL, Harvard's Guide to Using Sources, UNC's Writing Center, The News Manual, Digital.gov and the open business-communication textbooks) all treat these habits as the core of competent writing, and the exam's three blocks test them in different forms. **Put the most important point first**: a topic sentence with a controlling idea, a summary that opens with the author's thesis, a news lead built on the single most newsworthy fact, an email whose request sits in its opening lines, and minutes headed by decisions and owned actions. **Attribute everything that is not yours or not undisputed fact, and paraphrase by changing structure as well as words**, because Harvard classes a synonym-swapped paraphrase as plagiarism "even if you do provide a citation" ([Harvard Guide to Using Sources](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)). **Earn the course's four C's (clear, concise, coherent, complete) by deleting padding and adding specifics** such as numbers, units, names, dates, owners and deadlines. **Answer every concept question by naming the framework, defining it and tying it to a fact in the scenario**, whether that framework is a news value, a defamation element, a Tuckman stage or a Hofstede dimension. The stakes leave no way to recover lost marks: the midterm is worth 35% and falls in week 8 (24–30 October 2026), in-class assignments cannot be made up, and late work scores zero. There are five days left before the window opens on Saturday 24 October. The best use of them is one timed, exam-style piece of writing per day, checked against published rules and answer keys, using the templates, model answers and plan below. Outside research cannot reveal Prof. Khankeldiyev's rubric, the question formats or the exact terms in Langan & Albright's *Exploring Writing*, so wherever this report's vocabulary differs from the class materials, use the class wording.

## A cited synonym swap is still plagiarism

The SPQ (summarize, paraphrase, quote) assignment and the plagiarism material reward one distinction above all others. An acceptable paraphrase changes the source's **words and its sentence structure**, keeps its meaning, puts any distinctive phrase in quotation marks and carries a citation. UNC's Writing Center says plainly what does not count. Changing "a word or two," rearranging a sentence "while maintaining the original words," or swapping in synonyms is "plagiarizing, not paraphrasing," and a paraphrase still needs a citation ([UNC Writing Center](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)).

Harvard supplies the vocabulary a grader uses: **verbatim plagiarism** (copying without quotation marks), **mosaic plagiarism** (bits of one or more sources with a few words changed), **inadequate paraphrase** (language so close to the original that it is plagiarism "even if you do provide a citation"), **uncited paraphrase** and **uncited quotation** ([Harvard Guide to Using Sources](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)).

Two Harvard rules catch students who believe they are safe. A website "that has no clear authorship" must still be cited, and ideas a professor offers in lecture should be cited too ([Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)). Not meaning to plagiarize is no defence either. UNC warns that a student who omits credit through ignorance faces "the same consequences as if you intentionally stole someone else's work." Its rule, "When in doubt, give a citation," is the safest default in an exam ([UNC](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/plagiarism/)). The exception is common knowledge, which Purdue describes as information found undocumented "in at least five credible sources" ([Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/avoiding_plagiarism/common-knowledge_attribution.html)).

The grid below sorts every case a grader will meet. The verdicts are Harvard's. "Patchwriting" is the common informal name for the second row.

| What the student wrote | Words | Structure | Citation | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copied text without quotation marks | Source's | Source's | With or without | Plagiarism (verbatim) |
| Synonym swaps or reshuffled phrases | Mostly source's | Source's | With or without | Plagiarism (inadequate paraphrase or mosaic) |
| Genuinely new wording and structure | Yours | Yours | None | Plagiarism (uncited paraphrase) |
| New wording and structure, unique phrases quoted | Yours | Yours | Yes | **Acceptable paraphrase** |
| Exact words in quotation marks | Source's | Source's | None | Plagiarism (uncited quotation) |
| Exact words in quotation marks | Source's | Source's | Yes, with page | **Acceptable quotation** |

### Cover the source, rebuild the sentence, then check

The reliable method is to **write with the original out of sight**. Purdue's six steps are to reread the passage until you grasp its full meaning, "Set the original aside" and write your paraphrase, note how you plan to use it, check your version against the original for accuracy, put quotation marks around "any unique term or phraseology you have borrowed exactly from the source," and record the source and page ([Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)).

Harvard's final check is that you have not reused "the same words or sentence structure" ([Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)). In practice, rebuilding the structure means starting from a different element (the effect before the cause), changing the sentence type, or switching verbs and nouns, all while keeping the author's hedges: Harvard's model paraphrase of Milgram turns "perhaps" into "may have" ([Harvard: Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)).

The authorities disagree on length. Harvard says a paraphrase is "about the same length" as the original, and Purdue says it is "usually shorter." Both agree it keeps far more detail than a summary ([Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0); [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/index.html)). Purdue's worked example shows the comparison a grader makes ([Purdue OWL: Paraphrase](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/paraphrasing.html)):

> **Original (Lester):** "Students frequently overuse direct quotation in taking notes, and as a result they overuse quotations in the final [research] paper. Probably only about 10% of your final manuscript should appear as directly quoted matter. Therefore, you should strive to limit the amount of exact transcribing of source materials while taking notes."
>
> **Plagiarized:** "Students often use too many direct quotations when they take notes, resulting in too many of them in the final research paper. In fact, probably only about 10% of the final copy should consist of directly quoted material. So it is important to limit the amount of source material copied while taking notes."
>
> **Legitimate paraphrase:** "In research papers, students often quote excessively, failing to keep quoted material down to a desirable level. Since the problem usually originates during note taking, it is essential to minimize the material recorded verbatim (Lester 46-47)."

The plagiarized version has no citation, and it also follows the original sentence for sentence. Harvard's Sandel example shows the same fault surviving a citation. The student swapped "deeper danger" for "bigger problem" and "aspiration" for "desire," but left "hyperagency," "Promethean" and "drive to mastery" unquoted ([Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)). The engineering pair below is constructed, with a fictional author, and applies the same test to technical prose.

| | Text | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Original (Rahimova, 2024, p. 12) | "Because concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension, engineers embed steel reinforcing bars in the regions of a beam that will be stretched under load. The steel carries the tensile forces, while the concrete resists compression and protects the steel from corrosion." | — |
| Patchwritten | "Since concrete is strong when compressed but weak when pulled, engineers put steel reinforcing bars in the areas of a beam that will be stretched by loads. The steel takes the tensile forces, while the concrete resists compression and shields the steel from rust (Rahimova, 2024)." | **Fails**: same two-sentence skeleton, one-for-one swaps, "resists compression" copied |
| Acceptable | "Rahimova (2024) explains that a reinforced-concrete beam combines two materials with opposite strengths: concrete handles the squeezing forces, while steel bars placed where the beam lengthens under load take the pulling forces, and the concrete around them also keeps the steel from corroding (p. 12)." | **Passes**: new structure and order, technical terms kept, narrative citation with page |

### Quote rarely, frame every quotation and cite to the page

**Quote only when the exact words matter.** APA gives three reasons to quote: reproducing "an exact definition," quoting an author who "has said something memorably or succinctly," and responding to "exact wording" ([APA Style: Quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations)). Harvard observes that "Social scientists and STEM scholars rarely quote in their writing, paraphrasing their sources instead" ([Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/what-constitutes-plagiarism-0)).

A quotation needs your own words on both sides. UNC's four steps are context, attribution, an explanation of significance, and a citation ([UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)). Harvard's shorthand is "Don't float the quotation!" ([Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)).

The signal verb is part of the meaning. "Sandel argues" signals a claim, while "Sandel notes" signals neutral reporting ([Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)). Last's engineering textbook bans "says" and "writes" as too vague and "talks about" as too informal ([Last, *Technical Writing Essentials*](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/)). APA prefers past-tense signal verbs ("Doe asserted"), while MLA uses the present tense ("argues") ([Purdue OWL: Signal Phrases](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/using_research/quoting_paraphrasing_and_summarizing/signal_and_lead_in_phrases.html)).

The citation details that cost marks are short enough to memorise ([APA Style: Author–Date](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/basic-principles/author-date); [Purdue OWL: APA In-Text](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/in_text_citations_the_basics.html); [Purdue OWL: MLA In-Text](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_in_text_citations_the_basics.html)).

| Point | APA 7 | MLA 9 |
|---|---|---|
| Basic form | (Luna, 2020) or Luna (2020) | (Wordsworth 263): no comma, no "p." |
| Two authors | (Salas & D'Agostino, 2020); write "and" in a narrative citation | (Best and Marcus 9) |
| Three or more authors | (Martin et al., 2020), from the first citation onward | (Franck et al. 327) |
| Quotation | Page always: (Ervin et al., 2018, p. 470) | Page always |
| Paraphrase | Page optional; encouraged for long works | Page if the source is paginated |
| End punctuation | After the closing parenthesis | After the parenthetical citation |

### Summaries lead with the thesis and never judge

Harvard's summary rules are to identify the author and the source, "Present the source's central claim clearly," avoid summarizing "each point in the same order as the original source," and use your own words ([Harvard: SPQ](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)).

Last adds that an academic summary "does NOT analyze, evaluate, or critique," and that padding it with quotations "will lower your mark" ([Last, Appendix B](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/)). UNC flags lazy openers such as "[This essay] is about…" ([UNC: Summary](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/summary-using-it-wisely/)).

One real conflict is worth asking the professor about. Last's revision checklist asks "Does it follow the order of the original text?", while Harvard says not to follow that order ([Last](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/appendixb-writingsummary/); [Harvard](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting-0)). Ordering by importance is the safer default because it satisfies Harvard and still lets you state the thesis first.

The template below combines the three sources. The worked example uses a constructed passage with a fictional author.

```
S1  In "[Title]," [Author] ([Year]) argues that [thesis, in your own words].
S2  [He/She/They] [identifies / contends / shows] that [key points, grouped by importance].
S3  [Optional: the evidence or mechanism that carries the argument.]
S4  [Author] concludes / recommends that [conclusion]. (p. x where required)
    Check: no opinion, no "This article is about", at most one short quoted phrase.
```

> **Passage (Aliyeva, 2025, "Why Small Bridges Fail"):** "Most bridge failures do not involve dramatic collapses of famous structures. Instead, they happen on small, ageing bridges that receive little attention. Three causes recur. First, scour—the erosion of riverbed material around foundations during floods—can undermine supports without any visible warning above the water. Second, corrosion of steel slowly reduces load capacity, especially where drainage is poor. Third, many older bridges now carry heavier vehicles than they were designed for. Because inspection budgets favour large, high-profile bridges, local authorities should adopt low-cost sensors and schedule inspections according to risk rather than reputation."
>
> **Model summary:** In "Why Small Bridges Fail," Aliyeva (2025) argues that bridge failures mostly occur on minor, neglected structures rather than on well-known ones. She identifies three repeated causes: floods washing away the soil that supports foundations, gradual rusting that lowers how much weight a bridge can bear, and modern vehicles that exceed original design loads. Since funding tends to go to prominent bridges, she urges local governments to install inexpensive monitoring devices and to prioritise inspections by risk.

### American quotation marks follow five placement rules

US practice, which Purdue, APA, MLA and Harvard all teach, rests on five rules. **Periods and commas go inside** the closing mark, "except when a parenthetical reference follows"; **colons and semicolons go outside**; and **question marks and exclamation points go inside only when they belong to the quoted words** ([Purdue OWL: Quotation Mark Rules](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/more_quotation_mark_rules.html)). **Single marks enclose a quotation inside a quotation** ([Purdue OWL: Extended Rules](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/extended_rules_for_quotation_marks.html)), and **short works** (articles, chapters, episodes, songs) take quotation marks while long works take italics ([Purdue OWL: Titles](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_with_fiction.html)).

Long quotations become indented **block quotations with no quotation marks**. In APA the threshold is 40 words or more, and the citation goes after the final period with no period after it ([APA Style: Quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations)). In MLA it is more than four lines of prose ([Purdue OWL: MLA Quotations](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_formatting_quotations.html)).

Three smaller rules also cost marks: capitalise a quoted full sentence but not a quoted fragment, never capitalise the second half of an interrupted quotation ([Purdue OWL: Quotation Marks](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/index.html)), and use no comma after "that" before a quotation ([UNC: Quotations](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/quotations/)).

British usage is not uniform. Cambridge says single marks are "becoming more common," while the UK government's house style uses double marks for direct quotations ([Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/punctuation); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/style-guide/a-to-z-of-gov-uk-style)). Write US style unless told otherwise, and never mix the two systems in one answer. The drills below are constructed. Purdue's 11-item exercise adds more, with an answer key ([Purdue OWL: Exercise](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_exercise_and_answers.html)).

| Rule tested | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|
| Comma inside, before the tag | "The bridge will reopen on Monday" the engineer announced. | "The bridge will reopen on Monday," the engineer announced. |
| Interrupted quotation | "If the load exceeds the rating" the manual warns "The fuse will blow." | "If the load exceeds the rating," the manual warns, "the fuse will blow." |
| Your own question | Did the professor really call our design "elegant?" | Did the professor really call our design "elegant"? |
| The speaker's question | The client asked, "When will the prototype be ready"? | The client asked, "When will the prototype be ready?" |
| Semicolon outside | …"a predictable result of poor maintenance;" the city disagreed. | …"a predictable result of poor maintenance"; the city disagreed. |
| Colon outside | …what she called "three critical gaps:" training, documentation, and testing. | …what she called "three critical gaps": training, documentation, and testing. |
| Period after the citation (APA) | Chen (2021) argued that the standard is "outdated." (p. 8) | Chen (2021) argued that the standard is "outdated" (p. 8). |
| Period after the citation (MLA) | …the test "failed to account for humidity". (45) | …the test "failed to account for humidity" (45). |
| Nested quotation | "The operator told me the valve was "already leaking" before the shift." | "The operator told me the valve was 'already leaking' before the shift." |

## Count clauses, unify paragraphs, then cut and specify

### Every sentence error is a clause error

Sentence patterns, run-ons and fragments all come down to one skill: **finding the clauses**. An independent clause has a subject and a verb and "expresses a complete thought." A dependent clause has both but cannot stand alone. It is usually introduced by a marker word such as *because, although, when, if* or *while* ([Purdue OWL: Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html)).

The four sentence types are simply clause counts: a **simple** sentence has one independent clause, a **compound** sentence has two or more, a **complex** sentence has one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause, and a **compound-complex** sentence has two or more independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause ([Purdue OWL: Sentence Types](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/sentence_types.html)).

A compound verb does not create a second clause, so "The glacier melted, broke apart, and slipped into the sea" is still a simple sentence ([Guide to Grammar and Writing](https://guidetogrammar.org/grammar/objects.htm)).

Inside each clause, *Writing for Success* names six subject–verb patterns. It says that writers who use them should be able to "avoid writing fragments" ([Writing for Success](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)). The Guide to Grammar adds the object complement, as in "They named their daughter Natasha" ([Guide to Grammar](https://guidetogrammar.org/grammar/objects.htm)). The course may count five, six or seven patterns, so learn all seven in the table below. The example sentences are constructed.

| Kind | Label | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | S-V | The circuit overheated. |
| Pattern | S-V-Adv | The train arrived late. |
| Pattern | S-LV-N (predicate noun) | Copper is a good conductor. |
| Pattern | S-LV-Adj (predicate adjective) | The solution looks cloudy. |
| Pattern | S-V-DO | The technician replaced the fuse. |
| Pattern | S-V-IO-DO | The manager sent the client a revised quote. |
| Pattern | S-V-DO-OC (object complement) | The safety panel declared the design acceptable. |
| Type | Simple | The turbine stopped at 25 m/s. |
| Type | Compound | The turbine stopped, and the alarm sounded. |
| Type | Complex | When the wind speed exceeded 25 m/s, the turbine stopped. |
| Type | Compound-complex | When the wind speed exceeded 25 m/s, the turbine stopped, and the operators logged the event. |

The three classic errors are clause errors. A **comma splice** is "the use of a comma between two independent clauses," a **fused sentence** (run-on) joins two independent clauses with no punctuation at all, and a **fragment** is a dependent clause or phrase punctuated as if it were a sentence ([Purdue OWL: Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html)).

There are four standard repairs for splices and run-ons: a period, a semicolon, a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*), or subordination. With a conjunctive adverb such as *however* or *therefore*, "a semicolon is needed before the independent marker word" ([Purdue OWL: Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html); [Purdue OWL: Run-ons](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/runonsentences.html)).

UNC adds the dash as a fifth repair, a quick test (a run-on cannot be turned into a single yes/no question), and the margin codes graders actually write: "frag," "S.F." and "R.O." ([UNC: Fragments and Run-ons](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/fragments-and-run-ons/)).

Most fragments are pieces cut off from a neighbouring sentence. Purdue's fix is to "remove the period between the fragment and the main clause" ([Purdue OWL: Fragments](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/sentence_fragments.html)).

Correct sentences still need variety. Purdue warns that sentences of the same structure and length "can grow monotonous" and advises varying their length and openings ([Purdue OWL: Sentence Variety](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html)). Last notes that technical writing favours simple sentences and an average of "around 20 to 30 words per sentence," with short sentences reserved for main points ([Last, 2.2](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)). The repairs below are constructed.

| Problem | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Comma splice | The sensor failed, the system shut down. | The sensor failed, so the system shut down. *Or:* Because the sensor failed, the system shut down. |
| "However" splice | The test passed, however the report was late. | The test passed; however, the report was late. |
| Fused sentence | The beam deflected too much it needed a deeper section. | The beam deflected too much; it needed a deeper section. |
| Dependent-clause fragment | We rejected the batch. Because the tolerance was exceeded. | We rejected the batch because the tolerance was exceeded. |
| "Such as" fragment | We tested three alloys. Such as aluminium, steel, and titanium. | We tested three alloys: aluminium, steel, and titanium. |
| "-ing" fragment | Using a finite-element model. The team predicted the peak stress. | Using a finite-element model, the team predicted the peak stress. |
| Choppy series | The bridge was built in 1965. It carries heavy traffic. Engineers inspected it last month. They found fatigue cracks. | Built in 1965, the bridge now carries heavy traffic, and when engineers inspected it last month, they found fatigue cracks. |
| Same opening every time | The robot picks up the part. The robot scans the barcode. The robot places the part on the conveyor. | After picking up the part, the robot scans its barcode. Once the scan is confirmed, it places the part on the conveyor. |

### Paragraphs need one controlling idea and explained support

Purdue's rule of thumb is "keep one idea to one paragraph." Its four requirements are unity, coherence, a topic sentence and adequate development, and it tells writers to be "wary of paragraphs that only have two or three sentences" ([Purdue OWL: Paragraphs](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)).

*Writing for Success* sharpens each part. A topic sentence contains "both a topic and a controlling idea or opinion" and "does not include supporting details"; most paragraphs carry "three to six supporting sentences," each offering a reason, fact, statistic, quotation or example; length runs to roughly 75–200 words; and the concluding sentence restates the main point "without restating it in exactly the same words," adding no new ideas ([Writing for Success](https://opentextbc.ca/writingforsuccess/chapter/chapter-3-putting-ideas-into-your-own-words-and-paragraphs/)).

UNC's development rule is the one students break most often: "NONE of your examples should be left unexplained" ([UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)). When a paragraph uses sources, Harvard wants the topic sentence in your own words, never a quotation ([Harvard: Nuts & Bolts](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/nuts-bolts-integrating-0)).

Transitions "tell readers what to do with the information you present to them," but they "cannot substitute for good organization" ([UNC: Transitions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/transitions/)). Purdue sorts them by job: adding (*furthermore*), contrasting (*in contrast*), proving (*because, indeed*) and concluding (*as a result, thus*) ([Purdue OWL: Transitional Devices](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/transitions_and_transitional_devices/transitional_devices.html)).

The class may grade paragraphs on Langan's four "bases" (unity, support, coherence and sentence skills). If so, these outside criteria map onto them one to one. This research could not verify the wording *Exploring Writing* uses, so check the textbook before using those terms in an answer.

The method of development follows from the paragraph's purpose and decides the order of the support. UNC names narration, description, process, classification and illustration ([UNC: Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/)). Purdue adds defining, comparing, causes, effects and chronology ([Purdue OWL: Paragraphs](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)). The topic sentences in the table are constructed.

| Method | Order of support | Typical transitions | Engineering topic sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Claim, then two or three explained cases | for example, for instance, similarly | Redundancy keeps critical systems running when a part fails. |
| Narration | Chronological | first, then, later, finally | The 2 a.m. power failure exposed three weaknesses in our shutdown procedure. |
| Description | Spatial, or feature by feature | above, below, inside | The failed bearing showed classic signs of overheating. |
| Process | Step by step | first, next, then, finally | Measuring resistance with a multimeter takes four steps. |
| Cause and effect | Cause, mechanism, effect | because, as a result, consequently | De-icing salt is a major cause of bridge-deck deterioration. |

| Argument | Claim, evidence, counterargument, reply | because, therefore, admittedly | The university should replace its fluorescent lighting with LEDs. |

```
TS  [Topic] + [controlling idea]          own words; no detail; never a quotation
S1  [transition] [specific fact, number or example]  ->  [why it supports the TS]
S2  [transition] [second support]                    ->  [explanation]
S3  [transition] [third support]                     ->  [explanation]

```

> **Model paragraph (cause and effect, constructed):** De-icing salt is a major cause of damage to concrete bridge decks in cold climates. When salt is spread on roads in winter, chloride ions dissolve in the meltwater and gradually soak into the concrete. *Once* enough chloride reaches the steel reinforcing bars, it breaks down the protective layer around the steel, and the bars begin to rust. *Because* rust takes up more space than the original steel, it pushes outward and causes the surrounding concrete to crack and flake off. *As a result*, even more water and salt can enter, *so* the damage speeds up over time. *In short*, a chemical that keeps roads safe in winter slowly weakens the structures that carry those roads.

### The four C's reward deletions and specifics

The course grades on four C's. Suzan Last's open engineering textbook places them inside a longer list of seven: clear, coherent, concise, concrete, correct, complete and courteous ([Last, 2.2](https://pressbooks.bccampus.ca/technicalwriting/chapter/communicatingprecision/)). Last defines **clear** writing as conveying "the purpose of the document immediately," with each sentence carrying one idea; **concise** writing as using "the least words possible to convey the most meaning while still maintaining clarity"; **coherent** writing as linked by transitions, "planned repetition" and parallel lists, without which it "often sounds 'choppy'"; and **complete** writing as including "all requested information" and answering "all relevant questions" (same source).

When the C's conflict, Last's tie-breaker is to "always give priority to being clear." *Communication at Work* adds two clarity rules that cost students marks: write "a 10% budget cut," not "a change in the budget," and "Always spell out months," because "4/5/27" could mean April 5th or May 4th ([Communication at Work, 4.5](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/4-5-standard-business-style/)).

Most four-C repairs are **deletions or substitutions**. Purdue's principle is that "Concise writing does not always have the fewest words, but it always uses the strongest ones" ([Purdue OWL: Concision](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html)). Its toolkit cuts filler (*kind of, really, basically*) and redundant pairs (*end result, past history, free gift*) ([Purdue OWL: Eliminating Words](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/eliminating_words.html)). It also removes expletive openings, so "There are four rules that should be observed" becomes "Four rules should be observed"; turns nominalizations (verbs turned into nouns, such as "collection") back into verbs, so "The function of this department is the collection of accounts" (10 words) becomes "This department collects accounts" (4 words); and replaces roundabout phrases such as "due to the fact that" with "because" ([Purdue OWL: Common Pitfalls](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/avoid_common_pitfalls.html)).

Engineering students should note UNC's caveat. The passive voice "is not a grammatical error, and it can be useful, especially in scientific writing" ([UNC: Writing Concisely](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conciseness-handout/)). So convert the passives that hide who acted, not every passive.

Revision is where these marks are won, and it is where students put in the least time. Workplace writers spend **about 40% of their time planning and 30% revising**, while some studies find that students spend **only 3–5% of their writing time revising** ([Business Writing For Everyone, KPU](https://kpu.pressbooks.pub/businesswriting/chapter/the-writing-process-in-the-workplace/)). The pairs below are constructed except where marked.

| C | Grader's test | Before | After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clear | Can I state the point after one read? | The thing with the sensor might have caused some issues with the readings at some point. | The temperature sensor on Line 2 read 4 °C too high from 10:00 to 14:00 on 14 October. |
| Clear (concrete) | Are there measurable details instead of "better" or "big"? | The new design is much better. | The new bracket is 18% lighter and passed 10⁶ load cycles without cracking. |
| Concise | Can any word go without losing meaning? | Due to the fact that the pump was in a state of malfunction, it was decided by the team that a replacement should be made. (25 words) | Because the pump malfunctioned, the team replaced it. (8 words) |
| Concise (Purdue's pair) | Is every word earning its place? | The teacher demonstrated some of the various ways and methods for cutting words from my essay that I had written for class. (22 words) | The teacher demonstrated methods for cutting words from my essay. (10 words) |
| Coherent | Are the relationships between ideas signalled? | The new valve reduced leaks. Maintenance costs fell. Installation took longer than planned. Operators needed training. | The new valve reduced leaks; as a result, maintenance costs fell. However, installation took longer than planned, partly because operators first needed training on the new design. |

The **complete** test asks who, what, how much, what action and by when. A one-line message fails it. The rewrite below passes, and is courteous as well.

> **Before:** "hi, the test failed. pls fix asap."
>
> **After:** "Dear Ms. Karimova, The tensile test on sample B-3 failed this morning: it fractured at 410 MPa, below the 450 MPa specification. I have attached the stress–strain curve and the test log. Could you check the heat-treatment record for batch B and let me know by Thursday, 22 October, whether we should retest? Thank you, Timur Rakhimov, Materials Lab"

## News writing rewards ranking facts, not reciting them

The news block covers news values, the inverted pyramid, leads, where to begin and angles. All of it tests one decision, made again at every scale: **which fact matters most to this audience, right now**. Students who list facts in the order they happened, or who list news values without linking them to the scenario, lose marks for the same reason. They have not ranked anything.

### Name the news value and the fact that triggers it

Purdue presents two models of newsworthiness. One, attributed to Galtung and Ruge, lists relevance, timeliness, simplification, predictability, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, elite people, elite countries and negativity. The other, attributed to Shoemaker and colleagues (1987), lists timeliness, proximity, importance or impact, interest, conflict, sensationalism, prominence and novelty, which is the "man bites dog" test ([Purdue OWL: Newsworthiness](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/components_of_newsworthiness.html)).

The News Manual's simpler tests ask whether a story is new, unusual, interesting, significant and about people, and it stresses that "The same event can have different levels of interest in different societies" ([The News Manual, Ch. 1](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_01.htm)). Media Helping Media adds **currency** (topics "already being talked about by the public"), a "so what" test, and an evidence rule that news must "never" rest on "gossip or unsourced rumours" ([Media Helping Media](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/spotting-a-news-story/)).

If asked for dates, give the right one. Purdue dates the Galtung and Ruge model to 1973, but their original study appeared in 1965. Harcup and O'Neill's 2017 update found that "shareability and immediacy increasingly determine what gets reported" ([Wikipedia: News values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_values)).

In an exam, a bare list of values earns little. **Name the value, define it, point to the fact in the scenario that triggers it, and say how it shapes the lead.**

The commonest slip is confusing prominence (who is involved) with impact (how many people are affected). Proximity also includes emotional or cultural closeness, such as an Uzbek student winning abroad. The memory aid CHIP PUT in the table below is constructed, as are the examples.

| Value | Test question | Campus example |
|---|---|---|
| **C**onflict | Who disagrees, and about what? | Students petition against a new attendance rule |
| **H**uman interest | Is there a person whose story moves readers? | A student builds a prosthetic hand for her brother |
| **I**mpact | How many people are affected, how seriously, for how long? | Tuition rises 20% for all 3,000 students |
| **P**rominence | Is anyone famous or powerful involved? | A minister speaks at graduation |
| **P**roximity | How close is this, geographically or emotionally, to my readers? | A road closure outside the main gate |
| **U**nusualness | Would readers say "really?" | The first all-female team wins a national robotics title |
| **T**imeliness | Why publish this now? | Results announced this morning |

### The inverted pyramid must survive the cut test

Purdue defines the inverted pyramid as placing "the most fundamental information in the lead paragraph of the story" and then arranging "the remaining details, from most important to least important." Editors can then cut from the bottom without losing anything essential ([Purdue OWL: Inverted Pyramid](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html)). The form grew up with the telegraph. Poynter's classic example is the AP lead after Lincoln was shot: "The President was shot in a theater to-night and perhaps mortally wounded." Poynter also records critics who call the form "outdated, unnatural, boring, artless" ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/writing-from-the-top-down-pros-and-cons-of-the-inverted-pyramid/)).

A story must answer who, what, where, when, why and how, but the News Manual insists that "they should not all be answered in your intro." Its method is to list the key points, rank them by newsworthiness and lead with the winner, as in "Six people were killed and more than 100 left homeless when Cyclone Victor hit the Solomon Islands yesterday" ([The News Manual, Ch. 4](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_04.htm)).

After any soft opening, the story needs a **nut graf**. Poynter says it "tells the reader what the writer is up to." It is named because, "like a nut, it contains the 'kernel'" of the story. The *Wall Street Journal*'s Ken Wells calls it "a paragraph that says what this whole story is about and why you should read it" ([Poynter: The nut graf](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/the-nut-graf-part-i/)). Purdue uses "nut graphs" loosely for all the body paragraphs, so give Poynter's single-paragraph meaning in an exam.

Attribution follows strict rules. The News Manual says to "Attribute ALL opinions and any information which is not a clear and undisputed fact," to default to "said," and to avoid "stated" and "pointed out," which imply undisputed fact, as well as "claimed," which suggests disbelief. The reason is simple: "If you do not attribute an opinion to an individual, your audience will assume it is your own" ([The News Manual, Ch. 9](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_09.htm)).

AP style, via Purdue, uses the day of the week rather than "yesterday," spells out numbers below 10, and writes times as "1 p.m." ([Purdue OWL: AP Style](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/ap_style.html)). A hard-news pyramid ends on its least important fact, never on a summary or a moral. The six-paragraph template and model story below are syntheses of these sources. The facts, institution and people in the story are fictional.

| Paragraph | Role | Usual content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lead | The most newsworthy W's: what happened, to whom, where, when |
| 2 | Support and source | How and why; who says so |
| 3 | Consequence (often the nut graf) | Decisions, next steps, who else is affected |
| 4 | Best quote or human element | Emotion, vivid detail, a person |
| 5 | Secondary details | Costs, closures, timelines |
| 6 | Background | History; the first paragraph an editor cuts |

> **(1)** A fire blamed on a charging robot battery forced about 120 people out of a Chorsu Technical Institute engineering building on Monday and sent two students to the campus clinic.
>

Aziz Rakhimov, the fire officer in charge. Firefighters put it out within 20 minutes, and it did not spread beyond the lab, he said. >

The cause is attributed ("blamed on," "said") because it is preliminary. The quotes carry emotion and the official position, not routine facts.

### Leads run 20 to 30 words, put facts first and avoid opening quotes

Purdue calls the lead "the most important part of a news story." It is "generally 25 to 30 words and should rarely be more than 40" ([Purdue OWL: Leads](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)). The News Manual sets a tighter ceiling of 20 words and adds that "Two short sentences are better than one long, crowded and confused sentence" ([The News Manual, Ch. 4](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_04.htm)). The safe exam rule is to aim for 20–25 words and never exceed about 35.

The News Manual's five golden rules come with right and wrong examples, reproduced in the table below. The passive is allowed when the object is more newsworthy than the actor ([The News Manual, Ch. 5](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_05.htm)).

| Golden rule | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|
| Keep it short and simple | "Mr Mug Wump, president of the Port Moresby Waterside Workers' Union, yesterday condemned…" | "A Port Moresby union leader yesterday condemned politicians…" |
| Use the active voice | "A new copra subsidy scheme has been demanded by growers." | "Copra growers have demanded a new subsidy scheme." |
| Put facts first | "While the Prime Minister was attending a concert last night, thieves broke into his official residence." | "Thieves broke into the Prime Minister's official residence last night, while he was attending a concert." |
| Keep it up to date | "It was announced in Funafuti last night that Prince Charles would visit Tuvalu in August." | "Prince Charles will visit Tuvalu in August." |
| "Never begin with direct quotation" | Opening with the Finance Minister's own words | "Income tax is to rise by seven percent in January." |

Purdue adds three cautions: avoid starting with "it," avoid flowery language, and remember that a lead is "an implicit promise to your readers" ([Purdue OWL: Leads](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/writing_leads.html)).

Purdue's three named types are the summary, anecdotal and question leads. A soft or delayed lead is "typically followed by a nut graph" ([Wikipedia: News style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_style)). ### Begin with what changed, then narrow topics into story questions

**Begin with what changed**: the decision, result, finding or injury, not the process that led to it. A meeting story opens with the decision, a speech story with the most newsworthy thing said, and a research story with the finding and what it means for people. A feature opens with the moment or person that best embodies its point and then delivers the nut graf, and a follow-up opens with the newest development rather than a rerun of the original story ([The News Manual, Ch. 24](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_24.htm)).

Three quick tests help: what would you tell a friend first, what headline does the lead deliver, and would the story survive if only paragraph 1 ran?

A topic is not a story. Media Helping Media's "so what" factor holds that "Numbers, second sources, and added information provide the context that turns an observation into a piece of journalism." In its example, young people making a living from a city dump become a story only when the reporter shows that one day's work feeds a family of four for a week ([Media Helping Media: So what](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/news-sources-numbers-and-the-so-what-factor/)). Its angle questions, "Who is benefiting from this narrative?" and "What information is missing?", turn routine material into original stories ([Media Helping Media: Angles](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/how-to-find-and-develop-important-news-angles/)). Its case study shows a resident cut off at a council meeting becoming a story about "a systemic failure in public infrastructure" ([Media Helping Media: Original story](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/the-evolution-of-an-original-news-story/)). For follow-up ideas, the News Manual suggests reviewing stories from six months, one year and five years ago. "If a politician promised action a year ago, now is the time to ask him what he has achieved" ([The News Manual, Ch. 24](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%201/volume1_24.htm)). The funnel below is a synthesis.

```
TOPIC           broad subject                   "air pollution"
ISSUE           a tension inside it             "indoor air in crowded classrooms"
ANGLE           specific, new, local, human     "Afternoon lectures in our oldest building may exceed
                                                 indoor CO2 guidelines, and no one is measuring."
STORY QUESTION  answerable before deadline      "What do CO2 sensors in Building A's largest hall read at
                                                 9 a.m. vs 3 p.m. this week, and what does facilities say?"
SO WHAT?        who is affected, how, why now
SOURCES         documents, measurements, people on all sides
LEAD            25 words or fewer, plus a working headline
```

The class's "murder hornets" sample topic shows the funnel at work, using Wikipedia's documented timeline ([Wikipedia: Asian giant hornet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_giant_hornet)). As a topic it is too broad, because biology, invasion, media hype and eradication are separate stories. An **eradication** angle can draw on the sourced timeline: hornets were first detected near Nanaimo, British Columbia, and Blaine, Washington, in 2019; in October 2020 a nest of about 500 live hornets, including about 200 queens, was found in a tree cavity in Blaine; three more nests were found in 2021; and Washington's agriculture department declared the insect eradicated in December 2024. Its story question is how the nests were found and what made eradication possible. A **naming** angle starts from the May 2020 *New York Times* article that helped spread the "murder hornet" nickname and the 2022 decision by entomologists to adopt "northern giant hornet," citing concerns about "potentially discriminatory language"; it ties directly to the sensationalism news value. A **localized** angle asks which hornets threaten Tashkent-region beekeepers and who monitors invasive insects. This research contains no Uzbek facts on that, so it is a question to report, not a claim to make.

## Media law turns on provable facts, and Uzbekistan puts the burden on the writer

### Defamation needs a false statement of fact, and "in my opinion" is no shield

**Defamation** is "a statement that harms another person's reputation." **Libel** covers "written or otherwise recorded statements," which includes posts and broadcasts, and **slander** covers "spoken statements" ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)). The Digital Media Law Project lists six US elements: **publication** to a third party; a statement **"of and concerning" the plaintiff**, which can identify someone without naming them if "those who know the person will recognize the statement"; **defamatory meaning**; **falsity**; **fault**; and **no applicable privilege** ([DMLP](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation)).

Accusations of a serious crime or of professional incompetence are classic **defamation per se**, where damages can be presumed ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)).

The main defences are truth, opinion and privilege. **Truth** protects "substantially true" statements even if minor details are wrong ([DMLP](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation)). **Opinion** protects pure opinion that cannot be proven true or false, but the label does not work like a password. DMLP's example "in my opinion, the mayor killed her husband" is not protected ([DMLP: Opinion](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/opinion-and-fair-comment-privileges)), and in *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal* (1990) the Supreme Court held that "In my opinion John Jones is a liar" can imply facts, while rhetorical hyperbole stays protected ([Cornell LII: Milkovich](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/497/1)). **Privilege** is absolute for statements made in judicial and legislative proceedings ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)), and a fair report privilege may protect accurate reports that rely on public documents or official statements ([DMLP: Defenses](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/defamation-privileges-and-defenses)).

In the US, public officials must prove **"actual malice"**: publication "with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not" (*New York Times v. Sullivan*, 1964). *Curtis v. Butts* (1967) extended the rule to public figures. Under *Gertz* (1974), private figures may need to show only negligence ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation)).

Commonwealth practice, as the News Manual describes it, is harsher on writers: the journalist must prove the words true, quoting someone else is **no defence**, and innuendo and cartoons can defame ([The News Manual, Ch. 69](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_69.htm)).

The Commonwealth defences are justification, fair comment, privilege for fair and accurate reports of courts and parliament, public interest, and consent. "No comment" is not consent, and malice defeats every defence ([The News Manual, Ch. 70](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_70.htm)).

### Privacy, copyright and ethics set three separate limits

US privacy law recognises four torts: **intrusion on seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light and appropriation** ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/invasion_of_privacy)). A **private-facts** claim needs a public disclosure of a private fact that is "highly offensive to a reasonable person" and not newsworthy, and newsworthiness does not cover "a morbid and sensational prying into private lives for its own sake" ([DMLP: Private facts](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/publication-private-facts)). **False light** protects against emotional distress rather than reputational harm; its textbook case is a stock street photo used to illustrate a story about prostitution ([DMLP: False light](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/false-light)). **Appropriation** targets the commercial use of a person's name or likeness, and news reporting is often exempt ([DMLP](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/publishing-personal-and-private-information)).

Copyright protects expression, not information. "Copyright does not protect facts, ideas, systems, or methods of operation, although it may protect the way these things are expressed," and it does not cover "names, titles, slogans, or short phrases" ([U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-protect.html)). Fair use weighs four factors: purpose and character, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market. "There is no formula" for a safe number of words ([U.S. Copyright Office: Fair Use](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)), and "In cases of doubt, the Copyright Office recommends that permission be obtained" ([Copyright Office FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html)). The classic journalism case is *Harper & Row* (1985), where about 300 words taken from an unpublished memoir weighed against fair use ([Cornell LII: Fair use](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/fair_use)). Commonwealth fair dealing allows short, attributed extracts, but "Simply rearranging the paragraphs is not enough to avoid copyright" ([The News Manual, Ch. 73](https://www.thenewsmanual.net/Manuals%20Volume%203/volume3_73.htm)). The exam point is that **credit is not permission**. Plagiarism is an ethical wrong that crediting the source cures. Infringement is a legal wrong that crediting does not cure.

Ethics sets a higher standard than law. The SPJ Code's four principles are **Seek Truth and Report It, Minimize Harm, Act Independently, and Be Accountable and Transparent**, and the code says it is not "legally enforceable." Lines worth quoting include "Never plagiarize. Always attribute," the rule that anonymity is "a last resort," the instruction "Do not pay sources in exchange for interviews or information," and the reminder that "legal access to information differs from the ethical justification for reporting it" ([SPJ Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)). SPJ's live code page shows a 2026 revision, while its revision-project page says the 2014 code stays in force until members approve the change. The four principle headings are the same in both versions, so they are safe to cite ([SPJ revision project](https://www.spj.org/spj-ethics-code-revision-project-2026/)).

### Uzbekistan escalates slander from fines to crimes

Uzbekistan's 2023 Constitution guarantees freedom of thought, speech and information, which may be restricted only by law and for listed aims (Art. 33). It declares the mass media free and "responsible for the reliability of the information they provide" (Art. 81), states that "Censorship is not permitted" (Art. 82), and protects privacy, honour, dignity and personal data (Art. 31) ([lex.uz: Constitution](https://lex.uz/docs/6445145); translations unofficial).

Liability escalates in three steps. First come **administrative fines**: the Code of Administrative Responsibility fines slander (Art. 40, 20–60 times the base calculation amount) and insult (Art. 41, 20–40 times) ([lex.uz: Administrative Code](https://lex.uz/docs/97664)). Next comes **criminal liability**. Criminal Code Art. 139 makes slander a crime when it follows an administrative penalty for the same act (Part 1), while Part 2 covers slander in print, the mass media, telecom networks or the internet, with penalties that include fines of 200–400 times the base amount or two to three years of correctional labour; Art. 140 mirrors this for insult. Finally, Art. 158(3) separately punishes **public insult or slander of the President** with up to five years' imprisonment ([lex.uz: Criminal Code](https://lex.uz/docs/111457)).

On the civil side, the Supreme Court's Plenum Resolution No. 21 of 24 November 2025 puts the burden on the **defendant to prove that the published information is true**. It also rules that opinions cannot be ordered refuted but give the target a right of reply, requires media to publish refutations within one month, and requires website owners and bloggers to "immediately remove false information" ([lex.uz: Plenum No. 21](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/7901166)).

The practical climate matters for anyone writing in Tashkent. RSF ranked Uzbekistan **147th of 180 in 2026** ([RSF](https://rsf.org/en/country/uzbekistan)). Freedom House reports that "In the first 10 months of 2024, over 30,000 people were prosecuted under defamation and insult charges in the Administrative Code," with more than 200 reportedly imprisoned ([Freedom House](https://freedomhouse.org/country/uzbekistan/freedom-net/2025)). Uzbekistan has been party to the Berne Convention since 2005 ([WIPO Lex](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/members/profile/UZ)).

| Question | United States | UK and Commonwealth | Uzbekistan |
|---|---|---|---|

## Professional messages succeed when the reader can act after one read

### Diagnose miscommunication by naming the component that failed

Communication is "the process of understanding and sharing meaning." *Business Communication for Success* breaks it into eight components: source, message, channel, receiver, feedback, environment, context, and interference (noise) ([Business Communication for Success, 1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)). The source **encodes** thoughts into a message, and the receiver **decodes** it. Noise can be **environmental** (physical distraction) or **semantic** (a symbol or abbreviation the receiver does not understand) ([Communication in the Real World, 1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)). It can also be **psychological**, as in the textbook's example of an employee who, thinking ahead to the reply, sends only two of the three items a 4:45 p.m. email requested. Feedback is the cure: "As the amount of feedback increases, the accuracy of communication also increases" ([Business Communication for Success, 1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)).

The three classic models build on each other ([Communication in the Real World, 1.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/01%3A_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/1.02%3A_The_Communication_Process)). The **transmission** model is "a linear, one-way process," the legacy of Shannon and Weaver, and suits a text message. The **interaction** model adds turn-taking with feedback within physical and psychological contexts, and suits an email thread. The **transaction** model holds that "we are simultaneously senders and receivers" within social, relational and cultural contexts, and suits a live design review.

```
 SENDER --encode--> MESSAGE --via CHANNEL--> --decode--> RECEIVER
   ^                                                        |
   |<------------------------ FEEDBACK ---------------------|
 NOISE anywhere: environmental, semantic, psychological
 CONTEXT around everything: physical, psychological, social, relational, cultural
```

The exam habit is to diagnose any scenario by naming the component that failed. Jargon is semantic noise. Skim-reading is psychological noise. Bad news sent by text is a channel error. No confirmation of understanding is a missing feedback loop.

On channels, *Communication at Work* warns that "Addressing sensitive situations exclusively by email (or, even worse, by text message) tends to intensify conflict." It calls in-person conversation "the most information-rich channel" and advises discussing problems face to face, then confirming the action plan by email ([Communication at Work, 2.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/2-3-selecting-appropriate-channels/)). The logic comes from media richness theory ([Daft & Lengel, 1986](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.32.5.554)): use rich channels for sensitive, complex or negotiable matters, and lean written channels for routine facts that need a record. Two rules govern online behaviour. Virginia Shea's first rule of netiquette is "Remember the human" ([Albion.com](http://www.albion.com/netiquette/corerules.html)). The textbook's test for any business email is "Never write or send anything that you wouldn't want read in public or in front of your company president" ([Business Communication for Success, 9.1](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s13-01-text-e-mail-and-netiquette.html)).

### Adapt every message to its reader

Audience analysis comes first. Ask "How big is the audience?" and "Do they already know about your topic?" ([Business Communication for Success, 3.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s07-04-getting-to-know-your-audience.html)), then map the readers with Purdue's "complex audience": a **primary** audience (the decision maker), a **secondary** audience (a technical expert), a **shadow** audience ("others who may read your communication"), a **gatekeeper** such as your supervisor, and the **stakeholders** affected by the decision ([Purdue OWL: Audience Analysis](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/audience_analysis/index.html)).

The adaptation rules are consistent across sources. Purdue's target **tone** is "confident, courteous, and sincere," stressing the "you" attitude ([Purdue OWL: Tone](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/tone_in_business_writing.html)). **Positive emphasis** means avoiding words that deny or carry unpleasant associations, choosing to "Open with action rather than apology or explanation," and putting good news at the beginning and end ([Purdue OWL: Accentuating the Positives](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/accentuating_the_positives.html)). The **"you" view** works because messages using "you" and "your" "have a much greater impact when the message is positive or even neutral" ([Communication at Work, 4.5](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/4-5-standard-business-style/)). **Bias-free language** follows APA's point that "using 'man' to refer to all human beings is not as accurate or inclusive" as "individuals" or "people," and its instruction to "describe only relevant characteristics" ([APA Style: Bias-Free Language](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/bias-free-language/general-principles)). On **formality**, "external communications tend to be more formal" ([Business Communication for Success, 4.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s08-04-style-in-written-communication.html)).

**Plain language** even has a legal definition: "writing that is clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience" ([Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ274/html/PLAW-111publ274.htm)). Digital.gov's first rule is "write for your audience," which does not mean "dumb down" ([Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language)); it favours "shorter words, short sections, active voice, present tense," and flags hidden verbs, which end in *-ment, -tion, -sion* or *-ance* ([Digital.gov: Writing](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing)). The archived federal guidelines add "place the main idea before exceptions and conditions" and "use 'must' to indicate requirements" ([GSA plain-language archive](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov)).

### Meetings exist to make decisions; minutes record who does what by when

Teams move through **forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning** ([Tuckman, 1965](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022100); [Tuckman & Jensen, 1977](https://doi.org/10.1177/105960117700200404)). The stages are "not always experienced in a linear fashion," and groups return to forming as members come and go ([Communication in the Real World, 13.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/13%3A_Small_Group_Communication/13.02%3A_Small_Group_Development)). Storming, when "conflict emerges as people begin to perform their various roles," is normal. The danger is getting stuck there (same source).

Member roles fall into three groups. **Task roles** include the expediter, who manages the agenda, the gatekeeper, who "manages the flow of conversation… so that all group members get to participate," and the recorder; **maintenance roles** include the harmonizer and the interpreter; and **negative roles** include the monopolizer, the blocker and the withdrawer, who is more likely to appear "when groups exceed five members" ([Communication in the Real World, 14.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/14%3A_Leadership_Roles_and_Problem_Solving_in_Groups/14.02%3A_Group_Member_Roles)). Belbin's nine Team Roles add a behavioural lens (Plant, Monitor Evaluator, Specialist, Shaper, Implementer, Completer Finisher, Resource Investigator, Teamworker and Co-ordinator), and most people are comfortable in two or three of them ([Belbin](https://www.belbin.com/about/belbin-team-roles)).

Conflict styles sit on two axes, concern for self and concern for others, and yield **competing**, **avoiding**, **accommodating**, **compromising**, which "is not a win/win solution; it is a partial win/lose," and **collaborating**, which "ultimately leads to a win/win situation" ([Communication in the Real World, 6.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/06%3A_Interpersonal_Communication_Processes/6.02%3A_Conflict_and_Interpersonal_Communication)). The same source warns that these styles were "formulated with a Western bias": participants in the US, Germany, China and Japan all saw avoiding as showing concern for the other person.

For feedback without blame, the Center for Creative Leadership's **SBI** model says to "Clarify the Situation, Describe the specific Behaviors observed, and Explain the Impact." CCL reports that it reduces both the giver's anxiety and the recipient's defensiveness ([CCL: SBI](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/)).

A meeting is "a group communication in action around a defined agenda, at a set time, for an established duration" ([Business Communication for Success, 15.3](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-03-meetings.html)). "Getting together just to get together is called a party, not a meeting" ([Business Communication for Success, 19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)). The discipline matters: executives now average nearly 23 hours a week in meetings, up from less than 10 in the 1960s ([HBR, 2017](https://hbr.org/2017/07/stop-the-meeting-madness)).

*Communication at Work* sets the rules for agendas and minutes. Agendas list items "in order of priority," give each a time allowance, and go out "a week in advance." Minutes are "a permanent record of the meeting's discussion" that "summarizes major points raised, decisions taken, and actions assigned," with action items highlighted, as in "ACTION: Melinda to investigate new software…," and they stay "tentative until officially approved at the next meeting" ([Communication at Work, 11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/)).

The chair closes by summarizing what was decided and what actions members are to take ([Business Communication for Success, 19.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s23-04-business-and-professional-meet.html)). The template is a synthesis of these sources.

```
[TEAM NAME] — MINUTES OF [MEETING NAME]
Date: [Day, DD Month YYYY]   Time: [start]–[end]   Location: [room or link]
Chair: [name]   Recorder: [name]
Present: [names]   Absent (with apologies): [names]

1. Call to order: [time]
2. Previous minutes: approved as circulated / approved with corrections [...]
3. [Agenda item]: summary in 1–3 neutral lines.
   DECISION: [what was agreed or the vote result]
   ACTION: [Owner] to [specific task] by [date].
4. Next meeting: [date, time, place]
5. Adjournment: [time]

ACTION ITEMS:  # | Action | Owner | Due
Draft until approved at the next meeting.
```

The commonest faults in minutes are a verbatim transcript instead of a summary, recorded emotion ("!!") or opinions, a passive "It was agreed that something should be done" with no owner or deadline, minutes sent weeks late, and private details such as "absent (sick)" where "absent with apologies" belongs.

### Listen actively and read culture as tendency, not destiny

"Active listening refers to the process of pairing outwardly visible positive listening behaviors with positive cognitive listening practices." Active listeners "ask appropriate clarifying and follow-up questions and paraphrase information to check understanding" ([Communication in the Real World, 5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence)). MindTools' five steps are "paying attention; showing that you're listening; providing feedback; deferring judgment; and responding appropriately." It warns that active listening can suggest agreement you do not feel ([MindTools](https://www.mindtools.com/az4wxv7/active-listening)). CCL models two of its techniques: to **reflect**, "So Emma's people skills are great, but accountability is a problem," and to **clarify**, "Let me see if I'm understanding. Are you talking about …?" ([CCL: Active Listening](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/coaching-others-use-active-listening-skills/)).

Nonverbal communication runs through six channels: **kinesics** (movement), **haptics** (touch), **vocalics** (pitch, rate and fillers), **proxemics** (space), **chronemics** (time), and **personal presentation and environment** ([Communication in the Real World, 4.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/04%3A_Nonverbal_Communication/4.02%3A_Types_of_Nonverbal_Communication)).

Phone calls have five stages: opening, feedforward ("do you have five minutes…?"), business, feedback and closing ([Business Communication for Success, 15.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s19-02-telephone-voip-communication.html)). In video meetings, put the camera "at eye-level," "Look directly into the camera when speaking," "Mute your microphone if you're not talking" and avoid multitasking ([Communication at Work, 11.3](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/11-3-group-meetings-and-web-conferencing/)).

Intercultural questions draw on four frameworks. On **context**, low-context styles carry most meaning in the words, while high-context styles carry it in "nonverbal and contextual cues," and high-context cultures "value silence as a form of communication." The same source notes that in some cultures, where flat refusal is too direct, a person may answer "maybe" instead of "no" ([Communication in the Real World, 5.3](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/05%3A_Listening/5.03%3A_Improving_Listening_Competence)). A systematic review found that most research using Hall's country rankings rests on "less-than-adequate evidence," so treat them as tendencies ([Kittler, Rygl & Mackinnon, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470595811398797)). On **time**, monochronic cultures "consider one thing at a time," while polychronic cultures schedule many things at once and treat time "in a more fluid sense"; a visitor who arrives at 3:44 for a 3 p.m. meeting in a monochronic culture "will disappoint all who took pains to be there at 2:55pm" ([Communication at Work, 10.4](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/communicationatwork/chapter/10-4-intercultural-communication/)). One open textbook misspells the terms as "monochromatic" and "polychromatic," so do not copy that ([Business Communication for Success, 18.4](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s22-04-divergent-cultural-characteris.html)).

**Hofstede's six dimensions** are power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation and indulgence ([Hofstede](https://geerthofstede.com/culture-geert-hofstede-gert-jan-hofstede/6d-model-of-national-culture/)), and The Culture Factor now labels masculinity "Motivation towards Achievement and Success" ([The Culture Factor](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool?countries=kazakhstan%2Crussia%2Csouth+korea%2Cunited+states)). The fourth framework separates **ethnocentrism**, judging another culture "based on one's own cultural norms," from **cultural relativism**, assessing a culture "by its own standards"; **culture shock** is the resulting "disorientation and frustration," and **xenocentrism** is the belief that another culture is superior ([OpenStax Sociology](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-sociology-3e/pages/3-1-what-is-culture)).

The trap for Tashkent students is citing Hofstede scores for Uzbekistan. **No official Hofstede scores exist for Uzbekistan.** It has no row in Hofstede's 2015 dataset ([Hofstede data matrix](https://geerthofstede.com/research-and-vsm/dimension-data-matrix/)), and The Culture Factor's country tool does not list it ([The Culture Factor](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool?countries=kazakhstan%2Crussia%2Csouth+korea%2Cunited+states)). Kazakhstan is the nearest documented neighbour, but any similarity is a hypothesis.

Scores also shift with method: South Korea's individualism score is 58 on the current tool but 18 in the 2015 dataset. The Culture Factor itself warns that "Differences among the values of individuals in one country tend to be bigger than the value differences between countries" (both sources as above). The table shows current Culture Factor scores, except the US power-distance figure, which comes from the 2015 dataset.

| Dimension | Kazakhstan | Russia | South Korea | United States |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Power distance | 88 | 93 | 60 | 40 |
| Individualism | 20 | 46 | 58 | 60 |
| Motivation (formerly masculinity) | 50 | 36 | 39 | 62 |

An intercultural answer earns marks in three moves. **Name the framework** (context, time, power distance, collectivism or uncertainty avoidance), **link it to an observed behaviour** (silence when objections are invited, a late start, reluctance to pilot without full specifications), and **propose a fix that saves face**: ask open, specific questions rather than "Do you understand?"; collect input in writing or one to one where power distance is high; give criticism privately; and confirm decisions in writing. Close by noting that individuals vary more than countries do.

### Codes of conduct turn norms into rules; gaslighting is a pattern, not a disagreement

US securities regulation defines a code of ethics as "written standards that are reasonably designed to deter wrongdoing" and to promote five things: honest and ethical conduct, including the handling of conflicts of interest; full and accurate disclosure; compliance with the law; "prompt internal reporting of violations"; and "Accountability for adherence to the code" ([17 CFR § 229.406](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/229.406)). Groups run on unstated **norms**, while **rules** are explicit, written in handbooks or "codes of conduct," and their violations "typically result in more explicit punishments" ([Communication in the Real World, 13.2](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communication_in_the_Real_World_-_An_Introduction_to_Communication_Studies/13%3A_Small_Group_Communication/13.02%3A_Small_Group_Development)).

That explains how codes are written. They use plain second-person "must," because plain-language practice says "You must do it," not "It must be done" ([Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing)). The rest of their design follows from the five elements (a synthesis, not a quotation): examples and Q&A turn values into behaviour, named reporting channels with a promise of no retaliation make reporting feel safe, and a signed acknowledgment documents that expectations were communicated.

Gaslighting means "to manipulate another person into doubting their perceptions, experiences, or understanding of events." The APA dictionary calls the term "usually… a colloquialism" and traces it to the 1938 play *Gaslight* ([APA Dictionary of Psychology](https://dictionary.apa.org/gaslight)). Cleveland Clinic stresses "specific patterns of behavior" and notes that gaslighting occurs in the workplace ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/gaslighting)). Merriam-Webster's definition adds "usually over an extended period of time" ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/gaslighting)). Sociologist Paige Sweet ties gaslighting to "power-laden" relationships, which helps explain why boss–subordinate hierarchies enable it ([Sweet, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419874843)).

Cleveland Clinic's signs include calling the target overly emotional or sensitive, blaming them for things they didn't do, denying fault, isolating them from support and withholding information. Its first response is to **document interactions** with screenshots, messages and written records ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/gaslighting)). The comparison below is a synthesis of these definitions.

| | Ordinary disagreement | Honest mistake | Tough but fair feedback | Gaslighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Target | The idea or issue | A fact | Your work or behaviour | Your perception, memory or sanity |
| Evidence | Welcomes it | Corrected when shown the record | Specific and observable | Denies or rewrites documented facts |
| Pattern | One-off or topic-specific | Occasional | Tied to goals, with a path to improve | Repeated over time |
| Example | "I think option B is cheaper; here's my spreadsheet." | "Sorry, I thought the deadline was Friday; you're right, it's Thursday." | "Your test plan missed the load case; please add it by Monday." | "I never asked you for that report. You always imagine things." |

A target should create a written record at the time of each conversation, keep a private dated log, and escalate through the code's reporting channels with facts rather than labels. "This pattern of contradictory instructions…" works better than "My boss is a gaslighter." The log should record the date, time and place; who was present; the exact words used; the document that contradicts them; and the impact on your work. The confirmation email template below is a synthesis.

```
Subject: Confirming today's instruction – [topic]
Hi [name],
To confirm our [14:00] conversation today: you asked me to [deliver X] by [Friday, 23 October].
Please let me know if I have misunderstood anything.
Thanks, [name]
```

### On exam day

Read every prompt to the end and number each requested element before writing. The textbook's 4:45 p.m. email example shows how a reply goes incomplete when the writer starts composing halfway through the request ([Business Communication for Success, 1.2](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-communication-for-success/s05-02-what-is-communication.html)). Budget revision time on purpose, since students typically give revision only 3–5% of their writing time ([KPU](https://kpu.pressbooks.pub/businesswriting/chapter/the-writing-process-in-the-workplace/)). A workable split for a 20-minute item is five minutes planning, ten drafting and five revising.

Structure every concept answer as name, define, apply to a fact in the scenario, give the limit or defence, and conclude. Finish each written answer with three quick passes: the **four C's** (point first, padding cut, numbers present, months spelled out), **clauses** (no splices or fragments), and **punctuation and citations** (US placement, page numbers on quotations).

## Conclusion

The midterm's fifteen-odd topics are best understood as one skill used in five kinds of writing: **ranking information for a reader**. Ranking the facts and leading with the one that matters most to this audience is how you write a news lead. It is also how you write a summary's first sentence, a paragraph's topic sentence, an email's opening request and minutes that lead with decisions and owned actions. Plain language's rule of "main idea before exceptions" is the same decision again. A student who practises that ranking decision carries it across all three blocks. A student who memorises five formats separately does not. This is why the plan above drills outputs rather than notes. It is also why, where authorities disagree (paraphrase length, summary order, lead length, US versus UK punctuation, US versus Uzbek burden of proof, the missing Uzbek Hofstede scores), one sentence stating which convention you follow shows the examiner understanding rather than recall.

The subtler lesson is that **attribution is necessary but never sufficient**. A citation does not rescue a patchwritten paraphrase. Credit does not license copying. Repeating a defamatory claim under "allegedly" or "police said" still republishes it, outside privileged reports of official proceedings. And in the workplace, only records made at the time, in factual language, protect someone being gaslit. In every case the protection comes from something the label cannot supply: a genuinely new structure, permission, provable truth or a dated record. The attribution only makes that protection visible. The students who earn top marks treat a citation, a "said" or a confirmation email as the final step of honest work, not a substitute for it.
