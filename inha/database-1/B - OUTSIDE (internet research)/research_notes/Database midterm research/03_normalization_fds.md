## 1. Why normalize? Redundancy, update/insertion/deletion anomalies, and good vs. bad (lossy) decompositions

### Takeaway
Storing the same fact many times (redundancy) causes three kinds of anomaly: update (modification), insertion and deletion. Normalization fixes this by splitting a relation into smaller ones. A split is only acceptable if it is **lossless** (non-additive): natural-joining the pieces must give back exactly the original relation, with no spurious tuples. Dependency preservation is desirable and may be sacrificed. Losslessness may never be sacrificed.

### Cited Findings
- Redundantly stored information "wastes storage" and "causes problems with update anomalies": insertion anomalies, deletion anomalies and modification anomalies. Source: [E&N 7e Ch. 14 slides (Purdue CS448)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- Elmasri's anomaly examples on EMP_PROJ(Emp#, Proj#, Ename, Pname, No_hours). Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
  - **Update:** renaming project P1 "from 'Billing' to 'Customer-Accounting' may cause this update to be made for all 100 employees working on project P1".
  - **Insert:** you "Cannot insert a project unless an employee is assigned to it", and conversely cannot insert an employee who is not assigned to a project.
  - **Delete:** deleting a project deletes all employees who work on it, and deleting the only employee on a project deletes the project.
- Elmasri's design guidelines. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
  - GUIDELINE 2: design schemas that do not suffer from insertion, deletion or update anomalies.
  - GUIDELINE 3: tuples should have as few NULLs as possible.
  - GUIDELINE 4: relations should satisfy the lossless join condition, and "No spurious tuples should be generated by doing a natural-join of any relations".
- Elmasri names two important properties of a decomposition. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
  - (a) Non-additivity (losslessness) of the join is "extremely important and cannot be sacrificed".
  - (b) Preservation of the FDs is "less stringent and may be sacrificed".
- Silberschatz's motivating example: combining instructor and department into inst_dept causes repeated information. With dept_name → building, budget, "because dept_name is not a candidate key, the building and budget of a department may have to be repeated. This indicates the need to decompose inst_dept". Source: [DSC 6e Ch. 8 author slides (db-book.com)](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- "Not all decompositions are good." Splitting employee(ID, name, street, city, salary) into employee1(ID, name) and employee2(name, street, city, salary) loses information: "we cannot reconstruct the original employee relation -- and so, this is a lossy decomposition". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- Silberschatz's goal: decide whether R is in "good" form. If it is not, decompose it into {R1, …, Rn} so that each piece is in good form and the decomposition is lossless-join. "Preferably, the decomposition should be dependency preserving." Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- CMU 15-445 lists the same three anomalies for a student/course/room table. Source: [CMU 15-445 F17 Lecture 04 notes](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/04-notes-functionaldependencies.pdf)
  - Update: a change to a room number means all records need to be updated.
  - Insert: it may be impossible to add a student who is not enrolled in a course.
  - Delete: deleting all students may lose the room number for a course.
- Elmasri's sample EMP_DEPT state (Fig. 14.4) shows the redundancy concretely. The Research department (Dnumber 5, Dmgr_ssn 333445555) is repeated in the tuples of Smith, Wong, Narayan and English. Headquarters (Dnumber 1) appears only in Borg's tuple. The figure notes that such joined relations "may be stored as base relations for performance reasons". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides, Fig. 14.4](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)

### Inferences
**Worked anomaly table.** This is a subset of Elmasri's EMP_DEPT sample state (Fig. 14.4), with Bdate and Address omitted.

| Ename | Ssn (PK) | Dnumber | Dname | Dmgr_ssn |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smith, John B. | 123456789 | 5 | Research | 333445555 |
| Wong, Franklin T. | 333445555 | 5 | Research | 333445555 |
| Narayan, Ramesh K. | 666884444 | 5 | Research | 333445555 |
| English, Joyce A. | 453453453 | 5 | Research | 333445555 |
| Zelaya, Alicia J. | 999887777 | 4 | Administration | 987654321 |
| Borg, James E. | 888665555 | 1 | Headquarters | 888665555 |

The FDs are Ssn → Ename, Bdate, Address, Dnumber and Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn.

- **Update (modification) anomaly.** Giving department 5 a new manager means changing 4 rows. If you miss one, the database says department 5 has two managers.
- **Insertion anomaly.** A new department 7 with no employees yet cannot be inserted without a NULL in the primary key Ssn, which violates entity integrity. Also, every new Research employee must repeat "Research, 333445555" consistently.
- **Deletion anomaly.** Deleting Borg, the only Headquarters employee, silently destroys the fact that department 1 is "Headquarters" managed by 888665555.
- **Root cause.** Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn, but Dnumber is not a key of EMP_DEPT. This is a transitive dependency on the key.
- **Fix.** Split into ED1(Ename, Ssn, Bdate, Address, Dnumber) and ED2(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn). Each department fact is then stored once and all three anomalies disappear. The split is lossless because the shared attribute Dnumber is the key of ED2.

**Lossy decomposition illustrated** (my own toy data, on the same schema as the Silberschatz slide):
- employee = {(10101, Kim, Main St, Tashkent, 75000), (20202, Kim, North St, Samarkand, 67000)}.
- employee1(ID, name) ⋈ employee2(name, street, city, salary) joins on name = 'Kim'. That gives 2 × 2 = **4 tuples**: the 2 real ones plus 2 spurious ones, (10101, Kim, North St, Samarkand, 67000) and (20202, Kim, Main St, Tashkent, 75000).
- The result has more tuples but less information: we can no longer tell who lives where. That is why it is called "lossy".
- It fails the binary lossless test because the common attribute `name` is not a superkey of either piece (name → ID does not hold).

**Rule of thumb.** A split is safe (lossless) when the shared attributes form a key (superkey) of at least one of the two pieces. Every Elmasri 2NF/3NF step does exactly this: it splits R on X → Y into (X ∪ Y) and (R − Y), and X is the key of the first piece.

## 2. Functional dependencies and keys: definitions you must be able to state precisely

### Takeaway
α → β holds on R when every legal instance obeys "same α-values ⇒ same β-values". An instance can refute an FD but can never prove one. Keys are special FDs: K is a superkey if K → R, and a candidate key is a minimal superkey. Normal forms are then stated using prime vs. nonprime attributes and full vs. partial vs. transitive dependencies.

### Cited Findings
- **FD definition.** Let α ⊆ R and β ⊆ R. Then α → β holds on R iff, for any legal r(R), whenever two tuples t1 and t2 agree on α they also agree on β: t1[α] = t2[α] ⇒ t1[β] = t2[β]. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **"Satisfies" vs. "holds".** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - If a relation r is legal under F, we say r *satisfies* F.
  - F *holds* on R if all legal relations on R satisfy F.
  - "A specific instance of a relation schema may satisfy a functional dependency even if the functional dependency does not hold on all legal instances." For example, an instance of instructor may by chance satisfy name → ID.
- **Elmasri on instances.** From an instance, "all we can conclude is that an FD may exist", but "What we can definitely conclude is – that certain FDs do not exist because there are tuples that show a violation of those dependencies". In the TEACH(Teacher, Course, Text) instance (Fig. 14.7), Text → Course may exist, while Teacher → Course, Teacher → Text and Course → Text are ruled out. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **CMU on instances and combining FDs.** "You can check if an FD is violated by an instance, but you can't prove a FD using just an instance." X → Y and X → Z can be written X → YZ, "But XY → Z is not the same as X → Z and X → Y". Source: [CMU Lecture 04](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/04-notes-functionaldependencies.pdf)
- **Trivial FDs.** An FD is trivial if it is satisfied by all instances. In general "α → β is trivial if β ⊆ α", for example ID, name → ID and name → name. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **Keys via FDs (Silberschatz).** K is a superkey for R iff K → R. K is a candidate key iff K → R and, for no α ⊂ K, α → R. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **Keys and prime attributes (Elmasri).** Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
  - A superkey S is a set such that no two tuples in any legal state agree on S.
  - A key is a superkey from which no attribute can be removed.
  - With several keys, each is a candidate key; one is "arbitrarily designated to be the primary key" and the others are secondary keys.
  - "A Prime attribute must be a member of some candidate key"; a nonprime attribute "is not a member of any candidate key".
- **Prime is defined using candidate keys, not superkeys.** Otherwise every attribute would be prime, because R itself is a superkey. Also, any superkey X determines any set Y of attributes in the schema. Source: [UTA lecture notes on normalization (C. Li)](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
- **Full vs. partial dependency (Elmasri).** A full FD Y → Z is one where removing any attribute from Y means the FD no longer holds. {SSN, PNUMBER} → HOURS is full. {SSN, PNUMBER} → ENAME "is not a full FD (it is called a partial dependency) since SSN → ENAME also holds". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Partial dependency (Silberschatz).** α → β is a partial dependency if there is a proper subset γ of α with γ → β. Source: [DSC 6e Practice Exercise 8.17 (official solutions)](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **Transitive dependency (Elmasri slides).** It is an FD X → Z "that can be derived from two FDs X → Y and Y → Z", for example SSN → DMGRSSN via DNUMBER. "we consider this a problem only if Y is not a candidate key". In EMP(SSN, Emp#, Salary), SSN → Emp# → Salary is fine because Emp# is a candidate key. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Transitive dependency (Silberschatz exercise).** Suppose α → β holds, β → α does not, A ∉ α, A ∉ β, and β → A. Then A is transitively dependent on α. 3NF is equivalent to "no nonprime A transitively dependent on a key". Source: [DSC PE 8.16 solutions](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **Testing an FD on stored data with SQL.** `select b from r group by b having count(distinct c) > 1` returns rows iff b → c does not hold on r. It can also be written as a `create assertion`, but no DBMS implements assertions. Source: [DSC PE 8.9 solutions](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf). CMU likewise notes "No major DBMS supports SQL-92 assertions": [CMU Lecture 04](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/04-notes-functionaldependencies.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Exam wording.** "F holds on R" is a schema constraint that comes from the meaning of the data. "r satisfies F" is about one instance and can be checked. From a table you may only answer "may hold" or "definitely does not hold".
- **Checking an FD on a table quickly.** Group the rows by their α-values. If any group contains two different β-values, the FD is violated.
- **Worked check (Elmasri Fig. 14.8).** R(A,B,C,D) has tuples (a1,b1,c1,d1), (a1,b2,c2,d2), (a2,b2,c2,d3), (a3,b3,c4,d3).
  - **Ruled out:** A → B (t1, t2), B → A (t2, t3), D → C (t3, t4), D → A (t3, t4).
  - **May hold (every minimal single-RHS FD not violated):** B → C, C → B, AB → D, AC → D, AD → B, AD → C, BD → A, CD → A. This list was verified with my script.
- **Partial vs. transitive at a glance:**
  - *Partial:* a nonprime attribute depends on **part of** a candidate key. This fails "the whole key".
  - *Transitive:* a nonprime attribute depends on a set that is **not a (super)key**, usually another nonprime attribute. This fails "nothing but the key".

### Gaps
- I could not verify the textbook's exact formal definition of transitive dependency from a free source. Some editions phrase it with the middle set being "neither a candidate key nor a subset of any key". The slides only give the simplified "X → Y → Z" form plus the "only if Y is not a candidate key" note. For exam safety, use the general 3NF test in §4, which is unambiguous.

## 3. First normal form (1NF): atomic domains, composite and multivalued attributes, nested relations, and the three techniques

### Takeaway
1NF means every attribute value is atomic: no composite attributes, no multivalued (set) attributes and no nested relations.
- Composite attributes are flattened into their simple components.
- A multivalued attribute is removed with one of three techniques. The preferred technique moves it into its own relation together with the owner's key, as in COMPANY's DEPT_LOCATIONS.
- A nested relation is split off by propagating the primary key.

### Cited Findings
- **Silberschatz's definition.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - "Domain is atomic if its elements are considered to be indivisible units."
  - Non-atomic examples are a set of names, composite attributes, and "Identification numbers like CS101 that can be broken up into parts".
  - "A relational schema R is in first normal form if the domains of all attributes of R are atomic."
  - Non-atomic values complicate storage and encourage redundant storage.
- "Atomicity is actually a property of how the elements of the domain are used". Roll numbers like CS0012 or EE1127 become non-atomic if the first two characters are extracted to find the department. Doing so "is a bad idea: leads to encoding of information in application program rather than in the database". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **Elmasri's definition.** 1NF "Disallows composite attributes, multivalued attributes, nested relations; attributes whose values for an individual tuple are non-atomic". It is "Considered to be part of the definition of a relation", and "Most RDBMSs allow only those relations to be defined that are in First Normal Form". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Elmasri Fig. 14.9.** DEPARTMENT(Dname, Dnumber, Dmgr_ssn, Dlocations) is "A relation schema that is not in 1NF", with sample tuple (Research, 5, 333445555, {Bellaire, Sugarland, Houston}). Part (c) is the "1NF version of the same relation with redundancy", with one tuple per (department, location). Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **The three techniques**, as summarized in an E&N-based course (wording checked against the raw page). Source: [Loyola Univ. Chicago COMP 353/453 notes, week 10](https://pld.cs.luc.edu/courses/353/now/notes/10.html)
  1. "dealing with the multi-valued location attribute of DEPARTMENT meant that we had to create a new table LOCATIONS".
  2. "Alternative ways … would be making ⟨dnumber, location⟩ the primary key".
  3. "or supplying a fixed number of location columns loc1, loc2, loc3, loc4. For the latter approach, we must know in advance how many locations we will need; this method also introduces NULL values".
  - The same notes add that "Composite attributes were handled by making each of their components a separate attribute".
- **Nested relations (Elmasri Fig. 14.10).** EMP_PROJ(Ssn, Ename, PROJS(Pnumber, Hours)) is normalized into EMP_PROJ1(Ssn, Ename) and EMP_PROJ2(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours) "by propagating the primary key". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **ER-to-relational mapping, Step 6 (Elmasri).** Source: [E&N 7e Ch. 9 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter09.pdf)
  - "For each multivalued attribute A, create a new relation R" holding A plus the owner's primary key K as a foreign key. The primary key of R is the combination of A and K.
  - If the multivalued attribute is composite, include its simple components. The example is DEPT_LOCATIONS(Dnumber, Dlocation).
  - Entity mapping uses "all the simple attributes", that is, the simple components of composite attributes.
- **The resulting COMPANY schema (Fig. 5.5)** has six relations. Source: [E&N 7e Ch. 5 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter05.pdf)
  - EMPLOYEE(Fname, Minit, Lname, Ssn, Bdate, Address, Sex, Salary, Super_ssn, Dno). The composite name has become Fname, Minit, Lname.
  - DEPARTMENT(Dname, Dnumber, Mgr_ssn, Mgr_start_date)
  - DEPT_LOCATIONS(Dnumber, Dlocation)
  - PROJECT(Pname, Pnumber, Plocation, Dnum)
  - WORKS_ON(Essn, Pno, Hours)
  - DEPENDENT(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship)

### Inferences
**Comparing the three 1NF techniques** on DEPARTMENT with a multivalued Dlocations:

| Technique | Result | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Separate relation (preferred) | DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn) + DEPT_LOCATIONS(Dnumber, Dlocation), PK {Dnumber, Dlocation}, Dnumber is an FK | No redundancy, no NULLs, unlimited locations. The result is already BCNF. | One extra join |
| 2. Expand the key | DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn, Dlocation), PK {Dnumber, Dlocation} | One table | Dname and Dmgr_ssn are repeated per location. **The relation is not in 2NF**, because Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn is a partial dependency on the key (script-verified). 2NF normalization turns it back into technique 1. |
| 3. Fixed columns | DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn, Dlocation1, Dlocation2, Dlocation3) | One table, key unchanged | You must know the maximum number of locations; NULLs appear; "departments in Houston" needs an OR over 3 columns |

**Composite attributes.** Replace them by their components (Name → Fname, Minit, Lname). Whether Address must be split depends on use: if you query by city, split it; if it is only ever used as a whole string, it can count as atomic (DSC: atomicity depends on how the domain is used).

**Worked 1NF example (COMPANY-style).** Start with EMP_INFO(Ssn, Name(Fname, Minit, Lname), Bdate, Dno, Phones{…}, Dependents{(Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship)}).
1. **Composite attribute:** replace Name by Fname, Minit, Lname.
2. **Multivalued attribute (technique 1):** move Phones into EMP_PHONE(Ssn, Phone), with PK {Ssn, Phone} and FK Ssn → EMPLOYEE.
3. **Nested relation:** move Dependents into DEPENDENT(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship), with PK {Essn, Dependent_name}. The primary key is propagated as in Fig. 14.10, and the result is exactly COMPANY's DEPENDENT relation.
4. **Result:** EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Minit, Lname, Bdate, Dno), EMP_PHONE(Ssn, Phone) and DEPENDENT(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship). All three are in 1NF, and with their natural FDs (key → rest) they are also in BCNF (script-verified).

Had technique 2 been used for the phones, the result EMP(Ssn, Phone, Fname, Lname, Dno) with PK {Ssn, Phone} would be in 1NF only. Ssn → Fname, Lname, Dno is a partial dependency (script-verified).

**Exam note.** Questions that give "R and F" normally assume 1NF already holds. If a value is a set, list, repeating group or composite, fix 1NF before anything else.

## 4. 2NF and 3NF the Elmasri way: primary-key definitions, general (candidate-key) definitions, and the normalization recipe (CORE)

### Takeaway
2NF removes **partial** dependencies: a nonprime attribute depending on part of a key. 3NF removes **transitive** dependencies: a nonprime attribute depending on a set that is not a key.
- Elmasri first states both relative to the primary key, then gives general definitions relative to every candidate key.
- The general 3NF test is: for every nontrivial X → A, either X is a superkey or A is prime. This is the same condition as Silberschatz's 3NF.
- The mechanical process is: find all keys → split off partial dependencies → split off transitive dependencies → check losslessness and dependency preservation.

### Cited Findings
- **2NF, primary-key version.** "A relation schema R is in second normal form (2NF) if every non-prime attribute A in R is fully functionally dependent on the primary key", and "R can be decomposed into 2NF relations via the process of 2NF normalization or 'second normalization'". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **3NF, primary-key version.** "A relation schema R is in third normal form (3NF) if it is in 2NF and no non-prime attribute A in R is transitively dependent on the primary key". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Informal versions.** 1NF: "All attributes depend on the key". 2NF: "All attributes depend on the whole key". 3NF: "All attributes depend on nothing but the key". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Why general definitions are needed.** The earlier definitions "consider the primary key only". The general ones "take into account relations with multiple candidate keys", and "Any attribute involved in a candidate key is a prime attribute". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **General 2NF.** "every non-prime attribute A in R is fully functionally dependent on every key of R". In LOTS, "the FD County_name → Tax_rate violates 2NF", so LOTS becomes LOTS1(Property_id#, County_name, Lot#, Area, Price) + LOTS2(County_name, Tax_rate). Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **General 3NF.** "whenever a FD X → A holds in R, then either: (a) X is a superkey of R, or (b) A is a prime attribute of R". LOTS1 violates 3NF "because Area → Price ; and Area is not a superkey in LOTS1". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **Interpreting the general 3NF.** Condition (a) catches two kinds of violation: a prime attribute determining a nonprime one (a 2NF violation) and a nonprime attribute determining a nonprime one (a transitive dependency). Elmasri's "ALTERNATIVE DEFINITION": every nonprime attribute is "fully functionally dependent on every key of R" and "non-transitively dependent on every key of R". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **BCNF (Elmasri).** "whenever an FD X → A holds in R, then X is a superkey of R". Every 2NF relation is in 1NF, every 3NF relation is in 2NF and every BCNF relation is in 3NF. "There exist relations that are in 3NF but not in BCNF". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **3NF (Silberschatz).** R is in 3NF if, for every α → β in F⁺, at least one of these holds: (1) α → β is trivial (β ⊆ α); (2) α is a superkey for R; (3) "Each attribute A in β – α is contained in a candidate key for R", where "each attribute may be in a different candidate key". BCNF implies 3NF, and the third condition "is a minimal relaxation of BCNF to ensure dependency preservation". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **2NF (Silberschatz, in an exercise).** R is in 2NF if each attribute either appears in a candidate key or is not partially dependent on a candidate key. The solution proves that every 3NF schema is in 2NF. Source: [DSC PE 8.17 solutions](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **Equivalent statements of the normal forms.** Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
  - 2NF: there is no FD X → y with y nonprime and X a proper subset of a candidate key.
  - 3NF: for any FD X → y, either y is prime or X is a superkey.
  - BCNF: for any FD X → y, X is a superkey.
  - The notes add that "The lower one 2NF is often insufficient".
- **A rigor caveat about the textbook's summary table.** Its 3NF test, "no nonkey attribute functionally determined by another nonkey attribute (or by a set of nonkey attributes)", is "not fully rigorous". It should say "…or by a set of attributes that is not a superkey". As written it misses a nonkey attribute determined by a mixed (prime + nonkey) set that is not a superkey. Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
- **Loyola's wording.** Source: [Loyola notes](https://pld.cs.luc.edu/courses/353/now/notes/10.html)
  - 2NF: "every nonprime attribute A (that is an attribute not a member of any key) is functionally dependent on K (ie K⟶A), but that this fails for any proper subset of K".
  - "if a relation has a single-attribute primary key, as does EMP_DEPT, then 2NF is automatic. (Actually, the general definition of 2NF requires this for every candidate key…)".
  - 3NF: "the relation is in 2NF and also there is no dependency X⟶A for nonprime attribute A and for attribute set X that does not contain a candidate key (ie X is not a superkey)".
- **In practice.** Designers "need not normalize to the highest possible normal form (usually up to 3NF and BCNF. 4NF rarely used in practice.)". Denormalization means storing the join of higher-normal-form relations as a base relation. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)

### Inferences
**The normalization recipe (Elmasri-style, mechanical).**

1. **Read the FDs.** Split right-hand sides using the decomposition rule (X → YZ becomes X → Y and X → Z). Drop trivial FDs.
2. **Find keys and prime attributes.** Find **all** candidate keys using closures and the L/R/B shortcut in §7. Mark each attribute prime or nonprime. Note which key is the primary key.
3. **Achieve 1NF.** Flatten composite attributes. Remove multivalued attributes and nested relations (§3, technique 1).
4. **Test 2NF.** For every proper subset S of every candidate key (in the PK-only version, of the primary key), compute S⁺. If S⁺ contains a nonprime attribute, that is a partial dependency and R is not in 2NF. Only composite keys can cause this. If every candidate key is a single attribute, R is automatically in 2NF.
5. **Decompose to 2NF.** For each minimal such S:
   - Create R_S = S ∪ (the nonprime attributes in S⁺).
   - Delete those nonprime attributes from the original relation, but keep S there; it becomes an FK to R_S.
   - The original relation keeps the full key plus the attributes fully dependent on it.
   - Carry along the nonprime attributes that depend on S *transitively*. For example, with Ssn → Dno → Dname, Dname moves with Ssn; the 3NF step will split it off later.
   - Do **not** move prime attributes out (see trap P20).
   - Always keep a relation containing the original key, even an "all-key" one (see P9).
6. **Test 3NF (per relation).** For every FD X → A inside a relation, with A nonprime: if X is not a superkey of that relation, it is a transitive dependency and the relation is not in 3NF.
7. **Decompose to 3NF.** Create R_X = X ∪ (the nonprime attributes X determines within the relation). Delete those attributes from the original and keep X there as an FK. Repeat until no violation is left.
8. **Check the result:**
   - Each R_X has X as its key.
   - The decomposition is lossless: every split was made on some X → Y, and (X ∪ Y) ∩ (R − Y) = X with X → X ∪ Y, so the binary rule (§8) applies.
   - It is dependency-preserving: every original FD lies inside one relation, or can be derived from FDs that do.
   - State the final highest normal form of every relation.

**Quick-reference table** (A is a nonprime attribute):

| NF | Violation pattern | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1NF | A non-atomic value (set, composite or nested) | Flatten, or make a new relation that includes the owner's key |
| 2NF | (proper part of a candidate key) → A | Split into (part, its nonprime dependents) and the rest |
| 3NF | (non-superkey X) → A | Split into (X, its nonprime dependents) and the rest |
| BCNF (beyond class) | (non-superkey X) → any attribute, even a prime one | Split into X⁺ ∩ R and R − (X⁺ − X) |

**PK-only vs. general definitions: what to do on the exam.**
- If R has only one candidate key, the two versions coincide.
- If the FDs reveal further candidate keys, say so and apply the general definitions, which are stricter for 2NF. LOTS is "in 2NF with respect to its primary key Property_id#" but violates general 2NF (§5, W3).
- If you are unsure which version is expected, show both and label them.

**Why 2NF is not enough.** EMP_DEPT is in 2NF because its key is a single attribute, yet it has all three anomalies from §1. The cause is a transitive dependency, which only 3NF removes.

**Elmasri ≡ Silberschatz for 3NF.** For a single-attribute RHS A ∉ X, Silberschatz's condition "A is contained in some candidate key" is exactly Elmasri's "A is prime". So the general 3NF test is the same in both books.

### Inferences
All steps below follow the §4 recipe and were verified by script (keys, normal forms, chase test, dependency preservation).

**W1. EMP_PROJ (pure 2NF violation)**
- **Schema:** R = EMP_PROJ(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours, Ename, Pname, Plocation). F = {Ssn Pnumber → Hours; Ssn → Ename; Pnumber → Pname, Plocation}.
- **Keys:** Ssn and Pnumber never appear on a right-hand side, so both must be in every key. (Ssn Pnumber)⁺ = R, so the **only** candidate key is {Ssn, Pnumber}, which is also the PK. Prime: Ssn, Pnumber. Nonprime: Hours, Ename, Pname, Plocation.
- **1NF:** all values are atomic. ✔
- **2NF test:** Ssn⁺ = {Ssn, Ename}, so Ename is partially dependent. Pnumber⁺ = {Pnumber, Pname, Plocation}, so Pname and Plocation are partially dependent. **Not 2NF.** Highest NF: 1NF.
- **2NF decomposition:** EP2(Ssn, Ename), EP3(Pnumber, Pname, Plocation), and what remains, EP1(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours).
- **3NF test:** each relation has one key and every FD's left side is that key, so all three are in 3NF (in fact BCNF).
- **Check:** EP1 ∩ EP2 = Ssn, which is the key of EP2, so that join is lossless. The result ∩ EP3 = Pnumber, the key of EP3, so the full join is lossless. Each FD lies inside one relation, so dependencies are preserved. ✔ (These are exactly Elmasri's EP1/EP2/EP3.)

**W2. EMP_DEPT (pure 3NF violation)**
- **Schema:** R = EMP_DEPT(Ename, Ssn, Bdate, Address, Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn). F = {Ssn → Ename, Bdate, Address, Dnumber; Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn}.
- **Keys:** Ssn is L-only and Ssn⁺ = R, so the only key is Ssn.
- **2NF:** the key is a single attribute, so no partial dependency is possible. ✔ 2NF.
- **3NF:** Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn. Dnumber is not a superkey (Dnumber⁺ = {Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn}) and the right-hand attributes are nonprime. So Ssn → Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn is a transitive dependency. **Not 3NF.** Highest NF: 2NF.
- **3NF decomposition:** ED2(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn) and ED1(Ename, Ssn, Bdate, Address, Dnumber). Both are BCNF.
- **Check:** ED1 ∩ ED2 = Dnumber, the key of ED2, so the split is lossless. Both FDs are kept. ✔

**W3. LOTS (two candidate keys: why the general definition matters)**
- **Schema:** R = LOTS(Property_id#, County_name, Lot#, Area, Price, Tax_rate) with FD1–FD4 as in Cited Findings.
- **Keys:** the script confirms exactly two candidate keys, Property_id# and {County_name, Lot#}. The PK is Property_id#. Prime: Property_id#, County_name, Lot#. Nonprime: Area, Price, Tax_rate.
- **PK-only 2NF test:** the PK is a single attribute, so there is no partial dependency on the PK. LOTS "passes" the PK-only test.
- **General 2NF test:** County_name ⊂ {County_name, Lot#} and County_name → Tax_rate, with Tax_rate nonprime. That is a partial dependency on a candidate key, so LOTS is **not 2NF**. Highest NF: 1NF.
- **2NF decomposition:** LOTS2(County_name, Tax_rate) and LOTS1(Property_id#, County_name, Lot#, Area, Price).
- **3NF test on LOTS1:** Area → Price, where Area is not a superkey of LOTS1 and Price is nonprime. **Not 3NF.** LOTS1 is 2NF only (script-verified).
- **3NF decomposition:** LOTS1A(Property_id#, County_name, Lot#, Area), LOTS1B(Area, Price) and LOTS2(County_name, Tax_rate). Under FD1–FD4 all three are in BCNF, and the decomposition is lossless and dependency-preserving.
- **BCNF extension (beyond class), adding FD5 Area → County_name:**
  - LOTS1A now has candidate keys Property_id#, {County_name, Lot#} **and {Area, Lot#}**. This third key is my script-verified addition; the slide does not list it.
  - Every attribute of LOTS1A is now prime, so LOTS1A is still in 3NF.
  - Area → County_name has a non-superkey left side, so LOTS1A is **not BCNF**.
  - BCNF split: LOTS1AY(Area, County_name) and LOTS1AX(Property_id#, Area, Lot#). This is lossless (Area is the key of LOTS1AY), but FD2 {County_name, Lot#} → Property_id#, Area is lost, exactly as Elmasri's caption says.

**W4. The COMPANY "universal" relation, normalized back into the COMPANY schema**
- **Schema:** R = EMP_PROJ_DEPT(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours, Ename, Bdate, Dno, Dname, Mgr_ssn, Pname, Plocation, Dnum).
- **FDs** (meanings follow the COMPANY schema):
  - Ssn → Ename, Bdate, Dno
  - Dno → Dname, Mgr_ssn
  - Pnumber → Pname, Plocation, Dnum
  - Ssn Pnumber → Hours
- **Keys:** Ssn and Pnumber are L-only, and (Ssn Pnumber)⁺ = R, so the only key is {Ssn, Pnumber}. Every other attribute is nonprime.
- **2NF test:**
  - Ssn⁺ = {Ssn, Ename, Bdate, Dno, Dname, Mgr_ssn}. Note that Dname and Mgr_ssn come in transitively, via Dno.
  - Pnumber⁺ = {Pnumber, Pname, Plocation, Dnum}.
  - Both are partial dependencies on the key, so R is **not 2NF**.
- **2NF decomposition:**
  - WORKS_ON(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours): BCNF.
  - EMP_DEPT(Ssn, Ename, Bdate, Dno, Dname, Mgr_ssn). Dname and Mgr_ssn are carried along with Ssn. This relation is 2NF only.
  - PROJECT(Pnumber, Pname, Plocation, Dnum): BCNF.
- **3NF:** in EMP_DEPT, Dno → Dname, Mgr_ssn has a non-key left side, so it is a transitive dependency. Split it into EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Ename, Bdate, Dno) and DEPARTMENT(Dno, Dname, Mgr_ssn).
- **Final result:** WORKS_ON, EMPLOYEE, DEPARTMENT and PROJECT. This is the COMPANY design (Fig. 14.1 / 5.5) up to attribute names, and all four relations are in BCNF.
- **Checks:** the decomposition is lossless (chase-verified) and dependency-preserving.
- **Variant:** add Mgr_ssn → Dno (each manager runs exactly one department). DEPARTMENT then has two candidate keys, Dno and Mgr_ssn, and is **still BCNF**, because a dependency through a candidate key is not a violation. EMP_DEPT, however, still violates 3NF.

**W5. ENROLL (student/course/advisor; same pattern as W4)**
- **Schema:** R = ENROLL(StuID, CourseNo, StuName, Major, Advisor, AdvOffice, CourseTitle, Grade).
- **FDs:** StuID → StuName, Major, Advisor; Advisor → AdvOffice; CourseNo → CourseTitle; StuID CourseNo → Grade.
- **Keys:** the only key is {StuID, CourseNo}; both attributes are L-only and their closure is R.
- **2NF test:** StuID⁺ = {StuID, StuName, Major, Advisor, AdvOffice}, where AdvOffice is implied via Advisor. CourseNo⁺ = {CourseNo, CourseTitle}. Both are partial, so R is in 1NF only.
- **2NF decomposition:** GRADE(StuID, CourseNo, Grade), STUDENT(StuID, StuName, Major, Advisor, AdvOffice) and COURSE(CourseNo, CourseTitle).
- **3NF:** in STUDENT, Advisor → AdvOffice has a non-key left side, so split it into STUDENT(StuID, StuName, Major, Advisor) and ADVISOR(Advisor, AdvOffice).
- **Final result:** 4 relations, all BCNF, lossless and dependency-preserving (verified).

**W6. Why the finished COMPANY schema is in 3NF (in fact BCNF)**
Check each relation under its natural FDs, where the key determines everything else:
- EMPLOYEE(…, Ssn, …, Super_ssn, Dno): Ssn → all.
- DEPARTMENT(Dname, Dnumber, Mgr_ssn, Mgr_start_date): Dnumber → all. If Dname or Mgr_ssn is also unique, it is just another candidate key.
- DEPT_LOCATIONS(Dnumber, Dlocation): all attributes form the key.
- PROJECT(Pname, Pnumber, Plocation, Dnum): Pnumber → all.
- WORKS_ON(Essn, Pno, Hours): {Essn, Pno} → Hours.
- DEPENDENT(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship): {Essn, Dependent_name} → the rest.

Every nontrivial FD has a key on its left side, so every relation is in BCNF and therefore 3NF. The foreign keys (Dno, Super_ssn, Mgr_ssn, Dnum, Essn, Pno) determine nothing **inside** their own relation, so they cause no transitive dependency (see trap 5 in §11).

## 6. "What is the highest normal form of R given F?": a mechanical procedure

### Takeaway
1. Find all candidate keys first.
2. Then test from the top:
   - **BCNF:** every nontrivial given FD has a superkey on its left side.
   - **3NF:** every BCNF-violating FD has only prime attributes on its right side.
   - **2NF:** no nonprime attribute lies in S⁺ for any proper subset S of any candidate key.
   - Otherwise the answer is 1NF.
3. For R itself, the given F is enough for the BCNF and 3NF tests. For 2NF you must use closures, not just the listed FDs. When testing a decomposed piece, you must use the FDs projected from F⁺ onto it.

### Cited Findings
- "Procedure 3 (How to determine if a relation is in 2NF, 3NF, or BCNF?) Find all candidate keys based on given FDs. Then check if any FD violates the corresponding normal form". Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
- **BCNF test (Silberschatz).** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - For a nontrivial α → β, compute α⁺ and check that it contains all of R.
  - Simplified test: for R itself, it suffices to check only the dependencies in the given set F.
  - "However, simplified test using only F is incorrect when testing a relation in a decomposition of R." The slide's example: R = (A,B,C,D,E), F = {A → B, BC → D}. R2 = (A,C,D,E) looks fine using F, but AC → D ∈ F⁺ shows that R2 is not in BCNF.
- **Testing a decomposed relation Ri.** Either test Ri against the restriction of F⁺ to Ri, or, for every α ⊆ Ri, check that α⁺ contains either no attribute of Ri − α or all of Ri. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **3NF test (Silberschatz).** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - "Need to check only FDs in F, need not check all FDs in F+."
  - If α is not a superkey, verify that each attribute in β is contained in a candidate key.
  - Testing 3NF "has been shown to be NP-hard", because it requires the candidate keys.
- "Any relation schema with two attributes is in BCNF." Source: [UTA notes, Proposition 1](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)

### Inferences
**Procedure** (write these steps down on the exam):
1. **Keys.** Find all candidate keys (§7 L/R/B method). List the prime and nonprime attributes.
2. **Canonical form.** Split right-hand sides so each FD has one attribute on the right. Drop trivial FDs.
3. **BCNF.** For each X → A, compute X⁺. If X⁺ = R for every FD, R is in **BCNF**: stop.
4. **3NF.** For each FD that failed step 3, is A prime? If yes for all of them, R is in **3NF**: stop.
5. **2NF.** For each candidate key K and each proper subset S ⊂ K, compute S⁺. If some nonprime A ∈ S⁺, R is in **1NF** only. Otherwise R is in **2NF**.
   - Do not skip computing the closures. A partial dependency can be *implied* rather than listed (trap P20).

**Shortcuts:**
- If every attribute is prime, R is at least in 3NF.
- If every candidate key is a single attribute, R is at least in 2NF.
- Any 2-attribute relation is in BCNF.
- If every nontrivial FD has a key on its left side, R is in BCNF.

**Mini-examples (all script-verified):**

| R; F | Candidate keys | Highest NF | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| ABCD; AB→C, B→D | AB | 1NF | B→D is partial (D nonprime) |
| ABCD; A→B, A→C, C→D | A | 2NF | single-attribute key; C→D is transitive |
| ABCDE; A→BC, CD→E, B→D, E→A | A, E, BC, CD | 3NF | all attributes prime; B→D has a non-superkey LHS, so not BCNF |
| SCI; SC→I, I→C | SC, SI | 3NF | I→C with C prime |
| ABCD; AB→C, C→D, D→A | AB, BC, BD | 3NF | all prime; C→D and D→A violate BCNF |
| ABCDEF; AB→C, C→D, D→A, BE→F | ABE, BCE, BDE | 1NF | BE→F is partial on every key |
| ABCD; A→C, AC→D, BC→A | AB, BC | 1NF | the *implied* A→D is partial (A⁺ = ACD) |
| EP1(Ssn, Pnumber, Hours) | {Ssn, Pnumber} | BCNF | the only FD has the key on its left |

### Gaps
- None of substance. Exam instructions may override defaults: for example, some instructors expect only the PK-based 2NF test. Follow your instructor's convention if stated.

## 7. Beyond class (shorter): Armstrong's axioms, F⁺, α⁺, finding all candidate keys, canonical cover, equivalence

### Takeaway
- Armstrong's axioms (reflexivity, augmentation, transitivity) are sound and complete. Union, decomposition and pseudotransitivity follow from them.
- In practice everything is done with the attribute-closure algorithm α⁺. It tests superkeys (α⁺ = R), tests whether an FD is implied (β ⊆ α⁺), finds all keys (with the left/right/both shortcut) and computes the canonical cover Fc by removing extraneous attributes.
- Terminology differs between sources: Silberschatz's canonical cover merges FDs with the same left side, while Elmasri's minimal cover uses single-attribute right sides.

### Cited Findings
- **Armstrong's axioms.** Reflexivity: if β ⊆ α then α → β. Augmentation: if α → β then γα → γβ. Transitivity: if α → β and β → γ then α → γ. They are "sound (generate only functional dependencies that actually hold), and complete (generate all functional dependencies that hold)". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt). Elmasri states the same rules as IR1–IR3: [E&N 7e Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **Additional rules.** Union: α → β and α → γ ⇒ α → βγ. Decomposition: α → βγ ⇒ α → β and α → γ. Pseudotransitivity: α → β and γβ → δ ⇒ αγ → δ. "The above rules can be inferred from Armstrong's axioms." Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt). The official solutions prove union and pseudotransitivity using augmentation and transitivity: [DSC PE 8.4–8.5](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **F⁺** is "The set of all functional dependencies logically implied by F". In the example R = (A,B,C,G,H,I), F = {A → B, A → C, CG → H, CG → I, B → H}, F⁺ contains: Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - A → H (by transitivity);
  - AG → I (augment A → C with G, then apply transitivity);
  - CG → HI (by augmentations, then transitivity).
- **The α⁺ algorithm:** `result := α; while (changes to result) do for each β → γ in F do if β ⊆ result then result := result ∪ γ`. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Trace for (AG)⁺: AG → ABCG → ABCGH → ABCGHI.
  - The slide then asks whether AG is a superkey, and whether A or G alone is.
  - Elmasri gives the same procedure as Algorithm 15.1: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **Uses of α⁺.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Testing for a superkey: α is a superkey iff α⁺ contains all attributes of R.
  - Testing an FD: "To check if a functional dependency α → β holds (or, in other words, is in F+), just check if β ⊆ α+". This is "a simple and cheap test, and very useful".
  - Computing F⁺: for each γ ⊆ R, find γ⁺ and output γ → S for each S ⊆ γ⁺.
- **Elmasri's CLASS example.** {Classid}⁺ is the whole relation, so Classid is a key. {Course#}⁺ = {Course#, Credit_hrs}. {Course#, Instr_name}⁺ = {Course#, Credit_hrs, Text, Publisher, Classroom, Capacity}. Source: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **Elmasri Algorithm 15.2a (find one key).** Set K := R. For each attribute A in K, if (K − A)⁺ contains all attributes of R, set K := K − {A}. Source: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **Key-finding shortcut and worked examples.** Attributes that appear on no right-hand side "cannot be derived from any other attributes. Hence any candidate key must contain" them. Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
  - R(a,b,c,d,e) with a → bc, cd → e, b → d, e → a has keys a, e, bc, cd. The official solution for the same F gives the same keys: [DSC PE 8.6](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
  - R(a,b,c,d,e) with ab → c, cd → e, de → b has keys abd, acd, ade.
  - Once a and e are known to be keys, any multi-attribute key "must not contain a or e".
- **Published algorithm for all candidate keys.** "An Efficient Algorithm to Compute the Candidate Keys of a Relational Database Schema" (Saiedian & Spencer, The Computer Journal, 1996) exploits the arrangement of attributes in the FDs to find all keys. This is from the abstract-level record only; I could not open the full text. Source: [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220458258_An_Efficient_Algorithm_to_Compute_the_Candidate_Keys_of_a_Relational_Database_Schema)
- **Canonical cover (Silberschatz).** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Fc must satisfy four conditions: F logically implies Fc; Fc logically implies F; no FD in Fc contains an extraneous attribute; each left side in Fc is unique.
  - Algorithm: `repeat` { use the union rule to merge α₁ → β₁ and α₁ → β₂; find an FD α → β with an extraneous attribute in α or β (tested against Fc, not F) and delete it } `until F does not change`. The union rule may become applicable again after deletions.
- **Extraneous attributes.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - A ∈ α is extraneous if F logically implies (F − {α → β}) ∪ {(α − A) → β}. **Test:** compute ({α} − A)⁺ using F and check that it contains β.
  - A ∈ β is extraneous if (F − {α → β}) ∪ {α → (β − A)} logically implies F. **Test:** compute α⁺ under F′ = (F − {α → β}) ∪ {α → (β − A)} and check that it contains A.
  - Example 1: in {A → C, AB → C}, B is extraneous in AB → C.
  - Example 2: in {A → C, AB → CD}, C is extraneous in AB → CD.
- **Worked canonical cover (slide).** For F = {A → BC, B → C, A → B, AB → C}: Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  1. Combine A → BC and A → B into A → BC.
  2. A is extraneous in AB → C, because B → C is already present.
  3. C is extraneous in A → BC, because A → B and B → C give A → C.
  4. Result: Fc = {A → B, B → C}.
- **Canonical cover of the PE 8.6 set.** For F = {A → BC, CD → E, B → D, E → A}, every left side is unique and no attribute is extraneous, so Fc = F. Source: [DSC PE 8.7](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **Elmasri's minimal cover.** Source: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
  - A minimal set has (1) a single attribute on every right-hand side, (2) no FD that can be removed while keeping equivalence, and (3) no X → A that can be replaced by Y → A with Y ⊂ X.
  - Algorithm 15.2: split the right-hand sides, then remove extraneous left-side attributes, then remove redundant FDs.
  - Example: E = {B → A, D → A, AB → D} gives {B → D, D → A}.

- **Terminology varies across courses.** CMU's "canonical cover" requires "The RHS of every FD is a single attribute": [CMU Lecture 04](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/04-notes-functionaldependencies.pdf). UVA's Fall 2026 slides present Silberschatz's union-based canonical cover and separately define a "minimal basis" with "singleton right sides": [UVA CS 4750 F26 slides](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~up3f/cs4750/slides/4750meet10-11-3NF-BCNF.pdf)
- **Equivalence.** F and G are equivalent if every FD in F can be inferred from G and every FD in G can be inferred from F (F⁺ = G⁺). "F covers G" if every FD in G can be inferred from F. Source: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **A university solution.** R(A,B,C,D,E) with F = {AB → D, AC → E, A → B, BC → E, A → E} has canonical cover {A → BDE, BC → E}. Source: [Purdue CS44800 F21 Assignment 2 solutions](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/clifton/cs44800/Sol2.pdf)

### Inferences
**Finding ALL candidate keys: the L / R / B (+ N) method.**
1. Classify every attribute:
   - **L:** appears only on left sides.
   - **R:** appears only on right sides.
   - **B:** appears on both sides.
   - **N:** appears in no FD.
2. **Core = L ∪ N.** These must be in every key, because nothing derives them. **R attributes are in no key.** Proof sketch: if A never appears on a left side, removing it from a key K changes nothing else in the closure computation. Some FD still derives A from attributes in (K − A)⁺, so K − A would also be a superkey, contradicting minimality.
3. Compute Core⁺. If it equals R, Core is the **only** candidate key.
4. Otherwise try Core plus 1 attribute from B, then Core plus 2, and so on. Skip any superset of a key you have already found. Stop when no candidate of the current size is new.
5. Example, R(A,B,C,D,E) with F = {A → B, BC → E, ED → A}:
   - L = {C, D}, B = {A, B, E}, R = ∅.
   - (CD)⁺ = CD, so CD alone is not a key.
   - (ACD)⁺ = ABCDE ✔; (BCD)⁺ = BCDE + A = ABCDE ✔; (CDE)⁺ = ACDE + B = ABCDE ✔.
   - **Keys: ACD, BCD, CDE** (script-verified).

**Closure trace format** (use this on exams so partial credit is visible):
- (AG)⁺: start {A, G}.
- A → B gives {A, B, G}. A → C gives {A, B, C, G}. CG → H gives {A, B, C, G, H}. CG → I gives {A, B, C, G, H, I}.
- No change on a further pass, so the answer is ABCGHI.

**Canonical cover, exam recipe:**
1. Split the right-hand sides (optional, but it makes the tests clearer).
2. For each FD with a multi-attribute left side, test each left attribute A: if β ⊆ (α − A)⁺ under the current set, delete A.
3. For each right-side attribute A, delete it from the FD, compute α⁺ under the remaining set, and if A is still in α⁺, it is extraneous (redundant).
4. Merge FDs with the same left side (union rule).
5. Repeat until nothing changes.

**Worked trace (Purdue F):**
- AB → D: A⁺ = ABDE ⊇ D, so B is extraneous and the FD becomes A → D.
- AC → E: A⁺ ⊇ E, so C is extraneous and the FD becomes A → E, which duplicates the given A → E.
- BC → E: B⁺ = B and C⁺ = C, and without this FD (BC)⁺ = BC. So nothing in it is extraneous.
- **Fc = {A → BDE, BC → E}.**
- The key is AC: A and C are L-only, and (AC)⁺ = ABCDE. Script-verified.

**Equivalence recipe.** To test F ≡ G: for every α → β in G, check β ⊆ α⁺ computed under F. Then do the reverse check.

## 8. Beyond class (shorter): BCNF, lossless join, dependency preservation, BCNF decomposition vs. 3NF synthesis, and 4NF

### Takeaway
- BCNF requires every nontrivial FD to have a superkey on its left side.
- The BCNF decomposition algorithm always gives a lossless decomposition, but it may lose dependencies (JK → L, L → K is the classic case), and its result depends on which violation you pick.
- The 3NF synthesis algorithm (from Fc, plus a key relation if needed) is always lossless **and** dependency-preserving, at the cost of possible redundancy.
- Lossless test for two pieces: (R1 ∩ R2) → R1 or (R1 ∩ R2) → R2 must be in F⁺.

### Cited Findings
- **BCNF definition (Silberschatz).** "A relation schema R is in BCNF with respect to a set F of functional dependencies if for all functional dependencies in F+ of the form α → β where α ⊆ R and β ⊆ R, at least one of the following holds": α → β is trivial, or α is a superkey for R. For example, instr_dept is not in BCNF "because dept_name → building, budget holds on instr_dept, but dept_name is not a superkey". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **The split step.** For a violating α → β, decompose R into (α ∪ β) and (R − (β − α)). For example, inst_dept becomes (dept_name, building, budget) and (ID, name, salary, dept_name). Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **BCNF algorithm.** `result := {R}; while some Ri ∈ result is not in BCNF: pick a nontrivial α → β holding on Ri with α → Ri ∉ F⁺ and α ∩ β = ∅; result := (result − Ri) ∪ (Ri − β) ∪ (α, β)`. "each Ri is in BCNF, and decomposition is lossless-join". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Example: R(A,B,C), F = {A → B, B → C} gives (B,C) and (A,B).
  - Example: class(course_id, title, dept_name, credits, sec_id, semester, year, building, room_number, capacity, time_slot_id) gives course, classroom and section.
- **Elmasri's BCNF procedures.** Algorithm 15.5 replaces Q by (Q − Y) and (X ∪ Y) for a violating X → Y: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf). The "General Procedure" splits R into R − A and X ∪ A, repeating if either piece is still not BCNF: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- **UTA's BCNF procedure.** Pick a violating X → y, compute X⁺, and split into R1 = X⁺ and R2 = (R − X⁺) ∪ X. Repeat as needed. "it is guaranteed that the original relation can be recovered losslessly". Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
- **BCNF cannot always preserve dependencies.** R = (J, K, L) with F = {JK → L, L → K} has candidate keys JK and JL. R is not in BCNF, and "Any decomposition of R will fail to preserve JK → L". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **A 3NF example.** dept_advisor(s_ID, i_ID, dept_name) with F = {s_ID, dept_name → i_ID; i_ID → dept_name} has candidate keys {s_ID, dept_name} and {i_ID, s_ID}. It is in 3NF because dept_name is contained in a candidate key, but "There is some redundancy in this schema". Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **Lossless join.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Definition: r = Π_R1(r) ⋈ Π_R2(r) for every legal r.
  - Test: the decomposition is lossless if at least one of R1 ∩ R2 → R1 or R1 ∩ R2 → R2 is in F⁺.
  - "The above functional dependencies are a sufficient condition for lossless join decomposition; the dependencies are a necessary condition only if all constraints are functional dependencies."
- **Elmasri's NJB test.** "PROPERTY NJB (non-additive join test for binary decompositions)": D = {R1, R2} has the lossless join property with respect to F "if and only if" ((R1 ∩ R2) → (R1 − R2)) ∈ F⁺ or ((R1 ∩ R2) → (R2 − R1)) ∈ F⁺. Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- HKUST phrases the same test as "the common attribute of R1 and R2 must be a candidate key for R1 or R2". Source: [HKUST COMP 5311 L07](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~dimitris/5311/L07.pdf)
- **Dependency preservation.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Let Fi be the set of FDs in F⁺ that include only attributes of Ri. The decomposition preserves dependencies if (F1 ∪ … ∪ Fn)⁺ = F⁺.
  - Polynomial test for each α → β in F: `result = α; repeat for each Ri: t = (result ∩ Ri)⁺ ∩ Ri; result = result ∪ t`. The FD is preserved iff result ⊇ β.
- **Example on R(A,B,C) with F = {A → B, B → C}.** (A,B),(B,C) is lossless (B → BC) and dependency-preserving. (A,B),(A,C) is lossless (A → AB) but not dependency-preserving, because B → C cannot be checked without a join. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- **3NF synthesis algorithm.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  1. Start from Fc.
  2. For each α → β in Fc, add the schema αβ unless some existing schema already contains αβ.
  3. If no schema contains a candidate key of R, add one.
  4. Optionally delete any schema contained in another.
  - "each relation schema Ri is in 3NF" and the decomposition is "dependency preserving and lossless-join".
- **Worked synthesis (slides).** cust_banker_branch(customer_id, employee_id, branch_name, type) with FDs customer_id, employee_id → branch_name, type; employee_id → branch_name; customer_id, branch_name → employee_id. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - branch_name is extraneous on the right side of the first FD.
  - The loop yields (customer_id, employee_id, type), (employee_id, branch_name) and (customer_id, branch_name, employee_id).
  - (employee_id, branch_name) is deleted as a subset of another schema.
  - Final result: (customer_id, employee_id, type) and (customer_id, branch_name, employee_id). The "result will not depend on the order in which FDs are considered".
- **Elmasri Algorithm 15.4 (synthesis).** Find a minimal cover G. For each left side X, create the relation X ∪ {A1, …, Ak}. If no relation contains a key of R, add one. Source: [E&N Ch. 15 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf)
- **Why the key relation matters.** Including a relation that contains a candidate key guarantees that a dependency-preserving 3NF decomposition is also lossless. Source: [DSC PE 8.14](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **BCNF vs. 3NF.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - It is always possible to decompose into 3NF so that the decomposition is lossless and preserves the dependencies.
  - It is always possible to decompose into BCNF losslessly, but "it may not be possible to preserve dependencies".
  - Design goals: BCNF, lossless join and dependency preservation. If these cannot all be achieved, accept either a lack of dependency preservation or the redundancy of 3NF.
- **What other courses say:**
  - UVA: 3NF is lossless and "Always dependency preserving" but "Possible to have extra data (there may be redundancy)", while BCNF "Guarantee[s] redundancy free" but "May involve dependency across relations": [UVA CS 4750 F26](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~up3f/cs4750/slides/4750meet10-11-3NF-BCNF.pdf)
  - CMU: 3NF "Always preserves dependencies (unlike BCNF) but may have some anomalies": [CMU Lecture 05](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/05-notes-normalforms.pdf)
- **TEACH(Student, Course, Instructor).** fd1 {Student, Course} → Instructor and fd2 Instructor → Course; the relation is "in 3NF but not in BCNF". Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
  - D1 = {Student, Instructor} + {Student, Course}.
  - D2 = {Course, Instructor} + {Course, Student}.
  - D3 = {Instructor, Course} + {Instructor, Student}.
  - "All three decompositions will lose fd1", and by NJB only D3 is non-additive, because Instructor → Course holds.
- **Several valid BCNF decompositions exist.** R′ = (A,B,C,D) with F′ = {A → B, C → D, B → C} has at least three: {(A,B),(C,D),(B,C)}, {(A,B),(C,D),(A,C)} and {(B,C),(A,D),(A,B)}. The posted answer prints the second line twice. Source: [DSC PE 8.15 solutions](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **Keys after decomposition.** After a BCNF split on α → β, α should be the primary key of r1(α, β) and a foreign key in r2. In 3NF synthesis, α is the primary key of each ri(αβ), and the relation holding a candidate key has it as its primary key. Source: [DSC PE 8.11](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)
- **MVDs and 4NF.** Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
  - Definition: α →→ β holds if, for any tuples t1, t2 with t1[α] = t2[α], there exist tuples t3, t4 with t3[β] = t1[β], t3[R − β] = t2[R − β], t4[β] = t2[β] and t4[R − β] = t1[R − β].
  - Every FD is also an MVD.
  - 4NF: for every MVD α →→ β in D⁺, either it is trivial (β ⊆ α or α ∪ β = R) or α is a superkey. 4NF implies BCNF.
  - Example: inst_info(ID, child_name, phone) is in BCNF, yet adding one phone for an instructor with two children requires two tuples. It is better to split it into (ID, child_name) and (ID, phone).

### Inferences
**BCNF decomposition, exam recipe:**
1. Find the keys of the current relation.
2. Pick a violating X → Y (X not a superkey). Use the projected FDs if this is a decomposed piece.
3. Compute X⁺ ∩ Ri.
4. Split into R1 = X⁺ ∩ Ri and R2 = Ri − (X⁺ − X).
5. Recurse on any piece that still violates BCNF.
6. Afterwards, list which FDs were lost, using the preservation test.

**BCNF results depend on the order (script-verified).** Take R = CSJDPQV with F = {C → SJDPQV, JP → C, SD → P, J → S}.
- Splitting first on SD → P gives (D,P,S), (J,S), (C,D,J,Q,V). This loses JP → C.
- Splitting first on J → S gives (J,S), (C,D,J,P,Q,V). This loses SD → P.
- Both are correct BCNF answers. Always show the violating FD you used at each step.

**3NF synthesis, exam recipe:**
1. Compute Fc.
2. Make one schema per left side (α together with all its right-hand attributes).
3. Add a candidate key as its own schema if no schema contains one.
4. Delete any schema that is a subset of another.

Notes:
- Synthesis can split a relation that is already in 3NF. For PE 8.6 it gives (A,B,C), (C,D,E), (B,D), (A,E) even though R is already in 3NF (script-verified). So test the normal form first; if R is already in 3NF, it needs no decomposition.
- Because canonical covers can differ (Elmasri: "several equivalent minimal sets"), two students may get different but equally valid 3NF schemas.

**Choosing between BCNF and 3NF.** Prefer BCNF when it is dependency-preserving. If some FD would be lost (JK → L, TEACH), 3NF keeps it checkable inside one table at the cost of some redundancy.

### Inferences

**E1 (★☆☆).** R(A,B,C,D), PK AB, F = {AB → C, B → D}.
- **(a) Keys:** L = {A, B} and R-only = {C, D}. (AB)⁺: AB → C gives ABC; B → D gives ABCD = R. So the only key is AB. Prime: A, B. Nonprime: C, D.
- **(b) 2NF test:** A⁺ = A. B⁺ = BD, which contains the nonprime D, so B → D is partial. **Highest NF: 1NF.**
- **(c) Decomposition:** R1(B, D) with key B; R2(A, B, C) with key AB and FK B → R1. Both are in 3NF and BCNF.
- **(d) Checks:** R1 ∩ R2 = B and B⁺ = BD ⊇ R1, so the split is lossless. AB → C lies in R2 and B → D in R1, so it is dependency-preserving.

**E2 (★☆☆).** R(A,B,C,D,E,F), PK AB, F = {AB → C, A → D, D → E, C → F}.
- **(a) Keys:** L = {A, B}; R-only = {E, F}; B-type = {C, D}. (AB)⁺ = ABCDEF, so the only key is AB. Nonprime: C, D, E, F.
- **(b) 2NF test:** A⁺ = ADE, so D is partially dependent (given) and E is too (implied, via D). B⁺ = B. **Not 2NF; highest NF 1NF.**
- **(c) 2NF decomposition:** R1(A, D, E) carries A → D and D → E. R2(A, B, C, F) carries AB → C and C → F. F stays in R2 because it is fully dependent on AB, via C.
- **(d) 3NF:** in R1, D → E has a non-superkey left side and a nonprime right side, so split it into (A, D) and (D, E). In R2, C → F splits it into (A, B, C) and (C, F).
- **(e) Final:** (A,B,C), (C,F), (A,D), (D,E). All are BCNF; the decomposition is lossless and dependency-preserving. Synthesis from Fc = F gives the same four schemas.

**E3 (★★☆, the E&N-style exercise).** R(A,B,C,D,E,F,G,H,I,J), F = {AB → C, A → DE, B → F, F → GH, D → IJ}.
- **(a) Keys:** L = {A, B}; R-only = {C, E, G, H, I, J}; B-type = {D, F}. (AB)⁺ = ABCDEFGHIJ, so the only key is AB. Nonprime: C through J.
- **(b) 2NF test:** A⁺ = ADEIJ, so D, E, I, J are partially dependent. B⁺ = BFGH, so F, G, H are partially dependent. C is fully dependent on AB. **Highest NF: 1NF.**
- **(c) 2NF decomposition:** R1(A,B,C), R2(A,D,E,I,J), R3(B,F,G,H).
- **(d) 3NF:** R2 contains D → IJ, giving R2a(A,D,E) and R2b(D,I,J). R3 contains F → GH, giving R3a(B,F) and R3b(F,G,H).
- **(e) Final:** R1(A,B,C), R2a(A,D,E), R2b(D,I,J), R3a(B,F), R3b(F,G,H). All are BCNF; the result is lossless and dependency-preserving. It matches the Alberta solution.

**E4 (★★☆, two candidate keys; 3NF ≠ BCNF).** R = STU_COURSE(StuID, Email, CourseNo, Grade, StuName), F = {StuID → Email, StuName; Email → StuID; StuID CourseNo → Grade}.
- **(a) Keys:** CourseNo is L-only; Grade and StuName are R-only; StuID and Email are B-type. CourseNo⁺ = CourseNo. {StuID, CourseNo}⁺ = R and {Email, CourseNo}⁺ = R. **Keys: {StuID, CourseNo} and {Email, CourseNo}.** Prime: StuID, Email, CourseNo. Nonprime: Grade, StuName.
- **(b) 2NF test:** with PK {StuID, CourseNo}, StuID⁺ = {StuID, Email, StuName}. StuName is nonprime, so it is partially dependent. Under the general definition, Email → StuName is also partial on the other key. **Highest NF: 1NF.**
- **(c) 2NF decomposition** (move only the nonprime StuName): STUDENT(StuID, StuName) and SC(StuID, Email, CourseNo, Grade).
- **(d) 3NF test on SC:**
  - Keys of SC: {StuID, CourseNo} and {Email, CourseNo}.
  - StuID → Email is acceptable because Email is prime. Email → StuID is acceptable because StuID is prime. The remaining FD has a superkey on the left.
  - So **SC is in 3NF**, and the decomposition is lossless and dependency-preserving.
  - SC is **not BCNF**: StuID → Email has a non-superkey left side, so each student's email is repeated for every course.
- **(e) BCNF (beyond class):**
  - Split SC on StuID → Email, then merge the (StuID, Email) piece with STUDENT, since they share the same key.
  - Result: STUDENT(StuID, Email, StuName) and GRADE(StuID, CourseNo, Grade). Both are BCNF, and the result is lossless and dependency-preserving. 3NF synthesis gives the same.
  - Lesson: a prime attribute (Email) is never moved by the 2NF/3NF steps; only BCNF catches this kind of redundancy.

**E5 (★★☆, Silberschatz PE 8.6 / 8.1 / 8.13).** R(A,B,C,D,E), F = {A → BC, CD → E, B → D, E → A}.
- **(a) Closures:** A⁺ = ABCDE; E⁺ = ABCDE; B⁺ = BD; C⁺ = C; D⁺ = D; (BC)⁺ = ABCDE; (CD)⁺ = ABCDE; (BD)⁺ = BD.
- **(b) Keys:** every attribute is B-type. Single attributes: A and E are keys. Pairs avoiding A and E: BC ✔, CD ✔, BD ✘. **Keys: A, E, BC, CD.**
- **(c) Fc = F** (no extraneous attributes; left sides are unique).
- **(d) Normal form:** every attribute is prime, so R is in 3NF (and therefore 2NF). B → D has a non-superkey left side. **Highest NF: 3NF (not BCNF).**
- **(e) BCNF decomposition:** split on B → D into (B, D) and (A, B, C, E). The second piece has keys A, E and BC within it, so it is BCNF. The split is lossless (B → BD). CD → E is **lost**: C and D end up in different relations, and the preservation test confirms it.
- **(f) Checking two given decompositions:**
  - (A,B,C)+(A,D,E): lossless, because A is a key. Not dependency-preserving: B → D and CD → E are lost.
  - (A,B,C)+(C,D,E): **lossy**, because C⁺ = C.
- **(g) 3NF:** R is already in 3NF, so no decomposition is needed.

**E6 (★★☆, TEACH).** R(Student, Course, Instructor), F = {Student Course → Instructor, Instructor → Course}.
- **Keys:** Student is L-only. {Student, Course} and {Student, Instructor} are keys. All attributes are prime, so R is in **3NF**. Instructor → Course violates BCNF.
- **BCNF split:** (Instructor, Course) and (Instructor, Student). This is D3. It is lossless (Instructor → Course) and loses fd1.
- **D1** {S,I}+{S,C}: lossy, because the intersection S is not a superkey of either piece.
- **D2** {C,I}+{C,S}: lossy, because C → I does not hold.

**E7 (★★★, Purdue CSJDPQV).** R(C,S,J,D,P,Q,V), F = {C → SJDPQV, JP → C, SD → P, J → S}.
- **(a) Keys:**
  - Q and V are R-only, so they are in no key. There are no L-only attributes.
  - C⁺ = R, so **C** is a key.
  - Pairs without C: (JP)⁺: JP → C gives everything, so ✔. (JD)⁺: J → S, then SD → P, then JP → C gives everything, so ✔. (SD)⁺ = SDP ✘.
  - **Keys: C, JP, JD.** Prime: C, J, P, D. Nonprime: S, Q, V.
- **(b) Normal form:** J ⊂ JP and J → S with S nonprime, so it is a partial dependency. **Highest NF: 1NF.**
- **(c) Fc:**
  - In C → SJDPQV, S is extraneous (C → J → S).
  - P is also extraneous (C → D; C → S via J; SD → P).
  - **Fc = {C → JDQV, JP → C, SD → P, J → S}.**
- **(d) 3NF synthesis:** CJDQV, CJP, SDP, JS. C ⊆ CJDQV, so no key relation needs to be added. This matches the Purdue answer.
- **(e) BCNF:** depends on the order of violations (§8). One order gives {DPS, JS, CDJQV} and loses JP → C. The other gives {JS, CDJPQV} and loses SD → P.
- **(f) Elmasri-style manual 2NF split:** J → S gives (J,S) and (C,J,D,P,Q,V). This is already BCNF and lossless, but **SD → P is lost**. The manual process can lose an FD whose right side is prime; synthesis does not.

**E8 (★★★, PK-only vs. general; synthesis vs. BCNF).** R(A,B,C,D,E), F = {AB → C, C → D, D → B, D → E}.
- **(a) Keys:**
  - A is L-only (in every key); E is R-only (in no key).
  - A⁺ = A, so A alone is not a key.
  - (AB)⁺ = (AC)⁺ = (AD)⁺ = ABCDE. **Keys: AB, AC, AD.**
  - Prime: A, B, C, D. Nonprime: E.
- **(b) 2NF, PK-only reading (PK = AB):** A⁺ = A and B⁺ = B, so there is no partial dependency on the PK. R "is 2NF with respect to the PK".
- **(c) 2NF, general reading:** C ⊂ AC and C⁺ = BCDE ∋ E. So C → E (and D → E) are partial dependencies. **R is not 2NF; highest NF 1NF.**
- **(d) Fc = {AB → C, C → D, D → BE}.**
- **(e) 3NF synthesis:** (A,B,C), (C,D), (B,D,E). AB ⊆ ABC, so no key relation is needed.
  - Inside (A,B,C), C → B holds (via C → D → B). C is not a superkey there, but B is prime, so (A,B,C) is in 3NF and not BCNF.
  - The result is lossless and preserves all dependencies.
- **(f) BCNF:**
  - Split on C → BDE into (B,C,D,E) and (A,C).
  - Then split (B,C,D,E) on D → BE into (B,D,E) and (C,D).
  - Final: (A,C), (C,D), (B,D,E). It is lossless but **AB → C is lost**. This is the same trade-off as JK → L.

### Inferences
**P1. FDs from an instance (E&N Fig. 14.8).** R(A,B,C,D) has tuples (a1,b1,c1,d1), (a1,b2,c2,d2), (a2,b2,c2,d3), (a3,b3,c4,d3). For each FD, say "may hold" or "ruled out": A → B, B → C, C → B, B → A, D → C, AB → D, CD → B, D → A.
**Answer:**
- Ruled out: A → B (t1, t2), B → A (t2, t3), D → C (t3, t4), D → A (t3, t4).
- May hold: B → C, C → B, AB → D, CD → B.
- An instance can never prove that an FD holds.

**P2. Closures (DSC).** R(A,B,C,G,H,I), F = {A → B, A → C, CG → H, CG → I, B → H}. Find A⁺, (AG)⁺, (CG)⁺ and B⁺. Is AG a candidate key? Is AG → I implied? Is B → C implied?
**Answer:**
- A⁺ = ABCH; (AG)⁺ = ABCGHI; (CG)⁺ = CGHI; B⁺ = BH.
- AG is a superkey. A⁺ ≠ R and G⁺ = G ≠ R, so AG is a candidate key. It is the only one, since A and G are L-only.
- AG → I: yes. B → C: no.

**P3. All keys.** R(A,B,C,D,E), F = {A → B, BC → E, ED → A}.
**Answer:** keys ACD, BCD, CDE (see §7). Every attribute is prime, so the highest NF is **3NF**, not BCNF (A → B has a non-superkey left side).

**P4. All keys (UTA).** R(A,B,C,D,E), F = {AB → C, CD → E, DE → B}.
**Answer:** A and D are L-only. (AD)⁺ = AD. ABD, ACD and ADE are all keys. All attributes are prime, so the highest NF is **3NF**, not BCNF.

**P5. Keys and normal form.** R(A,B,C,D,E,F), F = {AB → C, C → D, D → A, BE → F}.
**Answer:**
- B and E are L-only; F is R-only. (BE)⁺ = BEF.
- Keys: ABE, BCE, BDE. Prime: A–E. Nonprime: F.
- BE ⊂ ABE and BE → F is a partial dependency, so the highest NF is **1NF**.

**P6. 1NF techniques.** DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname, Dmgr_ssn, Dlocations) has the multivalued attribute Dlocations. List three ways to reach 1NF. Which is best, and which leaves a relation that is not in 2NF?
**Answer:**
1. DEPT_LOCATIONS(Dnumber, Dlocation) plus DEPARTMENT without the locations. This is the **best** option.
2. Key {Dnumber, Dlocation} in one table. This introduces redundancy and is **not 2NF**, because Dnumber → Dname, Dmgr_ssn is partial.
3. Columns Dlocation1–3. This produces NULLs and needs a known maximum.

**P7. 2NF.** ORDER_LINE(OrderNo, ProductNo, Qty, ProductName, UnitPrice), F = {OrderNo ProductNo → Qty; ProductNo → ProductName, UnitPrice}.
**Answer:** the key is {OrderNo, ProductNo}. ProductNo → ProductName, UnitPrice is partial, so the relation is in **1NF**. Decompose into ORDER_LINE(OrderNo, ProductNo, Qty) and PRODUCT(ProductNo, ProductName, UnitPrice). Both are BCNF; the result is lossless and dependency-preserving.

**P8. 3NF.** PROJ_DEPT(Pnumber, Pname, Plocation, Dnum, Dname, Dmgr_ssn), F = {Pnumber → Pname, Plocation, Dnum; Dnum → Dname, Dmgr_ssn}.
**Answer:** the key is Pnumber, so the relation is in 2NF. Pnumber → Dnum → Dname, Dmgr_ssn is transitive, so it is not 3NF. Decompose into PROJECT(Pnumber, Pname, Plocation, Dnum) and DEPARTMENT(Dnum, Dname, Dmgr_ssn). Both are BCNF.

**P9. E&N-style exercise, second FD set.** R(A,…,J), G = {AB → C, BD → EF, AD → GH, A → I, H → J}. Find the key and decompose to 2NF, then 3NF.
**Answer:**
- A, B and D are L-only, and (ABD)⁺ = R, so the **key is ABD** (the only one).
- Closures of the proper subsets of ABD: A⁺ = AI; (AB)⁺ = ABCI; (BD)⁺ = BDEF; (AD)⁺ = ADGHIJ.
- **2NF:** (A,B,C), (B,D,E,F), (A,D,G,H,J), (A,I), **plus (A,B,D)**.
- **3NF:** split (A,D,G,H,J) on H → J into (A,D,G,H) and (H,J). Final: (A,B,C), (B,D,E,F), (A,D,G,H), (H,J), (A,I), (A,B,D). All are BCNF; lossless; dependency-preserving.
- Without (A,B,D), the chase test shows the decomposition is **lossy**. This is why the Alberta posted answer needs the key relation added.

**P10. COMPANY DEPENDENT variant.** DEP_X(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship, Ename), F = {Essn Dependent_name → Sex, Bdate, Relationship; Essn → Ename}.
**Answer:** the key is {Essn, Dependent_name}. Essn → Ename is partial, so the relation is in **1NF**. Decompose into DEPENDENT(Essn, Dependent_name, Sex, Bdate, Relationship) and EMP(Essn, Ename), the latter being part of EMPLOYEE. Both are BCNF.

**P11. Foreign-key trap.** Is EMPLOYEE(Fname, Minit, Lname, Ssn, Bdate, Address, Sex, Salary, Super_ssn, Dno) in 3NF, given that only Ssn → all holds?
**Answer:** yes, in fact BCNF. Foreign keys such as Dno and Super_ssn determine nothing inside EMPLOYEE. If Dname were stored in EMPLOYEE (with Dno → Dname), the relation would no longer be 3NF.

**P12. LOTS concept.** Is LOTS in 2NF using the primary key Property_id# only? Using the general definition? Which FD causes the 3NF violation in LOTS1?
**Answer:**
- PK-only: yes, there is no partial dependency on a single-attribute PK.
- General: no. County_name → Tax_rate is partial on the key {County_name, Lot#}.
- LOTS1 violates 3NF because of Area → Price.

**P13. TEACH.** Is TEACH(Student, Course, Instructor) with {Student, Course} → Instructor and Instructor → Course in 3NF? In BCNF? Which of D1, D2, D3 is lossless?
**Answer:** 3NF yes (Course is prime). BCNF no. Only **D3** = {Instructor, Course} + {Instructor, Student} is lossless, and all three lose fd1.

**P14. All-prime relation (UTA).** R(A,B,C,D), F = {AB → C, C → D, D → A}. Find the keys and the highest NF, and decompose into BCNF.
**Answer:**
- B is L-only. Keys: AB, BC, BD. All attributes are prime, so the highest NF is **3NF**.
- C → D and D → A violate BCNF.
- BCNF decomposition, for example: (B,C), (C,D), (A,D). It is lossless, and **AB → C is lost**.

**P15. Canonical cover (DSC).** F = {A → BC, B → C, A → B, AB → C}.
**Answer:** Fc = {A → B, B → C}. Steps: merge A → BC and A → B; A is extraneous in AB → C; C is extraneous in A → BC.

**P16. Canonical cover and key (Purdue).** R(A,B,C,D,E), F = {AB → D, AC → E, A → B, BC → E, A → E}.
**Answer:** Fc = {A → BDE, BC → E}. The key is AC. A → BDE is partial on AC, so the highest NF is **1NF**.

**P17. Lossless and dependency preservation (DSC).** Use F = {A → BC, CD → E, B → D, E → A} on R(A,B,C,D,E).
**Answer:**
- (A,B,C)/(A,D,E): lossless, because A → ABC. Not dependency-preserving: B → D and CD → E are lost.
- (A,B,C)/(C,D,E): lossy, because C⁺ = C is not a superkey of either piece.

**P18. 3NF synthesis (DSC).** cust_banker_branch(customer_id, employee_id, branch_name, type), F = {customer_id employee_id → branch_name type; employee_id → branch_name; customer_id branch_name → employee_id}.
**Answer:**
- Fc: delete branch_name from the right side of the first FD.
- Synthesis gives (customer_id, employee_id, type), (employee_id, branch_name) and (customer_id, branch_name, employee_id). The second is a subset of the third and is dropped.
- **Result:** (customer_id, employee_id, type) and (customer_id, branch_name, employee_id).
- Keys of R: {customer_id, employee_id} and {customer_id, branch_name}. R itself is already in 3NF but not BCNF (script-verified).

**P19. BCNF decomposition (DSC class example).**
**Answer:**
- course(course_id, title, dept_name, credits)
- classroom(building, room_number, capacity)
- section(course_id, sec_id, semester, year, building, room_number, time_slot_id)
- The result is lossless and dependency-preserving (script-verified).

**P20. Trap: an implied partial dependency.** R(A,B,C,D), F = {A → C, AC → D, BC → A}. What is the highest NF?
**Answer:**
- B is L-only; D is R-only. Keys: AB and BC. Prime: A, B, C. Nonprime: D.
- No **listed** FD shows a 2NF violation:
  - A → C has the prime C on its right.
  - AC → D has a left side that is not a proper subset of any key.
- But A⁺ = ACD, so the **implied** A → D is partial on AB. **Highest NF: 1NF.**
- The canonical cover reveals it: Fc = {A → CD, BC → A}.
- Correct 2NF decomposition: (A,D) and (A,B,C). This is lossless and dependency-preserving; (A,B,C) is 3NF but not BCNF.
- Wrong version: moving all of A⁺ out, giving (A,C,D) and (A,B), **loses BC → A** (script-verified).

## 11. Common exam traps

### Takeaway
Most lost points come from five things: missing candidate keys other than the PK; testing 2NF only against the listed FDs; mixing up the 3NF condition (prime right side allowed) with BCNF (superkey left side required); closure-computation slips; and decompositions that drop the key relation or move prime attributes out.

### Cited Findings
- The simplified BCNF test (check only F) is wrong for decomposed relations. Example: R2 = (A,C,D,E) with F = {A → B, BC → D}, where AC → D ∈ F⁺. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- A dependency through a candidate key is not a 3NF problem. Elmasri's example is EMP(SSN, Emp#, Salary). Source: [E&N Ch. 14 slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter14.pdf)
- The textbook summary table's 3NF wording misses mixed-attribute left sides. Source: [UTA notes](https://ranger.uta.edu/~cli/lecnotes/notes-db-normalization.pdf)
- Canonical cover vs. minimal cover vs. CMU's single-attribute-RHS "canonical cover". Sources: [DSC slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt), [E&N Ch. 15](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs448_Fall2017/lpdf/Chapter15.pdf), [CMU Lecture 04](https://15445.courses.cs.cmu.edu/fall2017/notes/04-notes-functionaldependencies.pdf)
- When testing extraneous attributes, the test is "done using Fc, not F", and the union rule may need re-applying after deletions. Source: [DSC Ch. 8 slides](https://codex.cs.yale.edu/avi/db-book/db6/slide-dir/PPT-dir/ch8.ppt)
- BCNF decompositions are not unique: PE 8.15 has three of them. Source: [DSC PE 8.15](https://www.db-book.com/db6/practice-exer-dir/8s.pdf)

### Inferences
1. **Only using the PK.** Always derive all candidate keys. LOTS and E8 show that the PK-only and general 2NF answers can differ. State which definition you used.
2. **Testing 2NF against listed FDs only.** Compute S⁺ for every proper subset of every key. Partial dependencies can be implied (P20; E2's A → E).
3. **Confusing 3NF and BCNF.** 3NF allows X → A with X not a superkey **if A is prime**. BCNF does not allow it at all. Examples: TEACH, PE 8.6, E4.
4. **Calling a dependency "transitive" when the middle set is a key** (SSN → Emp# → Salary; a unique Dname in DEPARTMENT). That is not a violation.
5. **Treating a foreign key as a violation.** Dno in EMPLOYEE is fine; it only becomes a problem if Dname is also stored there (P11).
6. **Closure slips:**
   - stopping after one pass;
   - forgetting that α ⊆ α⁺;
   - firing an FD whose left side is not yet entirely inside the result;
   - forgetting FDs that become usable later in the computation.
7. **Superkey vs. candidate key.** You must check minimality. Prime means "in some *candidate* key".
8. **2NF/3NF splitting errors:**
   - moving prime attributes out (P20, which loses BC → A);
   - forgetting to carry transitively dependent nonprime attributes into the 2NF piece (W4);
   - leaving a nonprime attribute in two places.
9. **Dropping the key relation.** Keep a relation that contains a key of R, or the join can be lossy (P9; Alberta's posted answer; DSC PE 8.14).
10. **Lossless test misuse.** Compute (R1 ∩ R2)⁺ and check that it covers R1 or R2. "There is some FD between the pieces" is not the test, and the intersection only needs to be a **superkey** of one side.
11. **Testing a decomposed piece with F instead of the projection of F⁺** (the DSC R2 = (A,C,D,E) example).
12. **Canonical-cover slips:**
    - testing a right-side attribute with the full F (it must use F′ with that attribute removed);
    - not re-applying the union rule;
    - expecting a unique answer, when several valid covers can exist.
13. **Expecting a unique BCNF answer.** Show the violating FD at every step; any valid order earns credit if done correctly.
14. **Forgetting that every 2-attribute relation is BCNF.**
15. **Trusting an FD read off a sample table.** An instance can only refute an FD (Fig. 14.7 / 14.8).
16. **Trivial FDs** (β ⊆ α) never violate any normal form, so ignore them.
17. **3NF synthesis slips:** forgetting to add the key schema, or forgetting to delete subsumed schemas. Also, applying synthesis to a relation that is already in 3NF is legal but unnecessary.
18. **Mixing notations.** Elmasri writes "nonadditive join" and "minimal cover". Silberschatz writes "lossless-join" and "canonical cover Fc". Use the course's terms (Silberschatz notation was requested) but recognize both.

**Self-check tools**
- **Carleton University "Functional Dependency Normalizer"** (Louis D. Nel). Computes a minimal cover, candidate keys and a lossless, dependency-preserving 3NF decomposition. For ≤ 10 attributes it lists all candidate keys, classifies prime/nonprime attributes and tests 1NF/2NF/3NF/BCNF. Input looks like `A,B->C,D`. [Getting started](https://people.scs.carleton.ca/~ldnel/normalizer/getting_started.html), [tool](https://people.scs.carleton.ca/~ldnel/normalizer/)
- **Raymond Cho's "Relational Database Tools".** Computes attribute closures of all subsets, candidate keys and superkeys, a minimum (canonical) cover and F⁺. Checks 1NF–4NF, gives a lossless dependency-preserving 3NF decomposition and a lossless BCNF decomposition. It is self-described as "a personal project and not a professionally managed service". [link](http://raymondcho.net/RelationalDatabaseTools/RelationalDatabaseTools.html)
- **arjo129 "Functional Dependencies Checker".** Computes attribute closure, FD closure, minimal cover and the 2NF/3NF/BCNF checks. Input looks like `{a,b,c}->{d}`. [link](https://arjo129.github.io/functionalDependencyCalculator/index.html)
- **University of Illinois Springfield "Database Design Tool".** Computes attribute closure, keys, minimal cover and a 3NF decomposition that is dependency-preserving with "no information lost". [link](https://uisacad5.uis.edu/cgi-bin/mcrem2/database_design_tool.cgi)

### Inferences
- **How to self-check safely:**
  1. Enter your FDs and compare the candidate keys and normal-form verdicts first; these should match exactly.
  2. For Fc and decompositions, check *equivalence* rather than identical text, because tools may use singleton-RHS minimal covers or a different violation order.
  3. Verify losslessness yourself with the binary test.
