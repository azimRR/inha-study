## (a) Perfect competition and the supply curve: conditions, P = MC, profit/loss, shut-down vs break-even price, short-run supply, long-run entry/exit

### Takeaway
A perfectly competitive (price-taking) firm faces a horizontal demand line at the market price, so MR = P. It maximizes profit by producing where **P = MC**, which Krugman calls the "price-taking firm's optimal output rule." Two cost minimums then tell you what the firm does:
- **Minimum ATC (the break-even price)** tells you whether it makes a profit.
- **Minimum AVC (the shut-down price)** tells you whether it produces at all in the short run.

So the short-run supply curve is the MC curve above minimum AVC. In the long run, entry and exit push the price to minimum ATC, and economic profit goes to zero.

#### Definitions and conditions (Krugman wording)
- "A price-taking producer is a producer whose actions have no effect on the market price of the good it sells." A price-taking consumer likewise cannot affect the price of what they buy. "A perfectly competitive market is a market in which all market participants are price-takers." — [Krugman & Wells *Economics* 3e, ch. 12 publisher lecture slides (hosted on a JLU Giessen course page)](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- Krugman gives **two necessary conditions** — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  1. "Many producers, none of whom have a large market share." Market share is the fraction of total industry output that one producer accounts for.
  2. A **standardized product**, also called a commodity: "consumers regard the products of different producers as the same good."
  - A third condition "is often satisfied as well": **free entry and exit**.
- On standardized products: "When it comes to defining the nature of competition, the consumer is always right." If consumers believe brands differ, the industry is not perfectly competitive, even when the products are physically identical — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- Free entry and exit means the number of producers can adjust to changing market conditions, and existing producers "cannot artificially keep other firms out" — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- Real-world case: when a popular drug's patent expires, many producers start selling standardized "generics", and "the shift to perfect competition is accompanied by a sharp fall in market price" — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)

#### Choosing the profit-maximizing output
- Marginal revenue is MR = ΔTR/ΔQ. The **optimal output rule**: profit is maximized at the output where the marginal cost of the last unit equals its marginal revenue — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- The **price-taking firm's optimal output rule**: produce where "the marginal cost of the last unit produced is equal to the market price." For a price-taking firm, MR = P, and the MR curve is "a horizontal line at the market price" — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- When working from a table: if no quantity makes MR exactly equal MC, "produce the largest quantity for which marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost" — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- OpenStax states the same rule: produce "where marginal revenue is equal to marginal cost—that is, where MR = MC." For perfect competitors this becomes P = MR = MC — [OpenStax *Principles of Economics* 3e, §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-2-how-perfectly-competitive-firms-make-output-decisions)
- Profit = (Price − Average cost) × Quantity, which is the same as TR − TC — [OpenStax §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-2-how-perfectly-competitive-firms-make-output-decisions)
- In Krugman's terms, per-unit profit is P − ATC when the firm is profitable, and per-unit loss is ATC − P when it is not — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)

#### Break-even price vs shut-down price
- The **break-even price** is "the market price at which it earns zero profits", which equals **minimum ATC** — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  - P > min ATC → profitable.
  - P = min ATC → breaks even.
  - P < min ATC → unprofitable.
- The **shut-down price** is **minimum AVC**: "A firm will cease production in the short run if the market price falls below the shut-down price." "Fixed cost is irrelevant to the firm's optimal short-run production decision" — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- Between the two prices, the firm makes a loss but keeps operating, because price is above AVC. It covers its variable costs and part of its fixed costs — [OpenStax §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-2-how-perfectly-competitive-firms-make-output-decisions)
- The **short-run individual supply curve** "shows how an individual producer's optimal output quantity depends on the market price, taking fixed cost as given." It is the MC curve at and above the shut-down price; below that price, output is zero — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- OpenStax: "the marginal cost curve is identical to the firm's supply curve starting from the minimum point on the average variable cost curve" — [OpenStax §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-2-how-perfectly-competitive-firms-make-output-decisions)
- "Fixed cost matters over time": if P stays below min ATC for an extended period, firms exit in the long run; if P is above min ATC, new firms enter — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)

#### Two textbook number sets to cross-check against
- **Krugman's own example (Jennifer & Jason's tomato farm)** — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  - Break-even price = $14 (min ATC, at 4 bushels). Shut-down price = $10 (min AVC).
  - At P = $18: the farm produces 5 bushels, ATC = $14.40, profit = ($18 − $14.40) × 5 = **$18**.
  - At P = $10: it produces 3 bushels, ATC = $14.67, loss ≈ 3 × $4.67 ≈ **$14**.
- **OpenStax raspberry farm** — [OpenStax §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-2-how-perfectly-competitive-firms-make-output-decisions):
  - P = $4 per pack; fixed cost = $62.
  - Profit-maximizing output is **80 packs**, where P = MC = $4.00. TR = $320, TC = $230, profit = **$90** (ATC $2.88, AVC $2.10).
  - The shutdown point is minimum AVC, about **$1.72**: "if the price falls below about $1.72 ... the firm must shut down."

#### The long run: entry, exit and zero economic profit
- Profits attract entry — [OpenStax §8.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-3-entry-and-exit-decisions-in-the-long-run):
  - "Entry of many new firms causes the market supply curve to shift to the right." Price falls and profits shrink.
  - Losses cause exit: "Exit of many firms causes the market supply curve to shift to the left," and price recovers.
  - Long-run equilibrium is "where P = MR = MC and P = AC": zero economic profit, with no incentive to enter or leave.
- Three shapes of long-run supply — [OpenStax §8.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-3-entry-and-exit-decisions-in-the-long-run):
  - **Constant-cost** industry: horizontal (e.g., corn farming).
  - **Increasing-cost**: slopes upward as input costs such as skilled-labor wages rise.
  - **Decreasing-cost**: slopes downward (e.g., high tech).
- Krugman's two industry supply curves — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  - The **short-run industry supply curve** holds the number of producers fixed.
  - The **long-run industry supply curve** allows time for entry and exit. It "is often horizontal", "may slope upward if there is limited supply of an input", and "is always more elastic than the short-run industry supply curve."
- What long-run equilibrium delivers — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  1. Every firm has the same MC, equal to price.
  2. With free entry and exit, each firm earns zero economic profit by producing at min ATC, so the industry's total cost of production is minimized.
  3. The outcome is efficient: "every consumer with a willingness to pay greater than or equal to marginal cost gets the good."
- "Economic profit, again": firms enter even though they will "do little more than break even", because cost includes opportunity cost — the return the owner could earn elsewhere. Zero *economic* profit is a normal return — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)
- Krugman's real-world illustrations — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf):
  - Corn's cost of production is approximately equal to its market price (a perfectly competitive industry).
  - "Cotton prices tripled between early 2010 and early 2011. And farmers responded by planting more cotton."

### Inferences

#### The graphs, explained in words
1. **Two side-by-side panels.**
   - Left panel (the market): downward-sloping demand and upward-sloping supply set the price P\*.
   - Right panel (one firm): a horizontal line at P\* labeled "D = MR = P". The firm can sell any amount at P\* and nothing above it. Draw U-shaped ATC and AVC curves. The rising MC curve cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
2. **Profit case (P > min ATC).** Where the price line crosses the rising MC curve gives Q\*. Go up from Q\* to the ATC curve to get cost per unit. Profit is the rectangle (P − ATC) high and Q\* wide.
3. **Loss, but keep producing (min AVC ≤ P < min ATC).** Same steps, but now ATC at Q\* lies above P. The rectangle (ATC − P) × Q\* is a loss. Because P is still above AVC, each unit pays its variable cost plus something toward fixed cost, so the loss is smaller than FC.
4. **Shut down (P < min AVC).** Any output loses more than FC, so the firm produces nothing and its loss equals FC.
5. **The firm's supply curve.** It is the MC curve from the minimum-AVC point upward. Below the shut-down price, supply sits on the vertical axis (Q = 0).
6. **Long run, constant-cost industry.**
   - If P > min ATC, entry shifts market supply right until P = min ATC.
   - If P < min ATC, exit shifts it left until P = min ATC.
   - The long-run industry supply curve is a horizontal line at min ATC.

#### Fully worked numeric example (constructed): Dilnoza's dried-apricot farm
Many farms sell identical dried apricots (a standardized product), so Dilnoza is a price taker. Her fixed cost FC = $40 (land rent and equipment).

| Q (boxes) | VC ($) | TC = FC + VC | MC of that box | AVC = VC/Q | ATC = TC/Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 40 | – | – | – |
| 1 | 20 | 60 | 20 | 20.00 | 60.00 |
| 2 | 30 | 70 | 10 | 15.00 | 35.00 |
| 3 | 42 | 82 | 12 | **14.00 (min)** | 27.33 |
| 4 | 58 | 98 | 16 | 14.50 | 24.50 |
| 5 | 80 | 120 | 22 | 16.00 | **24.00 (min)** |
| 6 | 110 | 150 | 30 | 18.33 | 25.00 |
| 7 | 150 | 190 | 40 | 21.43 | 27.14 |
| 8 | 202 | 242 | 52 | 25.25 | 30.25 |

**Step 1: find the two key prices.**
- Shut-down price = min AVC = **$14** (at Q = 3).
- Break-even price = min ATC = **$24** (at Q = 5).

**Step 2: apply P = MC at different prices.** Choose the largest Q where MC ≤ P.

| Market price | Q chosen (why) | TR | TC | Profit | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $34 | 6 (6th box MC $30 ≤ 34; 7th box $40 > 34) | 204 | 150 | **+$54** = (34 − 25) × 6 | Produce; economic profit |
| $24 | 5 (MC $22 ≤ 24; next $30 > 24) | 120 | 120 | **$0** | Break even (normal profit) |
| $18 | 4 (MC $16 ≤ 18; next $22 > 18) | 72 | 98 | **−$26** | Keep producing (see note) |
| $14 | 3 (or 0) | 42 | 82 | **−$40** | Indifferent: TR just covers VC; loss = FC either way (shut-down point) |
| $11 | 0 | 0 | 40 | **−$40** | Shut down: producing 2 boxes would give 22 − 70 = −$48, worse than −$40 |

Note on P = $18: losing $26 beats losing $40 by shutting down. Each box earns $18 − $14.50 = $3.50 above AVC, so 4 boxes contribute $14 toward FC, and the loss is $40 − $14 = $26.

**Step 3: the short-run supply schedule.** This is the MC curve above min AVC:

| Price | Below $14 | $14 | $18 | $24 | $34 | $45 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boxes supplied | 0 | 3 (or 0) | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |

**Step 4: the market and the long run.**
- With 100 identical farms, short-run industry supply at P = $34 is 600 boxes.
- Because $34 is above $24 (min ATC), farms earn economic profit. New farms enter, supply shifts right, and price falls.
- In a constant-cost industry, the price stops falling at $24. Each farm then produces 5 boxes at min ATC and earns zero economic profit.
- If the price had instead stayed at $18, losses would cause exit until the price returned to $24.

#### One-glance decision table
| Market price vs costs | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| P > min ATC | Produce where P = MC; economic profit | Entry → price falls toward min ATC |
| P = min ATC (break-even price) | Produce; zero economic profit | No entry or exit (long-run equilibrium) |
| min AVC ≤ P < min ATC | Produce where P = MC; loss smaller than FC | Exit → price rises toward min ATC |
| P < min AVC (shut-down price) | Shut down (Q = 0); loss = FC | Exit |

#### Exam traps
- **MR = MC is the general rule; P = MC applies only to price takers.** Writing "P = MC" for a monopolist is wrong.
- **The firm does not produce at min ATC in the short run**, unless P happens to equal min ATC. It maximizes *total* profit, not profit per unit. At P = $34, Dilnoza produces 6 boxes, not 5.
- **A loss does not mean shut down.** Compare P with **min AVC**, not min ATC. In the short run, fixed cost is paid either way.
- **Shutting down is not the same as exiting.** A short-run shutdown still pays FC. Exit is a long-run decision with no fixed cost left.
- **Zero economic profit is not "no profit".** The owner still earns a normal return, because opportunity cost is covered.
- **The supply curve starts at min AVC, not min ATC.** Between $14 and $24, Dilnoza supplies output while losing money.
- **The firm's demand curve is horizontal (perfectly elastic), but market demand slopes down.** Don't draw a downward-sloping demand curve in the firm panel.
- **In table questions, use the MC of the *next* unit.** Stop at the largest Q where MC ≤ P, and check profit directly (TR − TC) to be safe.
- **Free entry and exit** is Krugman's "often satisfied" third condition, not one of the two necessary conditions.

#### Bridge to monopoly (Ch. 8 — covered in separate notes)
Everything in this section depends on the firm being a price taker. That is why MR = P, and why the general optimal output rule (MR = MC) becomes P = MC — [KW ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf).

A monopolist is different in three ways:
- **Price.** It is the only seller, so it faces the downward-sloping market demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower its price, which makes MR lower than P. It still produces where MR = MC, but it charges the higher price buyers will pay for that quantity. So P > MC: it produces less than a competitive industry would, and there is a deadweight loss.
- **Long run.** Barriers to entry block the entry that wipes out profit in the long run under perfect competition. So a monopolist can keep making economic profit.
- **Exam rule of thumb.** MR = MC applies to every firm. "P = MC", "MR = P" and "zero economic profit in the long run" apply only to perfect competition.

*(This bridge is standard theory; the detailed monopoly sources are in the separate monopoly notes.)*

## (b) Market failure I — Externalities (*Essentials* Ch. 9): MSC vs MPC, MSB vs MPB, over/under-production, deadweight loss, remedies (Pigouvian taxes/subsidies, permits, regulation, Coase), and the Aral Sea case

### Takeaway
When an activity imposes costs or benefits on third parties, the market equilibrium ignores them. A good with a negative externality is over-produced; a good with a positive externality is under-produced. Either way there is a deadweight loss.

The remedy is to make decision-makers face the full social cost or benefit:
- Pigouvian taxes or subsidies equal to the marginal external cost or benefit;
- tradable permits;
- regulation;
- or private Coasean deals, which work only when property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap.

The Aral Sea is a well-documented, local case: Soviet-era irrigation, mainly for cotton, imposed huge costs on people downstream.

### Cited Findings

#### Core definitions
- An externality is "the effect of a market exchange on a third party who is outside or 'external' to the exchange." Externalities are also called spillovers — [OpenStax §12.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-1-the-economics-of-pollution)
  - Negative example: a country-music concert disturbs neighbors who hate country music.
  - Positive example: the same concert is free entertainment for neighbors who love it.
- With a negative externality, "social costs of production exceed social benefits to consumers, and the market produces too much of the product." This is a **market failure** — [OpenStax §12.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-1-the-economics-of-pollution)
- Positive externalities are "beneficial spillovers to a third party, or parties." Social benefits = the producer's private benefits + the value of all spillovers — [OpenStax §13.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-1-investments-in-innovation)

#### OpenStax numeric examples
- **Refrigerators (negative)** — [OpenStax §12.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-1-the-economics-of-pollution):
  - Market equilibrium using private costs: P = $650, Q = 45,000.
  - Pollution imposes costs of $100 per refrigerator on others, so the social-cost supply curve "shifts up by $100".
  - Social equilibrium: P = $700, Q = 40,000.
- **Innovation (positive)** — [OpenStax §13.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-1-investments-in-innovation):
  - The original inventor receives roughly "one-third to one-half of the total economic benefits from innovations."
  - In OpenStax's "Big Drug Company" example (8% interest rate), the firm invests $30 million based on private benefits. It would invest $52 million if it captured all the social benefits — an under-investment of $22 million.
- **Flu vaccines (positive):** subsidies move consumption from under-production toward the socially optimal quantity. The principle is to "help the party creating the positive externality receive a greater share of the social benefits" — [OpenStax §13.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-1-investments-in-innovation)
- **Compulsory immunization** is justified economically because it "prevents spillovers of illness to others—as well as helping the person immunized" — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)

#### Remedies
- **Pollution charge (Pigouvian tax)** — [OpenStax §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools):
  - It "gives a profit-maximizing firm an incentive to determine ways to reduce its emissions—as long as the marginal cost of reducing the emissions is less than the tax."
  - Example: a firm emits 50 lbs of particulates. Cutting each successive 10 lbs costs $300, $500, $900, $1,500 and $2,500. With a tax of $1,000 per 10 lbs, the firm cuts **30 lbs**, the three increments that cost less than the tax.
  - Real-world versions include gasoline taxes and 5–10-cent bottle deposits in 10 U.S. states.
- **Marketable permits (cap-and-trade)** — [OpenStax §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools):
  - The government sets a total limit and hands out permits that can be bought and sold. As a result, "the firms that find it least expensive to do so will reduce pollution the most."
  - In OpenStax's four-firm example, Gamma (low abatement cost) cuts emissions and sells 100 tons of permits, while Alpha and Delta buy 50 tons each.
  - Real program: the 1990 Clean Air Act amendments created a sulfur-dioxide cap-and-trade system for power plants. Permits were issued free of charge and declined over time.
- **Choosing between tools** — [OpenStax §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools):
  - Permits work best with "a few dozen or a few hundred parties."
  - Pollution charges suit cases where millions of users each emit small amounts, such as car engines or unrecycled cans.
  - Compared with command-and-control regulation, market tools "can achieve any desired reduction in pollution at a lower cost to society." A pollution tax reduces pollution "in a more flexible and cost-effective way."
- **Property rights and the Coase theorem** — [OpenStax §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools):
  - Coase's example: a train's sparks set fire to a farmer's field. "Should the farmer be required to build a tall fence ... or should the railroad be required to place a gadget on the locomotive's smokestack?"
  - The question cannot be settled "until one clearly defines property rights." Once rights are defined, the party holding them "will seek out and pay for the least costly method of reducing the risk."
- **Badly designed regulation backfires.** Under endangered-species land-use bans, "Rumors abounded of landowners who followed a policy of 'shoot, shovel, and shut up'." Paying landowners or giving them incentives works better than a blanket ban — [OpenStax §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools)
- **MRU:** Tyler Cowen's negative-externality example is antibiotic overuse. Users get the benefit, while society bears the cost of rising antibiotic resistance — [MRU, "What Are Negative Externalities?"](https://mru.org/courses/principles-economics-microeconomics/what-are-negative-externalities)
- **MRU videos:** ["An Introduction to Externalities"](http://mruniversity.com/courses/principles-economics-microeconomics/externalities-definition-pigovian-tax); ["External Benefits"](http://mruniversity.com/courses/principles-economics-microeconomics/flu-shot-positive-externalities-pigovian-subsidy) (flu shot and Pigouvian subsidy); "Command and Control Solutions"; "The Coase Theorem"; "Trading Pollution"; "A Deeper Look at Tradeable Allowances" — [MRU syllabus](https://mru.org/teacher-resources/principles-microeconomics-syllabus)

#### A private, Coase-style buy-out (good essay example)
- Cheshire, Ohio, 2002. Search-result excerpts from these sources report — [Tampa Bay Times archive (2002)](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/2002/05/08/dangerous-electricity-pollution/); [Wikipedia: Cheshire, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire,_Ohio):
  - American Electric Power's coal plant wrapped the village (population 221) in sulfuric haze and soot.
  - Instead of installing emission controls, AEP paid about **$20 million to buy out the village**.
  - Most residents left and released the company from future property and health claims.
- Sources disagree on the payment per home: about 3× home values according to [A. Guevorkian's notes on *Naked Economics*](https://andreigue.github.io/naked-econ-1/), versus 3.5× market value according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire,_Ohio).

#### The Aral Sea (Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan): a documented case
- **Cause.** "In the 1960s, the Soviet Union undertook a major water diversion project on the arid plains of Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan." The Syr Darya and Amu Darya rivers were redirected to irrigate the desert for "cotton and other crops." The Aral Sea was once the fourth-largest lake in the world — [NASA Earth Observatory, "World of Change: Shrinking Aral Sea"](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea)
- **Peer-reviewed confirmation.** The drying-up "started in the early 1960s and is overwhelmingly due to the expansion of irrigation that has drained its two tributary rivers," with wide ecological, human and economic impacts — [Micklin (2007), *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences* 35:47–72](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.earth.35.031306.140120)
- **Impacts** — [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea):
  - "Fisheries and the communities that depended on them collapsed."
  - The water became increasingly polluted.
  - "Blowing dust from the exposed lakebed, contaminated with agricultural chemicals."
  - Soils were degraded by salt.
  - The regional climate shifted: winters became colder and summers "hotter and drier."
  - The North and South seas separated, and the eastern lobe of the South Aral had vanished by 2014.
- **Fisheries at Aralsk (Kazakhstan).** Local fisheries "processed 20,000 tons of fish in the 1980s" but fell to "400 tons of salt-tolerant species (mainly flounder)." The sea had receded "almost 100 km" from the port of Aralsk — [World Bank, "Miraculous Catch in Kazakhstan's Northern Aral Sea" (27 June 2006)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/668681468050713490/text/9249602006Jun20a0Box0385367B0PUBLIC.txt)
- **Partial fix.** Kazakhstan built the Kok-Aral dike and dam, completed in 2005, separating the North and South seas. It restored the North Aral's fisheries but limits flow to the South Aral — [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea)
- **Results of the World Bank-funded dike** — [World Bank (2006)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/668681468050713490/text/9249602006Jun20a0Box0385367B0PUBLIC.txt):
  - It raised North Aral water levels "by an average of four meters."
  - The 2006 catch "increased tenfold."
  - The shoreline returned to about 25 km from Aralsk.
  - The project was expected to benefit "an estimated 1 million people."

### Inferences

#### The graphs, explained in words
1. **Negative externality in production** (e.g., pollution from a cement or fertilizer plant):
   - Axes: quantity on the horizontal axis, price/cost on the vertical axis.
   - Demand = MSB (assuming no consumption externality). Supply = MPC. Draw MSC above supply, higher by the marginal external cost (MEC).
   - The market produces Q_m, where demand meets MPC. The efficient output Q\* is where demand meets MSC. So Q\* < Q_m, and the market price is too low.
   - The **deadweight loss** is the triangle between MSC (above) and demand (below), from Q\* to Q_m. These are units whose social cost exceeds their benefit.
   - A **Pigouvian tax** equal to MEC at Q\* lifts private supply up to MSC.
2. **Positive externality in consumption** (e.g., vaccines, education):
   - Demand = MPB. Draw MSB above it, higher by the marginal external benefit (MEB). Supply = MC.
   - The market produces Q_m, where demand meets supply. The efficient output Q\* is where MSB meets supply, so Q_m < Q\*.
   - The **deadweight loss** is the triangle between MSB (above) and supply (below), from Q_m to Q\*.
   - A **Pigouvian subsidy** equal to MEB at Q\* lifts demand up to MSB.
3. **Krugman-style "socially optimal quantity of pollution" diagram.** Check this against your Ch. 9 slides; it comes from general knowledge of the Krugman framework, not a fetched source.
   - The horizontal axis shows the *quantity of pollution*.
   - The marginal social benefit of pollution slopes down (it represents the cost savings from being allowed to pollute). The marginal social cost of pollution slopes up.
   - The socially optimal quantity of pollution is where the two curves cross.
   - Left alone, polluters keep emitting until their own marginal benefit is zero. So the market produces too much pollution, and the best amount of pollution is **not zero**.

#### Worked example 1 (constructed): negative externality and a Pigouvian tax
Setup: demand (= MSB) is P = 100 − Q. Private supply (MPC) is P = 20 + Q. The marginal external cost is $20 per unit, so MSC is P = 40 + Q.
- **Market outcome:** 100 − Q = 20 + Q → **Q_m = 40, P = $60**.
- **Social optimum:** 100 − Q = 40 + Q → **Q\* = 30, P = $70**.
- **Deadweight loss** = ½ × (40 − 30) × (MSC at 40 − MSB at 40) = ½ × 10 × (80 − 60) = **$100**.
- **Pigouvian tax** = MEC = **$20 per unit**. Output falls to Q = 30; buyers pay $70, sellers keep $50, and tax revenue = 20 × 30 = $600.

#### Worked example 2 (constructed): positive externality and a Pigouvian subsidy (flu shots)
Setup: demand (MPB) is P = 80 − Q. The marginal external benefit is $20, so MSB is P = 100 − Q. Supply (MC) is P = 20 + Q.
- **Market outcome:** 80 − Q = 20 + Q → **Q_m = 30, P = $50**.
- **Social optimum:** 100 − Q = 20 + Q → **Q\* = 40**.
- **Deadweight loss** = ½ × 10 × (MSB at 30 − MC at 30) = ½ × 10 × (70 − 50) = **$100**.
- **Subsidy of $20 per shot:** output rises to Q = 40. Sellers receive $60, buyers pay $40 net, and the subsidy costs 20 × 40 = $800.

#### Worked example 3 (constructed): regulation vs tax vs tradable permits
Setup: firms A and B each emit 60 tons, and the target is 60 tons in total (a 60-ton cut). A's marginal abatement cost is a constant $10/ton; B's is a constant $40/ton.
- **Uniform standard** (each firm cuts 30 tons): cost = 30 × $10 + 30 × $40 = **$1,500**.
- **Emissions tax of $25/ton:**
  - A cuts all 60 tons, because $10 < $25. B cuts nothing, because $40 > $25, and pays 60 × $25 = $1,500 in tax.
  - That tax is a transfer to the government, not a resource cost, so the abatement cost is **$600**.
- **Cap-and-trade** (30 permits per firm): A cuts all 60 tons and sells its 30 permits to B at any price between $10 and $40, so both gain. Abatement cost = **$600**.
- **Why the market tools win:** they achieve the same total cut $900 more cheaply, because the cheapest abater does the cutting. This is the OpenStax logic.
- **Tax vs permits:** a tax fixes the *price* of pollution, so the amount of cutting is uncertain. A cap fixes the *quantity* of pollution, and the market sets the permit price.

#### Worked example 4 (constructed): the Coase theorem
Setup: a factory's waste costs a downstream fish farm $500 a year. A filter would cost $300 a year.
- If the fish farm owns the right to clean water, the factory buys the filter, because $300 is less than $500 in damages.
- If the factory owns the right to pollute, the fish farm pays the factory between $300 and $500 to install the filter.
- Either way the filter gets installed, which is the efficient outcome. The property right only decides **who pays**.
- Now suppose 5,000 households are harmed instead of one fish farm. Transaction costs — organizing everyone, free-riding, holdouts — block the bargain, which is the case for government action.
- Cheshire, Ohio was an exception: a buy-out was feasible because only about 221 residents were involved.

#### How to write up the Aral Sea in an exam answer
- **Negative externality:** upstream irrigators and planners paid a private cost for water far below its social cost (MPC < MSC). The costs fell on others: downstream fishing industries, residents breathing chemical-laden dust, and the regional climate. The result was over-use of water and too much irrigated cotton compared with the social optimum.
- **Common resource:** river water shared by several republics was rival (water used upstream never reaches the sea) but in practice not excludable. So it was over-used — a tragedy of the commons (see the public-goods section).
- **Why Coase fails here:** millions of affected people, several countries and central planning meant enormous transaction costs and unclear property rights.
- **Remedy in practice:** a government and international-finance project, the World Bank-funded Kok-Aral dike, partly reversed the damage in the North Aral.
- **For a balanced answer:** also note **government failure**. The original river diversion was a state plan, not a free-market outcome. This links to *Naked Economics* ch. 4.

#### Exam traps
- With a negative externality, market quantity is **too high** and price **too low**. With a positive externality, quantity is too low.
- A Pigouvian tax equals the **marginal** external cost at the optimal quantity, not the total damage.
- The goal is the **optimal** amount of pollution, not zero. In the OpenStax tax example, the firm still emits 20 of its 50 lbs.
- The Coase theorem needs two things: (i) clear property rights and (ii) low transaction costs. Who holds the right changes **who pays**, not the efficient outcome.
- A tax or subsidy on the activity causing the externality moves supply or demand onto the social curve. It corrects the market rather than distorting it.
- A competitive market can still be inefficient. Externalities are a **market failure**, unlike the efficient long-run competitive equilibrium in section (a).
- "Command-and-control" means regulation such as standards or bans. It works, but usually costs more than taxes or permits for the same amount of pollution cut.

## (b) Market failure II — Public goods (*Essentials* Ch. 9): non-rival and non-excludable, the four-way classification, free riders, the tragedy of the commons

### Takeaway
Classify any good with two yes/no questions:
1. Can people who don't pay be excluded?
2. Does one person's use leave less for others (is it rival)?

Each combination has its own typical problem:
- **Public goods** (non-excludable, non-rival) are under-provided by markets because people free ride, so they are usually paid for through taxes.
- **Common resources** (non-excludable, rival) are over-used — the "tragedy of the commons" — and need quotas, fees or property rights.
- **Artificially scarce or club goods** (excludable, non-rival) can be sold. But because the extra cost of serving one more user is near zero, charging a price leaves some valuable use unrealized.

### Cited Findings
- "Public goods have two defining characteristics: they are nonexcludable and non-rival" — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods):
  - **Non-excludable:** "it is costly or impossible to exclude someone from using the good." Even people who disagree with defense policy "cannot choose to be unprotected."
  - **Non-rival:** "When one person uses the public good, another can also use it." A pizza is different: "if Max is eating the pizza then Michelle cannot also eat it."
- Examples include national defense, fire and police services, and public health funding: "protecting some necessarily means protecting others, too" — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)
- **The free-rider problem** — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods):
  - People "have an incentive to let others pay for the public good and then to 'free ride' on the purchases of others." OpenStax models this as a prisoner's dilemma.
  - The fix: "if people come together through the political process and agree to pay taxes and make group decisions about the quantity of public goods, they can defeat the free rider problem."
- Private provision is sometimes possible. Radio stations sell advertising instead of charging listeners, and satellite radio makes broadcasts excludable through subscriptions — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)
- **Common resources** are "nonexcludable and rivalrous" — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods):
  - Fishermen tend to overharvest them (OpenStax's example is the queen conch).
  - Garrett Hardin's 1968 "tragedy of the commons": because "nobody owns the ocean," no individual has an incentive to protect it.
  - Remedies include fishing licenses, harvest limits and catch shares.
- A positive externality does not make something a public good. Education has "huge positive spillovers but can be provided by a private company" — [OpenStax §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)
- MRU videos: "Public Goods and Asteroid Defense", "A Deeper Look at Public Goods", "Club Goods", "The Tragedy of the Commons" — [MRU syllabus](https://mru.org/teacher-resources/principles-microeconomics-syllabus)
- Krugman & Wells' full *Microeconomics* has a separate chapter, "Public Goods & Common Resources" — [LoC TOC](https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/enhancements/fy1503/2014950828-t.html). *Essentials* covers it together with externalities in Ch. 9, "Externalities and Public Goods" (Ch. 10 in the 6th edition) — [Macmillan Learning](https://store.macmillanlearning.com/us/product/Essentials-of-Economics/p/1319330134)

### Inferences

#### The four-way grid (memorize)
| | **Rival** (my use leaves less for you) | **Non-rival** |
|---|---|---|
| **Excludable** | **Private good:** plov at a restaurant, a cotton shirt, a bus ticket | **Artificially scarce / club good:** a streaming subscription, satellite radio, cable TV, an uncongested toll road |
| **Non-excludable** | **Common resource:** fish in the sea, Aral Sea or river irrigation water, a congested free road, city air quality | **Public good:** national defense, a lighthouse, flood control, a fireworks show, asteroid defense, mosquito control |

What happens in each market:
- **Private goods:** markets work well.
- **Club goods:** they get provided, but charging a price above the near-zero marginal cost causes some inefficiency.
- **Common resources:** **over-used**.
- **Public goods:** **under-provided**, because of free riding.

Categories can change:
- Congestion makes a road rival, so a free road at rush hour becomes a common resource.
- Technology can make a good excludable, as satellite radio shows.

**The efficient amount of a public good.** Everyone consumes the same unit, so add up the individual marginal benefits and provide units until that sum equals marginal cost. Because no one can be charged according to their own valuation, taxes rather than prices pay for it. *(This is standard theory, not verified from a fetched source here — check Krugman Ch. 9.)*

#### Exam traps
- "Public good" refers to the **characteristics** (non-rival and non-excludable), not to "whatever the government provides." Governments provide many private goods, such as school places, and firms can provide public goods, such as advertising-funded radio.
- Vaccines and education are **positive-externality** goods (rival and excludable), not public goods. Their spillovers justify subsidies.
- Don't mix up the two problems: a common resource is **over**-used; a public good is **under**-provided.
- The tragedy of the commons is solved by making users bear the cost — through property rights, quotas, licenses, fees or catch shares. Simply adding "more market" with no rules does not fix it.
- Wheelan's black-rhino story (Ch. 2) is a tragedy of the commons fixed by giving local people a stake in the animals (see section c).

### Gaps
- Krugman's term for excludable, non-rival goods ("artificially scarce goods") was not confirmed from a fetched source. MRU and other textbooks call them "club goods". The task brief uses both terms, so check your textbook glossary.
- No fetched source covers the rule of adding up individual marginal benefits. It is standard theory, but verify the textbook's wording.

### Inferences

#### Practice set (constructed; Q3–Q7 and Q9 use Dilnoza's farm table in section (a))

**Q1 (multiple choice).** In Krugman's framework, which of these is **not** one of the two *necessary* conditions for perfect competition?
(a) Many producers, none with a large market share
(b) A standardized product
(c) Free entry and exit
(d) Both (a) and (b) are necessary

**A:** (c). Krugman lists two necessary conditions: many small producers and a standardized product. Free entry and exit is a third condition that is "often satisfied as well".

**Q2.** A wheat farm can sell as much as it wants at 3,000 soʻm/kg. What is its marginal revenue for the 101st kg, and what does its demand curve look like?

**A:** MR = P = 3,000 soʻm, because selling one more kilogram doesn't change the price. The farm's demand curve (which is also its MR curve) is a horizontal line at 3,000 soʻm.

**Q3.** The price is P = $34. How many boxes does Dilnoza produce, and what is her profit? Show the per-unit profit.

**A:** She produces Q = 6. The 6th box has MC $30 ≤ $34, but the 7th has MC $40 > $34. TR = $204, TC = $150, so profit = **$54**. Per unit: (P − ATC) × Q = ($34 − $25) × 6 = $54.

**Q4.** The price falls to $18. Should she produce in the short run? If so, how much, and what is her profit?

**A:** Yes, because $18 is above min AVC ($14). She produces Q = 4. TR = $72 and TC = $98, so profit = **−$26**. Shutting down would lose the full fixed cost of $40, so producing cuts the loss by $14 — that is, ($18 − $14.50) × 4 contributed toward fixed cost.

**Q5.** The price is $11. What should she do, and what is her loss?

**A:** Shut down, because $11 is below min AVC ($14). Her loss equals her fixed cost, **$40**. Even her best positive output (2 boxes) would lose $48.

**Q6.** State the break-even and shut-down prices. Then write the short-run supply schedule for P = $11, $14, $18, $24, $34 and $45.

**A:** Break-even price = min ATC = **$24**. Shut-down price = min AVC = **$14**. Quantity supplied at those prices: 0; 3 (indifferent with 0); 4; 5; 6; 7. This schedule is the MC curve above min AVC.

**Q7.** There are 100 identical farms and the price is $34. In a constant-cost industry, what happens in the long run to the price, output per farm, profit and the number of farms?

**A:** The $54 economic profit per farm attracts new farms, so industry supply shifts right and the price falls to min ATC, **$24**. Each farm then produces **5** boxes and earns **zero economic profit**. The number of farms grows until market demand at $24 is met. (If the price had stayed at $18 instead, farms would exit until it rose back to $24.)

**Q8 (true/false, explain).** "In long-run equilibrium, competitive firms earn zero profit, so their owners would be better off closing them."

**A:** False. Zero **economic** profit means revenue covers every cost, including the owner's opportunity cost. The owner earns exactly what they could earn elsewhere, which is a normal profit.

**Q9 (true/false, explain).** "A profit-maximizing competitive firm always produces where average total cost is lowest."

**A:** False. It produces where P = MC. That matches min ATC only when P = min ATC, which is long-run equilibrium. At P = $34, Dilnoza produces 6 boxes, not 5.

**Q10.** Demand is P = 100 − Q and private supply is P = 20 + Q. Each unit causes $20 of pollution damage to third parties. Find:
(a) the market quantity and price;
(b) the socially optimal quantity;
(c) the Pigouvian tax;
(d) the deadweight loss before the tax.

**A:**
(a) Q = 40, P = $60.
(b) MSC is P = 40 + Q, so Q\* = 30 (at P = $70).
(c) Tax = $20 per unit, equal to the marginal external cost. Buyers pay $70, sellers keep $50, and tax revenue is $600.
(d) DWL = ½ × 10 × $20 = **$100**.

**Q11.** Flu shots: MPB is P = 80 − Q, each shot gives other people $20 of external benefit, and MC is P = 20 + Q. Find the market quantity, the optimal quantity, the corrective policy and the deadweight loss.

**A:** The market quantity is 30, at $50. MSB is P = 100 − Q, so Q\* = 40. A **subsidy of $20 per shot** reaches Q\*: sellers get $60, buyers pay $40 net, and the subsidy costs $800. Without it, the market under-produces, with DWL = ½ × 10 × $20 = **$100**.

**Q12.** Firms A and B each emit 60 tons, and the government wants total emissions of 60 tons. A's marginal abatement cost is $10/ton and B's is $40/ton. Compare total abatement costs under:
(i) a uniform 30-ton cut for each firm;
(ii) a $25/ton tax;
(iii) 30 tradable permits for each firm.
Which is cheapest, and why?

**A:** (i) costs $1,500. (ii) costs $600: A cuts 60 tons and B pays the tax on its 60. (iii) costs $600: A cuts 60 tons and sells its 30 permits to B at $10–$40 each. The market tools are cheapest because the lowest-cost firm does the cutting, delivering the same pollution reduction at lower cost to society.

**Q13.** A factory's waste causes $500 a year of damage to one downstream fish farm. A filter costs $300 a year. If bargaining is costless, what happens under each assignment of property rights? What changes if 5,000 households are harmed instead of one fish farm?

**A:** If the fish farm has the right to clean water, the factory installs the filter, because $300 is less than $500. If the factory has the right to pollute, the fish farm pays it $300–$500 to install the filter. Either way the filter is installed (the efficient outcome); the rights only decide who pays — this is the Coase theorem. With 5,000 households, transaction costs and free-riding prevent a deal, so government action (a tax, a standard or permits) is needed — the same logic as the Aral Sea.

**Q14.** Classify each good and name the market problem:
(i) fish in the North Aral Sea;
(ii) Uzbekistan's national defense;
(iii) a film on a paid streaming service;
(iv) a plate of plov in a restaurant;
(v) a free Tashkent road at rush hour;
(vi) an Independence Day fireworks show;
(vii) a flu vaccination.

**A:**
(i) Common resource, so it is over-used.
(ii) Public good, so free riding leads to under-provision.
(iii) Artificially scarce or club good: excludable, but pricing it above its near-zero marginal cost limits use.
(iv) Private good, so the market works.
(v) Common resource, because congestion makes it rival; it is over-used.
(vi) Public good.
(vii) A **private good with a positive externality** (rival and excludable), so subsidize it. It is **not** a public good.

**Q15.** A mahalla of 200 households wants street lighting that costs $1,500. Each household values it at $10. Is it efficient to install? Will voluntary contributions pay for it? What would fix the problem?

**A:** The total benefit is 200 × $10 = $2,000, more than the $1,500 cost, so installing it is efficient. But the lighting is non-excludable and non-rival, so each household benefits whether or not it pays. Because of this free-rider problem, voluntary contributions fall short. The fix is compulsory financing through local taxes or fees decided collectively.
