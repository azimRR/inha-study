## 1. Price ceilings: binding vs non-binding, persistent shortage, Krugman's inefficiencies, deadweight loss, who gains and loses, why ceilings persist, real cases

### Takeaway
A price ceiling only matters (is **binding**) when it is set **below** the equilibrium price. Then quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. The **shortage persists** because the price is not allowed to rise, and the quantity actually traded falls to the quantity supplied. In surplus terms, a **rectangle is transferred** from producers to consumers and a **triangle is lost (DWL)**. Real-world costs are larger than the triangle because of Krugman's extra problems: inefficient allocation to consumers, wasted resources, inefficiently low quality, and black markets. Glaeser–Luttmer call misallocation costs "first order". Evidence from New York City, San Francisco and 1970s US gasoline matches the theory. Ceilings survive politically because their winners are visible, organised and sympathetic, while their losers are dispersed or invisible.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions and mechanics**
- Price control = "Laws that governments enact to regulate prices". Price ceiling = "A legal maximum price that one pays for some good or service". When a ceiling stops the price rising to equilibrium, "the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied, so there is a shortage." — [OpenStax 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-4-price-ceilings-and-price-floors)
- OpenStax's rent-control illustration: at the market rent of $600, 17,000 units are rented. Under the ceiling only 15,000 are rented, so the policy meant to help renters leaves "fewer apartments rented out under the price ceiling". — [OpenStax 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-4-price-ceilings-and-price-floors)
- Hugh Rockoff (Econlib): under a ceiling, queues become the rationing device. Black-market prices "may be above not only the official price but even the price that would prevail in a free market, because the buyers are unusually desperate and because sellers face penalties if their transactions are detected." — [Econlib, "Price Controls"](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)
- Quality cuts and evasion under US WWII controls: "fat was added to hamburger, candy bars were made smaller and of inferior ingredients, and landlords reduced their maintenance of rent-controlled apartments." Sellers also used tie-in sales: buying flour at the official price meant also buying unwanted rye or potato flour. — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)

**1970s US gasoline controls (and Nixon's controls)**
- "When the United States set maximum prices for gasoline in 1973 and 1979, dealers sold gas on a first-come-first-served basis, and drivers had to wait in long lines to buy gasoline." — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)
- Rockoff's arithmetic for 1979:
  - The controlled price was about $1.00/gallon; the uncontrolled price would have been about $1.20.
  - A driver buying 10 gallons nominally "saves" $2.00.
  - But 30 minutes in line, with time valued at $8/hour, costs $4.
  - So the true cost is $1.40/gallon, which is higher than the free-market price. — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)
- President Nixon imposed economy-wide wage-price controls from 1971 to 1973. — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)

**New York City**
- 2023 NYC Housing and Vacancy Survey: the net rental vacancy rate was 1.4% (Jan–mid-June 2023), with only about 33,000 apartments available. That is the lowest rate since 1968, one year before NYC introduced rent stabilization, which today restricts rents on more than 1 million apartments. — [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2024/02/08/survey-shows-nyc-housing-vacancy-rate-at-1-4-percent/)
- Vacancy among rent-stabilized units was 0.98% in 2023, down from 4.57% in 2021. The overall rate in 2021 was 4.54%. — reported in coverage of the HVS initial findings: [6sqft](https://www.6sqft.com/nyc-rental-vacancy-rate-drops-to-14-percent-lowest-in-50-years/); [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2024/02/08/survey-shows-nyc-housing-vacancy-rate-at-1-4-percent/) (these figures came from search-result summaries; the official HPD report was not opened)
- Glaeser & Luttmer (NBER WP 6220, 1997; *AER* 2003, 93(4): 1027–1046): "When there are binding price controls, there are shortages and the allocation of goods across consumers may not be efficient. In general, the misallocation costs of price controls are first order, while the classic welfare losses due to undersupply are second order." Their best estimate of the misallocation cost of NYC rent control is **about $200 per apartment per year**. — [NBER w6220](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6220)

**San Francisco: Diamond, McQuade & Qian (*AER* 2019, 109(9): 3365–3394)**
- Setting:
  - In 1979 San Francisco imposed rent control on standing buildings with 5 or more apartments. Small multi-family buildings were exempt.
  - A **1994 ballot initiative** removed that exemption for small multi-family buildings built before 1980. This created a quasi-experiment. — [Brookings (Diamond)](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-does-economic-evidence-tell-us-about-the-effects-of-rent-control/)
- Tenants:
  - Rent control "increased renters' probabilities of staying at their addresses by nearly 20%". — [NBER w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)
  - Five to ten years after the change, beneficiaries were "19 percent less likely to have moved".
  - Benefits were concentrated among older and long-tenure households, and were especially large for racial minorities. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-does-economic-evidence-tell-us-about-the-effects-of-rent-control/)
- Landlords:
  - Treated landlords "reduced rental housing supply by 15%". — [NBER w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)
  - Treated buildings were 8 percentage points more likely to convert to condos.
  - The number of renters living in rent-controlled units fell by 25 percentage points relative to 1994 levels.
  - Landlords also redeveloped buildings to exempt them from control. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-does-economic-evidence-tell-us-about-the-effects-of-rent-control/)
- Market-wide: the supply cut caused "a 5.1% city-wide rent increase". Renters without controlled units pay more. — [NBER w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)
- Policy conclusion: "If society desires to provide social insurance against rent increases, it may be less distortionary to offer this subsidy in the form of a government subsidy or tax credit." — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-does-economic-evidence-tell-us-about-the-effects-of-rent-control/); see also [NBER w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)
- Cambridge, MA, after rent control ended (Autor, Palmer & Pathak 2014, summarised by Diamond):
  - The market value of decontrolled properties rose 45%.
  - The value of the city's housing stock rose $2.0 billion between 1994 and 2004.
  - Only $300 million of that was the direct effect on formerly controlled units. The other $1.7 billion was spillover onto properties that had never been controlled. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-does-economic-evidence-tell-us-about-the-effects-of-rent-control/)

**Why ceilings persist politically**
- Rockoff gives three reasons:
  1. "The public does not always see the links between controls and the problems they create." For example, falling quality is blamed on business indifference.
  2. Controls "almost always benefit a subset of consumers who may have a particular claim to public sympathy and who, in any case, have a strong interest in lobbying for controls."
  3. In crises such as wartime inflation, controls may offer "a positive psychological benefit that outweighs the costs, at least in the short run." — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)
- Rent controls "do hold down the rent for those who already have an apartment when controls are instituted." — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)

**Local illustration: Uzbekistan's exchange-rate controls before September 2017**
- On 5 September 2017 the central bank set the dollar at 8,100 so'm, against the previous official rate of 4,210, a devaluation of almost half. Restrictions on buying foreign currency were abolished. — [RFE/RL](https://www.rferl.org/a/uzbekistan-devalues-currency/28718291.html); [Eurasianet](https://eurasianet.org/uzbekistan-returns-to-currency-convertibility-delivers-blow-to-black-market)
- The new rate was slightly above the going black-market rate of about 7,700 so'm per dollar. The reform was reported as a blow to the black market. — [Eurasianet](https://eurasianet.org/uzbekistan-returns-to-currency-convertibility-delivers-blow-to-black-market)

### Inferences
#### Binding vs non-binding: the rule
- **Ceiling below P\*** → binding. Shortage = Qd(Pc) − Qs(Pc). Quantity traded = Qs(Pc), the "short side".
- **Ceiling at or above P\*** → non-binding. The market stays at (P\*, Q\*), with no shortage and no DWL.
- A non-binding ceiling can **become binding** if demand rises or supply falls. My inference, consistent with Rockoff: gasoline controls bit in 1973 and 1979 when supply shocks pushed the market price up.
- The shortage is **persistent** because the force that would normally remove it, a rising price, is illegal.

#### Krugman's four problems, mapped to the cited evidence
| Krugman problem (ceilings) | What it means | Evidence above |
|---|---|---|
| Inefficient allocation to consumers | Units go to people who value them less than some who are shut out | Glaeser–Luttmer: misallocation is "first order" (~$200/apartment/yr in NYC). SF tenants become much less mobile (my inference: lock-in leads to mismatch) |
| Wasted resources | Time and effort spent searching or queuing | 1973/1979 gas lines; Rockoff's $1.40 effective price |
| Inefficiently low quality | Sellers cut quality because they cannot raise price | WWII fat in hamburger, smaller candy bars; landlords cut maintenance |
| Black markets | Illegal trades above the ceiling, sometimes above P\* | Rockoff on black-market prices; Uzbek "black rate" ~7,700 vs official 4,210 so'm/$ |

#### Worked Example A: a binding ceiling (own calculation)
**Market:** inverse demand P = 120 − Q; inverse supply P = Q. Equivalently, Qd = 120 − P and Qs = P.

1. **Free market.** 120 − Q = Q gives **Q\* = 60, P\* = $60**.
   - CS\* = ½·60·(120 − 60) = **1,800**
   - PS\* = ½·60·60 = **1,800**
   - Total surplus = **3,600**
2. **Ceiling Pc = $40.** This is below $60, so it binds. Qd = 80 and Qs = 40, so the **shortage = 40** and the quantity traded = **40**.
3. **At Q = 40:** the **demand price** is 120 − 40 = **$80**. The **supply price** is **$40**, equal to the ceiling.
4. **Surplus after the ceiling**, assuming the 40 units go to the buyers who value them most:
   - CS = trapezoid between demand and $40 over Q = 0 to 40 = ½·(80 + 40)·40 = **2,400**
   - PS = ½·40·40 = **800**
   - Total = **3,200**, so **DWL = 400**
5. **Area accounting.** Sketch the graph from these vertices:

| Area | Vertices (Q, P) | Size | What happens to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: rectangle | (0,40), (40,40), (40,60), (0,60) | 20 × 40 = 800 | **Transferred** from producers to consumers |
| B: triangle | (40,60), (40,80), (60,60) | ½·20·20 = 200 | **Lost** consumer surplus (part of DWL) |
| C: triangle | (40,40), (40,60), (60,60) | ½·20·20 = 200 | **Lost** producer surplus (part of DWL) |

- **Consumers:** +800 − 200 = **+600** (1,800 → 2,400).
- **Producers:** −800 − 200 = **−1,000** (1,800 → 800).
- **Society:** **−400**, which is B + C.

**DWL shortcut:** DWL = ½ × (Q\* − Q traded) × (demand price − supply price at Q traded) = ½ × 20 × (80 − 40) = **400**.
Using P\* − Pc = 20 as the height gives 200, which is wrong. This is a classic error: it counts only one of the two triangles.

#### Extension A2: inefficient allocation to consumers, in numbers (own calculation)
Suppose the 40 units are handed out at random (first come, first served, connections or luck) among the 80 people willing to buy at $40. Their valuations run evenly from $120 down to $40.
- The average recipient values a unit at **$80**, not the $100 average of the top 40 buyers.
- Consumer value falls from 4,000 to 3,200, an extra loss of 800.
- CS = 3,200 − 1,600 paid = **1,600**. That is *less* than the 1,800 consumers had before the control, so consumers as a group are now **worse off**.
- Total surplus falls by **1,200**, three times the textbook triangle. This is what "misallocation costs are first order" means.

#### Extension A3: black-market price and wasted resources (own calculation)
- The 40th buyer values the good at $80, so a black market could clear at up to **$80**. That is **above the free-market price of $60**, as Rockoff warns.
- If buyers must queue, time costs can absorb the whole $20 "saving". Compare Rockoff's gasoline arithmetic: $1.00 + $0.40 of waiting time = $1.40, which is more than the $1.20 market price.

#### Why ceilings persist: an exam paragraph
- **The winners** (sitting tenants, current buyers) are identifiable, organised and sympathetic.
- **The losers** are dispersed or invisible: future renters who cannot find a flat, landlords, and people who would have benefited from new building.
- **Many costs are not obviously traced to the control:** lower quality, less mobility, and higher rents on uncontrolled units (San Francisco's +5.1%).
- So voters see the benefit but not the cost (Rockoff's three reasons).

#### Reading the NYC, SF and Uzbekistan evidence carefully
- **NYC today mostly uses rent *stabilization*** (capped annual rent increases) for about 1 million units. This is a softer ceiling than old-style "rent control".
  - Very low vacancy among stabilized units (0.98%) is consistent with a binding ceiling.
  - But citywide low vacancy also reflects limited construction, so do not blame controls for all of it.
- **San Francisco shows both sides Krugman stresses:** incumbent tenants gain stability, while reduced supply and higher market rents hurt newcomers.
- **Uzbekistan before 2017 (my framing; numbers cited above):**
  - The official price of a dollar (4,210 so'm) was held far below the market-clearing level of about 7,700–8,100.
  - That is a **binding price ceiling on foreign currency**.
  - The predicted results followed: a shortage of dollars at the official rate, rationed access, and a black market.
  - Removing the ceiling closed the gap.

## 2. Price floors: persistent surplus, minimum wage and farm supports, Krugman's inefficiencies, the minimum-wage employment debate

### Takeaway
A floor only matters when it is set **above** equilibrium. Then Qs > Qd, giving a **persistent surplus**: unsold output, or in the labour market, unemployment. The quantity sold falls to Qd, a rectangle is transferred **from consumers to producers**, and a triangle is lost.
Krugman's floor problems are:
- inefficient allocation of sales among sellers;
- wasted resources;
- inefficiently high quality;
- illegal activity.

Farm supports show these problems clearly. The EU's "butter mountains" and "milk lakes" led it to introduce milk quotas (1984–2015). US dairy price supports left the government holding **560 million pounds of cheese** in 1981.
The minimum wage is the most tested floor, and the most contested:
- The simple model predicts job losses.
- The evidence is a real debate. Card–Krueger (1994) and Cengiz et al. (2019) find little or no job loss. Neumark–Shirley (2022) find "a clear preponderance of negative estimates". CBO (2021) projected −1.4 million jobs for a $15 federal minimum.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions and scale**
- Price floor = "The lowest price that one can legally pay for some good or service". A floor above equilibrium means quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, so there is a surplus. — [OpenStax 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-4-price-ceilings-and-price-floors)
- Farm supports are the biggest real-world floors. The EU "will spend about 58 billion euros per year, or 65.5 billion dollars per year" on agricultural support. High-income areas "spend roughly $1 billion per day in supporting their farmers". The US federal minimum wage has been $7.25 since 2009. — [OpenStax 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-4-price-ceilings-and-price-floors)

**EU "butter mountain" → milk quotas**
- European Commission: the milk quota regime was introduced in 1984 to address "the structural oversupply on the EU market that had led to the infamous 'milk lakes' and 'butter mountains'". Quotas ended on **31 March 2015** after 31 years. — [European Commission press release via PubAffairs Bruxelles](https://www.pubaffairsbruxelles.eu/eu-institution-news/milk-quotas-come-to-an-end-after-31-years-in-place/)

**US dairy price supports: "government cheese"**
- 22 December 1981: President Reagan released **30 million pounds** of surplus cheese, worth $43 million, from the Commodity Credit Corporation for needy people. That was only about 5% of the **560 million pounds** the government held.
  - The support program cost about **$2 billion a year**.
  - The price-support system required the government to buy cheese and other dairy products to keep prices from falling.
  - The report warned that the US could end up holding nearly half the world's cheese stock by the end of 1982. — [UPI, 22 Dec 1981](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1981/12/22/Reagan-frees-cheese-for-needy/5256377845200/); official statement: [Reagan Library](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/statement-about-distribution-cheese-inventory-commodity-credit-corporation)

**The minimum-wage employment debate**
- **Card & Krueger (*AER* 1994, 84(4): 772–793):**
  - On 1 April 1992 New Jersey's minimum wage rose from $4.25 to $5.05. Pennsylvania's stayed at $4.25.
  - They surveyed 410 fast-food restaurants in both states before and after the rise.
  - "Our empirical findings challenge the prediction that a rise in the minimum reduces employment. Relative to stores in Pennsylvania, fast food restaurants in New Jersey increased employment by 13 percent." — [NBER w4509](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4509)
- **Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (NBER WP 25434, 2019):**
  - They studied **138 state-level minimum-wage increases, 1979–2016**.
  - Method: compare the "excess jobs" paying at or just above the new minimum with the "missing jobs" paying below it.
  - Finding: the overall number of low-wage jobs remained "essentially unchanged over five years following the increase".
  - They found some evidence of reduced employment in tradable sectors. — [NBER w25434](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434)
- **Neumark & Shirley (*Industrial Relations* 2022, 61(4): 384–417):**
  - "There is a clear preponderance of negative estimates in the literature."
  - The evidence is stronger for teens, young adults and the less-educated, and stronger still for directly affected workers.
  - Studies of low-wage industries are "less one-sided". — [NBER w28388](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)
- **CBO (February 2021) on a $15 federal minimum by 2025:**
  - Employment falls by **1.4 million** (central estimate).
  - **900,000** fewer people in poverty.
  - Higher pay for about **17 million** workers, plus many of the 10 million earning slightly above $15.
  - Total pay rises by **$333 billion**. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/02/08/965483266/-15-minimum-wage-would-reduce-poverty-but-cost-jobs-cbo-says); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/02/08/raising-minimum-wage-to-15-would-cost-1point4-million-jobs-cbo-says.html)
- Rockoff names minimum-wage workers who keep their jobs among the vocal beneficiaries who sustain support for price controls. — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)

**Teaching cases**
- MRU's price-controls video series includes "Price Floors: The Minimum Wage" and "Price Floors: Airline Fares". — [EconEdLink / MRU](https://econedlink.org/resources/price-ceilings-and-price-floors-video-series-and-quiz/)

### Inferences
#### Krugman's floor problems, mapped to cases
| Krugman problem (floors) | What it means | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Inefficient allocation of sales among sellers | The sellers who make sales are not necessarily the lowest-cost ones | Workers willing to work for less cannot get jobs at the minimum wage (Extension B2) |
| Wasted resources | Surplus output is bought, stored or destroyed | EU butter mountains; 560 million lb of US government cheese, much of it deteriorating |
| Inefficiently high quality | Sellers compete on costly quality because they cannot cut price | Regulated airline fares led to lavish service (topic of the MRU "Airline Fares" video; I did not verify the details) |
| Illegal activity | Trades below the floor | Off-the-books jobs below the minimum wage (my inference) |

#### Worked Example B: a binding floor, same market as Example A (own calculation)
Demand P = 120 − Q; supply P = Q. P\* = $60, Q\* = 60, and CS\* = PS\* = 1,800.

1. **Floor Pf = $80.** This is above $60, so it binds. Qd = 40 and Qs = 80, so the **surplus = 40**. Quantity sold = **40**.
2. **At Q = 40:** demand price = $80; **supply price = $40**.
3. **Surplus after the floor:**
   - CS = ½·40·(120 − 80) = **800**
   - PS, assuming the lowest-cost sellers make the sales, = ½·(80 + 40)·40 = **2,400**
   - Total = 3,200, so **DWL = 400** = ½·(60 − 40)·(80 − 40)
4. **Who wins and loses:** the rectangle (80 − 60)·40 = **800 is transferred from consumers to producers**. Consumers lose 1,000 and producers gain 600.

#### Extension B2: inefficient allocation of sales among sellers (own calculation)
Suppose the 40 sales go at random to 40 of the 80 sellers willing to sell at $80, whose costs are spread evenly from $0 to $80.
- Average cost is then $40 instead of $20, an extra cost of 800.
- PS falls to 1,600, and the total loss becomes **1,200** (three times the triangle).

#### Extension B3: wasted resources when the government buys the surplus (own calculation)
This is the "price support" version: the government buys the 40-unit surplus at $80, at a cost of **$3,200**.
- Producers now sell 80 units, so PS = ½·80·80 = 3,200. CS = 800.
- If the stockpile is worthless (it rots or is destroyed), total surplus = 800 + 3,200 − 3,200 = **800**.
- The loss is therefore **2,800**, seven times the triangle.

This is the economics of butter mountains and government cheese. It is also why the EU switched to a **quantity control** (milk quotas) in 1984.

#### Minimum wage in numbers: the competitive model (own calculation)
- Labour demand (firms): Ld = 1,000 − 50W. Labour supply (workers): Ls = 50W − 200.
- Equilibrium: **W = $12, L = 400**.
- With a **minimum wage of $14**:
  - Firms hire **300** workers, but **500** want to work.
  - The **surplus of labour (unemployment) is 200**: 100 people who lost their jobs plus 100 newly attracted job-seekers.
  - DWL = ½·(400 − 300)·(14 − 10) = **200** (per hour).
- Remember: **workers are the sellers** and **firms are the buyers** in this market.

#### How to write the debate (exam paragraph)
1. **Competitive model:** a binding floor reduces employment and creates unemployment.
2. **Card–Krueger's natural experiment:** New Jersey fast-food employment rose 13% relative to Pennsylvania after New Jersey's increase.
3. **Large modern studies:** Cengiz et al. find low-wage job counts essentially unchanged after 138 state increases.
4. **Literature reviews:** Neumark–Shirley find a clear preponderance of negative estimates, especially for teens and the less-educated.
5. **Policy scorekeepers:** CBO's central estimate for $15 is −1.4 million jobs but 0.9 million fewer people in poverty, a trade-off.

**Conclusion:** modest increases appear to have small employment effects. Large increases relative to local wages are riskier. The size, and even the sign, of the effect is still debated.
The literature offers explanations such as employer market power (monopsony) and adjustment through hours or prices. These go beyond ch. 4 and were not sourced in this session.

## 3. Quantity controls (quotas and licenses): quota limit, demand price vs supply price, the wedge, quota rent = license value, DWL, NYC taxi medallions

### Takeaway
A **quota limit**, enforced by issuing **licenses**, caps quantity below equilibrium. At the quota quantity:
- the **demand price** (what buyers will pay for that quantity) is higher than
- the **supply price** (what sellers need to be paid to supply it).

That gap is the **wedge**. It equals the **quota rent** per unit, which goes to license holders, so a license's market price reflects (capitalises) the expected quota rents. The DWL is the triangle of mutually beneficial trades that the quota forbids.
NYC taxi medallions are the standard case:
- capped since 1937 (now 13,587);
- worth **over $1 million in 2014**;
- collapsed to **about $160,000 by 2016–18** once Uber and Lyft supplied rides outside the quota.

### Cited Findings
- NYC controls the number of taxi medallions under the Haas Act (1937). The number is "currently capped at 13,587". — [Columbia Human Rights Law Review](https://hrlr.law.columbia.edu/hrlr-online/distressed-drivers-solving-the-new-york-city-taxi-medallion-debt-crisis/)
- Medallion price history:

  | Year | Price |
  |---|---|
  | 1980 | ~$70,000 |
  | 1990 | ~$125,000 |
  | 2004 | just over $200,000 |
  | 2014 (peak) | over $1 million |
  | April 2015 | ~$600,000 |
  | 2016–2018 | ~$160,000 |

  — [Columbia HRLR](https://hrlr.law.columbia.edu/hrlr-online/distressed-drivers-solving-the-new-york-city-taxi-medallion-debt-crisis/)
- Press coverage puts the peak at about $1.3 million per medallion by 2014 and the trough at roughly $137,000–$150,000. — [Boston Globe via ABI](https://www.abi.org/feed-item/boston-globe-the-collapse-of-the-taxi-medallion-shakedown) (from a search-result excerpt)
- From Apr–Jun 2014 to Apr–Jun 2015, quarterly Uber rides rose from 2 million to 8 million while yellow-cab rides fell from 42 million to 38 million. — [Columbia HRLR](https://hrlr.law.columbia.edu/hrlr-online/distressed-drivers-solving-the-new-york-city-taxi-medallion-debt-crisis/)

  - Drivers' earnings fell by about $10,000 a year between 2013 and 2016.

- EU milk quotas (1984–2015) are a large quantity control. They were introduced to stop the oversupply behind the "milk lakes" and "butter mountains". — [European Commission via PubAffairs](https://www.pubaffairsbruxelles.eu/eu-institution-news/milk-quotas-come-to-an-end-after-31-years-in-place/)

UMN's practice-exam guide covers computing the quota price. — [UW–Madison Econ 101 HW2 answers (Spring 2019)](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~ekelly/econ101/answerstohomework2spring2019.pdf); [UMN Econ 1101 Midterm 1 guide](http://users.econ.umn.edu/~holmes/econ1101/practice_exams/Midterm1_2014_Guide.pdf) (descriptions from search results)

### Inferences
#### Vocabulary (Krugman's terms)
- **Quota limit:** the maximum quantity allowed. **License:** the right to sell a unit or operate, such as a taxi medallion.
- **Demand price** of a quantity: the price at which consumers demand exactly that quantity. Read up from Q to the demand curve.
- **Supply price** of a quantity: the price at which producers supply exactly that quantity. Read up from Q to the supply curve.
- **Wedge:** demand price − supply price at the quota quantity. This equals the **quota rent per unit**, which is what renting a license is worth per unit of use.
- A quota **at or above Q\*** is non-binding: no wedge, no rent, no DWL.

#### Worked Example C: a taxi quota (own numbers, textbook style)
**Market:** demand Qd = 20 − 2P; supply Qs = 2P. Q is in millions of rides per year; P is in $ per ride.

1. **Free market.** 20 − 2P = 2P gives **P = $5, Q = 10 million**.
2. **Quota of 8 million rides.**
   - Demand price = (20 − 8)/2 = **$6**.
   - Supply price = 8/2 = **$4**.
   - **Wedge = quota rent = $2 per ride.**
3. **Total quota rent** = $2 × 8M = **$16M per year**, which goes to license (medallion) holders. A driver without a medallion would pay up to $2 per ride to lease one.
4. **DWL** = ½ × (10 − 8)M × ($6 − $4) = **$2M per year**. These are rides worth $4–$6 to riders that would cost drivers less than that to supply, and that no longer happen.
5. **Surplus accounting:**

| | Free market | Quota of 8M |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | ½·10·(10 − 5) = $25M | ½·8·(10 − 6) = $16M |
| Producer surplus (supply side, excluding rent) | ½·10·5 = $25M | ½·8·4 = $16M |
| Quota rent (license holders) | 0 | $16M |
| **Total** | **$50M** | **$48M** → DWL $2M |

6. **A quota of 12 million rides** is above 10 million, so it is **non-binding**.
7. **Parallel with a tax:** a **$2 per ride excise tax** gives exactly the same outcome (Pd = $6, Ps = $4, Q = 8M). The $16M goes to the government instead of to license holders (see Section 4).

#### Why medallion prices rose and then crashed
- A medallion is an asset. Its price is roughly the present value of expected future quota rents.
- Illustration with **hypothetical numbers, not estimates from data:**
  - A rent of $50,000 per year at 5% interest gives a value of about $1,000,000.
  - If ride-hailing cuts the rent to $8,000 per year, the value falls to about $160,000.
- What happened:
  - Uber and Lyft added supply **outside** the quota: close substitutes.
  - Demand for medallion-cab rides fell. Uber rides rose from 2 million to 8 million a quarter while cab rides fell from 42 million to 38 million.
  - So the demand price at the quota fell, the wedge (quota rent) shrank, and medallion values collapsed.
  - Buyers who had borrowed to pay for future rents bore the loss, as the HRLR and NYT evidence documents.
- Quotas also create incentives for **illegal activity**, such as unlicensed cabs. This is the same logic as black markets under a ceiling.

## 4. Excise taxes: taxing producers vs consumers is equivalent, incidence depends on elasticities, revenue = tax × quantity, DWL / excess burden

### Takeaway
An excise tax drives a **wedge** equal to the tax between the price buyers pay (Pd) and the price sellers receive (Ps). Like a quota, it cuts quantity.
- **It makes no difference whether the law taxes buyers or sellers.**
- The **incidence** (who really pays) depends on **relative elasticities**: the less elastic side bears more.
- **Revenue = tax × the quantity after the tax.**
- The **excess burden (DWL) = ½ × tax × the fall in quantity.** It is larger when supply and demand are more elastic, and it rises with the **square** of the tax.

Real-world example: payroll taxes are legally split between employer and employee, but official analyses assign the burden to workers.

### Cited Findings
- "The analysis, or manner, of how a tax burden is divided between consumers and producers is called tax incidence." — [OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)
- "If demand is more inelastic than supply, consumers bear most of the tax burden, and if supply is more inelastic than demand, sellers bear most of the tax burden." — [OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)
- OpenStax examples:
  - Cigarettes (demand elasticity about 0.3): taxes are "mainly passed along to consumers in the form of higher prices".
  - Beachfront hotels (inelastic supply): the burden falls on sellers. — [OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)
- "The more elastic the demand and supply curves, the lower the tax revenue." — [OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)
- Khan Academy's hamburger example: a per-unit tax raises the price paid and lowers CS, PS and quantity. Tax revenue is the rectangle (tax × quantity), and the DWL is the triangle of units no longer traded. — [Khan Academy, "Taxation and dead weight loss"](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/microeconomics/consumer-producer-surplus/deadweight-loss-tutorial/v/taxation-and-dead-weight-loss)
- **Payroll taxes:**
  - They are legally split between employer and employee. Official distributional analyses, including CBO's, assign the whole burden to employees.
  - The usual reason given: employers can more easily hire fewer workers or substitute capital than workers can give up earnings by not working. In other words, labour supply is relatively inelastic.
  - Some recent evidence on firm-specific unemployment-insurance payroll taxes finds lower hiring but little pass-through to earnings, so this assumption is debated.
  - Sources: [CBO Working Paper 57089](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2021-06/57089-Payroll-Taxes.pdf); [Economic Report of the President 2004, ch. 4](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/ERP-2004/pdf/ERP-2004-chapter4.pdf); [arXiv 2304.05605](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.05605). These came from search-result excerpts; I did not check which statement comes from which document.

### Inferences
#### Worked Example D1: equal slopes (own calculation)
**Market:** demand P = 100 − Q; supply P = 20 + Q. This gives **Q\* = 40, P\* = $60**, with CS\* = ½·40·40 = 800 and PS\* = ½·40·40 = 800.
**Tax:** t = **$20 per unit, levied on sellers.** Supply shifts up to P = 40 + Q.

1. **New equilibrium.** 100 − Q = 40 + Q gives **Q = 30**. Buyers pay **Pd = $70**; sellers keep **Ps = $50**.
2. **Incidence.** Buyers pay $10 more and sellers get $10 less: **50/50**, because the slopes (and the elasticities at equilibrium) are equal.
3. **Revenue = $20 × 30 = $600** (the rectangle). **DWL = ½ × 20 × (40 − 30) = $100** (the triangle).
4. **Surplus changes.**
   - CS: 800 → 450, a loss of 350 = 300 (rectangle) + 50 (triangle).
   - PS: 800 → 450, a loss of 350 = 300 + 50.
   - Total loss = 700 = revenue 600 + **excess burden 100**.
5. **Tax size matters:**

| Tax | Quantity | Revenue | DWL |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 35 | 350 | 25 |
| $20 | 30 | 600 | 100 |
| $40 | 20 | 800 | 400 |

With linear curves, **doubling the tax quadruples the DWL**, while revenue less than doubles.

#### Worked Example D2: less elastic demand, same starting point (own calculation)
**Market:** demand P = 180 − 3Q; supply P = 20 + Q. Both pass through Q = 40, P = 60.
At that equilibrium:
- supply elasticity = 1 × 60/40 = **1.5**;
- demand elasticity = (1/3) × 60/40 = **0.5** in absolute value.

**Tax of $20:** 180 − 3Q − (20 + Q) = 20 gives **Q = 35**, **Pd = $75**, **Ps = $55**.
- Buyers bear $15 (**75%**) and sellers $5 (**25%**). The less elastic side, demand, pays more.
- Formula check: buyers' share = Es / (Es + |Ed|) = 1.5 / 2.0 = 0.75. Sellers' share = |Ed| / (Es + |Ed|) = 0.25.
- Revenue = 20 × 35 = **$700**, more than D1's $600. DWL = ½ × 20 × 5 = **$50**, less than D1's $100. **Less elastic means more revenue and less excess burden.**
- CS: 2,400 → 1,837.5, a loss of 562.5 = 525 + 37.5. PS: 800 → 612.5, a loss of 187.5 = 175 + 12.5.

#### Equivalence: taxing buyers instead of sellers (own calculation)
In D2, tax the **buyers** $20 instead.
- The demand curve as seen by sellers shifts down to P = 160 − 3Q.
- With supply P = 20 + Q, this gives Q = 35. Sellers get $55, and buyers pay $55 + $20 = **$75**.
- This is identical to taxing sellers. **Legal incidence is not economic incidence.**

#### Linear-curve shortcut for incidence
- Write the curves as demand P = a − bQ and supply P = c + dQ.
- Buyers' share of the tax = b/(b + d). Sellers' share = d/(b + d).
- The **steeper** curve at the equilibrium point is the less elastic side, and it bears more of the tax.

#### One market, four interventions (own calculation; Example A's market, demand P = 120 − Q, supply P = Q)
All four interventions cut quantity to 40 and create the same wedge: a demand price of $80 against a supply price of $40.

| Intervention | Buyers pay | Sellers get | CS | PS | Who ends up with the 40 × $40 = 1,600 wedge rectangle | DWL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling at $40 | $40 | $40 | 2,400 | 800 | **Consumers** | 400 |
| Floor at $80 | $80 | $80 | 800 | 2,400 | **Producers** | 400 |
| Quota of 40 | $80 | $40 | 800 | 800 | **License holders** (quota rent 1,600) | 400 |
| Excise tax of $40 | $80 | $40 | 800 | 800 | **Government** (revenue 1,600) | 400 |

- Compared with the free market (CS = PS = 1,800), the ceiling transfers 800 from producers to consumers, as in Example A.
- The ceiling and floor rows assume efficient allocation. With misallocation or wasted resources the loss is larger (Extensions A2, B2 and B3).
- **Exam insight:** the triangle is the same in all four cases. What differs is who captures the rectangle.

#### Other Krugman terms and special cases
- **Excess burden** of a tax = its DWL, the loss beyond the revenue raised.
- Taxes also have **administrative costs** (collection, compliance, avoidance). These are on top of the DWL triangle.
- **Special cases:**
  - Perfectly inelastic demand: buyers pay 100%, quantity is unchanged, and there is **no DWL**.
  - Perfectly inelastic supply (for example, land): sellers pay 100%, with no DWL.
  - Perfectly elastic supply: buyers pay 100%.

- Krugman's own numerical tax examples were not checked against the book.
- Empirical pass-through estimates for specific excise taxes (gasoline-tax holidays, cigarette or alcohol taxes) were not collected.
- Uzbekistan-specific excise taxes were not covered.

## 5. Exam traps: non-binding ceilings and floors, shortage vs surplus, DWL triangles, who bears a tax

### Takeaway
Most lost marks come from five mistakes:
1. putting binding ceilings and floors on the wrong side of equilibrium;
2. mixing up shortage and surplus;
3. using the wrong height for DWL triangles;
4. using the pre-tax quantity to compute revenue;
5. assuming the side the law taxes is the side that pays.

Check every answer against the formula sheet below.

### Cited Findings
- A ceiling is a legal **maximum** and a floor is a legal **minimum**. A ceiling below equilibrium causes a shortage; a floor above equilibrium causes a surplus. — [OpenStax 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-4-price-ceilings-and-price-floors)
- The tax burden falls more on the less elastic side, and more elastic curves raise less revenue. — [OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)
- Tax revenue is a rectangle; DWL is the triangle of units not traded. — [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/microeconomics/consumer-producer-surplus/deadweight-loss-tutorial/v/taxation-and-dead-weight-loss)
- Typical ECON 101 exams ask students to compute excess demand under a ceiling and the price under a quota. Homework keys compute license-holder revenue and DWL. — [UMN Econ 1101 guide](http://users.econ.umn.edu/~holmes/econ1101/practice_exams/Midterm1_2014_Guide.pdf); [UW–Madison HW keys](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~ekelly/econ101/answerstohomework3fall2017.pdf)

### Inferences
#### The traps
1. **"Ceiling above or floor below equilibrium."** A ceiling **above** P\* or a floor **below** P\* is **non-binding**, and nothing changes. Memory hook: a ceiling has to be *low* enough to hit you, and a floor has to be *high* enough to hold you up.
2. **Shortage vs surplus.**
   - Ceiling → shortage (Qd > Qs).
   - Floor → surplus (Qs > Qd). In the labour market, the surplus is unemployment.
3. **Quantity traded is the short side.**
   - Ceiling: Q = Qs(Pc), not Qd.
   - Floor: Q = Qd(Pf), not Qs, unless the government buys the rest.
4. **DWL height.** The height is demand price − supply price **at the traded quantity**, not P\* − Pc.
   - Example A: correct DWL = ½·20·40 = 400. Wrong DWL = ½·20·20 = 200.
5. **"Consumers always gain from a ceiling."** They gain the rectangle but lose a triangle. With misallocation, queues and quality cuts they can lose overall (Extension A2: CS 1,600 < 1,800).
6. **"Producers always gain from a floor."** They gain the rectangle but lose a triangle, and if sales are misallocated their gain shrinks or disappears (Extension B2).
7. **Who pays a tax.** It is not the side the law names; it is the **less elastic** side. Payroll tax: legally 50/50, economically mostly paid by workers.
8. **Revenue uses the new quantity.** Revenue = tax × **new** quantity. In D1 that is 20 × 30 = 600, not 20 × 40 = 800.
9. **Excess burden is not revenue.** The total fall in CS + PS = revenue + DWL. In D1: 700 = 600 + 100.
10. **DWL and tax size.** DWL grows with the **square** of the tax. With linear curves, doubling the tax roughly quadruples the DWL.
11. **Perfectly inelastic means no DWL.** If demand or supply is perfectly inelastic, quantity does not change, so DWL is zero. All of the burden falls on the inelastic side.
12. **Quota rent.**
    - Rent per unit = demand price − supply price at the quota.
    - Total rent = rent per unit × quota quantity.
    - The price of a license (medallion) reflects these rents.
    - A quota at or above Q\* is non-binding.
13. **Quality direction.** A ceiling leads to *inefficiently low* quality; a floor leads to *inefficiently high* quality.
14. **A black-market price can exceed P\*.** Example A3: $80 > $60.
15. **Labelling the labour market.** Firms are the **demand** for labour; workers are the **supply** of labour.
16. **Shift the right curve, the right way.**
    - A tax on sellers shifts **supply up by the amount of the tax** (a vertical shift), not to the right.
    - A tax on buyers shifts **demand down by the amount of the tax**.

#### Formula sheet (linear curves)
- **Shortage** (ceiling) = Qd(Pc) − Qs(Pc). **Surplus** (floor) = Qs(Pf) − Qd(Pf).
- **Wedge** at quantity Q̄ = Pd(Q̄) − Ps(Q̄).
  - Ceiling: Ps equals the ceiling.
  - Floor: Pd equals the floor.
  - Quota: the wedge is the quota rent.
  - Tax: the wedge is the tax t.
- **DWL** = ½ × (Q\* − Q̄) × wedge.
- **Transfer rectangle** relative to the free market:
  - ceiling: (P\* − Pc) × Qs(Pc);
  - floor: (Pf − P\*) × Qd(Pf).
- **Quota rent** = wedge × quota. **Tax revenue** = t × Q after the tax.
- **Buyers' share of a tax** = Es/(Es + |Ed|) = b/(b + d), where b and d are the slopes of the inverse demand and supply curves.

**Q17 (Graph reading).** For the $30 ceiling in Q6, name these areas and say which are DWL:

- (ii) the triangle (40,35)–(40,40)–(50,35);
- (iii) the triangle (40,30)–(40,35)–(50,35).

**Answer:**
- (i) = 200, **transferred** from producers to consumers.
- (ii) = 25, lost consumer surplus.
- (iii) = 25, lost producer surplus.
- **DWL = (ii) + (iii) = 50.**

---

**Q18 (Numeric + debate).** Labour demand Ld = 1,000 − 50W, labour supply Ls = 50W − 200, and a minimum wage of $14. Find employment, the labour surplus and the change in employment. Then evaluate: "All economists agree that minimum wages cause large job losses."

**Answer:**
- Equilibrium: W = 12 and L = 400.
- At $14, employment is **300** and 500 people want work, so **unemployment (the surplus) is 200**. Employment falls by **100**.
- The statement is **false**. The evidence is debated:
  - Card–Krueger: New Jersey employment rose 13% relative to Pennsylvania.
  - Cengiz et al.: low-wage jobs were essentially unchanged.
  - Neumark–Shirley: a clear preponderance of negative estimates, especially for teens.

---

**Q19 (Trap).** A student computes the DWL of the $30 ceiling as ½ × (50 − 40) × (35 − 30) = 25. What is wrong?

**Answer:** the height must be the wedge at Q = 40: demand price $40 − supply price $30 = $10. So **DWL = 50**. The student counted only the producer triangle.

---

**Q20 (Numeric).** In the practice market, the tax rises from $10 to $20. Find the new Q, Pd, Ps, revenue and DWL.

**Answer:**
- 50 − Q = 20, so **Q = 30**, **Pd = $45** and **Ps = $25**.
- Revenue = 20 × 30 = **$600**, up from $400 (less than double).
- **DWL = ½·20·20 = 200**, four times the previous 50.
