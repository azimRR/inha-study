## 1. Krugman's 12 principles: wording, explanation, memorable example, and how each is tested

### Takeaway
Krugman groups chapter 1 into three sets:
- **Individual choice (4 principles):** scarcity, opportunity cost, decisions at the margin, incentives.
- **Interaction (5 principles):** gains from trade, equilibrium, efficiency (vs equity), markets are usually efficient, and government can improve welfare when markets fail.
- **Economy-wide (3 principles):** one person's spending is another's income; total spending can get out of line with productive capacity (recession or inflation); government policy can change spending.

Exams usually test these in three ways:
1. "Which principle does this story illustrate?"
2. Computing an opportunity cost correctly: forgone earnings count; unavoidable and sunk costs do not.
3. Marginal decisions: do more while MB ≥ MC, and hire while wage ≤ P × MPL.

### Cited Findings

**Exact wording (Krugman & Wells *Economics/Microeconomics* 3e, Worth slides)**
- Individual-choice principles: "1. Resources are scarce. 2. The real cost of something is what you must give up to get it. 3. 'How much?' is a decision at the margin. 4. People usually take advantage of opportunities to make themselves better off." - Interaction principles: "1. There are gains from trade. 2. Markets move toward equilibrium. 3. Resources should be used as efficiently as possible to achieve society's goals. 4. Markets usually lead to efficiency. 5. When markets don't achieve efficiency, government intervention can improve society's welfare." — [Krugman & Wells 3e Ch.1 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- Economy-wide principles: "#10: One person's spending is another person's income. #11: Overall spending sometimes gets out of line with the economy's productive capacity. #12: Government policies can change spending." — [Krugman & Wells 3e Ch.1 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- Later-edition wording used in flashcard sets for newer Krugman texts (snippet):
  - 1: "People must make choices because resources are scarce."
  - 2: "The opportunity cost of an item—what you must give up in order to get it—is its true cost."

**Principle-by-principle definitions and Krugman's own examples**
- **P1 Scarcity.** "A resource is anything that can be used to produce something else" (land, labor, capital). Resources are scarce when "the quantity available isn't large enough to satisfy all productive uses" (petroleum, lumber, intelligence). — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P2 Opportunity cost.**
  - "The real cost of an item is its opportunity cost: what you must give up in order to get it". "All costs are ultimately opportunity costs."
  - Example: the cost of attending an economics class is what you give up to be there (sleep, TV, rock climbing, work).
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - "Got a Penny?": sixty years ago a penny was worth about 30 seconds of work; today it is slightly more than 2 seconds. "The rising opportunity cost of time in terms of money has turned a penny from a useful coin into a nuisance." — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - "A Woman's Work": 6% of married women worked for pay outside the home in 1900, versus about 60% by 2005. Home appliances, especially washing machines, lowered the opportunity cost of working outside the home. — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - **True cost of college (NY Fed, 2024 estimates):**
    - Direct costs are "tuition, fees, books, and supplies but do not include room and board, as those costs must be incurred whether one attends college or not." Average net direct cost for a four-year degree is about $30,000.
    - Opportunity cost is "the value of what one must give up while attending college, which essentially translates into forgone wages." It is measured by "the median wage earned by a high school graduate during the first four years of employment—a figure totaling $150,000 in 2024."
    - Total cost rose "from around $140,000 in the late 1990s to $180,000 in 2024". The estimated return to college is 12.5%.
    
    — [NY Fed Liberty Street Economics, "Is College Still Worth It?" (Apr 2025)](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2025/04/is-college-still-worth-it/)
  - MRU (Tyler Cowen) defines opportunity cost as "the value of your next best alternative" (snippet). — [MRU: Opportunity Cost lesson](https://learn.mru.org/lessons/opportunity-cost-definition)
- **P3 The margin.**
  - "Decisions about whether to do a bit more or a bit less of an activity are marginal decisions." Marginal analysis means "comparing the costs and benefits of doing a little bit more of an activity versus doing a little bit less."
  - Examples: "Hiring one more worker, studying one more hour, eating one more cookie, buying one more CD".
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - **Hiring rule (marginal analysis applied to labor):**
    - MPL = "the additional output a firm produces by adding one more worker hour". In perfectly competitive markets, VMPL = MPL × output price.
    - Firms hire "up to the point where: Wmkt = VMPL". A profit-maximizing firm "will never pay more (in terms of wages and benefits) for a worker than the value of their marginal productivity to the firm."
    - Table: workers 1–4 have MPL 4, 3, 2, 1. At a price of $4, VMPL = $16, $12, $8, $4. At a wage of $8 the firm hires 3 workers.
    - With market power, the firm uses MRP = MPL × marginal revenue instead.
    
    — [OpenStax 3e §14.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/14-1-the-theory-of-labor-markets)
- **P4 Incentives.** "An incentive is anything that offers rewards to people who change their behavior." Examples:
  - Gasoline prices rise, so people buy more fuel-efficient cars.
  - More well-paid jobs for economics graduates lead more students to major in economics.
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - Roland Fryer's 2007–2008 cash-incentive study ran in four districts:
    - New York: students were paid for test scores.
    - Chicago: paid for grades.
    - Washington D.C.: paid for attendance and behavior as well as grades.
    - Dallas: second-graders were paid per book read.
    
    Lesson: "How incentives are designed is very important". The link between effort and outcome and the speed of reward both matter. — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
  - "Boy or Girl? It Depends on the Cost": China's 1978 one-child policy. Births per woman fell from more than five in the 1970s to 1.8 by 2009. An unintended consequence was "too many 'unwanted girls.'" — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P5 Gains from trade.** "People can get more of what they want through trade than they could if they tried to be self-sufficient." The source is specialization: "The economy, as a whole, can produce more when each person specializes in a task and trades with others." Illustrated with the hunter/gatherer cartoon. — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P6 Equilibrium.** "An economic situation is in equilibrium when no individual would be better off doing something different." Examples:
  - A new checkout line opens at a busy supermarket.
  - Driving on the right was an equilibrium "long before it was the law": once established, the rule became "self-enforcing".
  - After the 1994 Los Angeles earthquake collapsed freeway bridges, traffic settled within weeks into "a new equilibrium, in which each commuter was making the best choice he or she could, given what everyone else was doing."
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P7 Efficiency vs equity.**
  - "An economy is efficient if it takes all opportunities to make some people better off without making other people worse off."
  - "Equity means that everyone gets his or her fair share. Since people can disagree about what's 'fair,' equity isn't as well-defined a concept as efficiency."
  - Example: handicapped-designated parking spaces. Equity favors them; efficiency objects when the spaces go unused.
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P8 Markets usually lead to efficiency.** The exception is market failure, defined on the slides as "the individual pursuit of self-interest found in markets makes society worse off". — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P9 Why markets fail.** There are three reasons:
  1. "Individual actions have side effects not taken into account by the market (externalities)".
  2. "One party prevents mutually beneficial trades from occurring in the attempt to capture a greater share of resources for itself" (market power).
  3. "Some goods cannot be efficiently managed by markets" (example: freeways in Los Angeles).
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)
- **P10–P12 (economy-wide).** The babysitting co-op illustrates them:
  - The co-op had too few tickets, so parents hoarded them. "One parent's decision to go out was another's chance to babysit", so tickets became hard to earn.
  - The co-op "finally solved its problem by handing out more tickets".
  - Chapter summary: "Spending below the economy's productive capacity, leads to a recession; spending in excess of the economy's productive capacity leads to inflation." Governments use their ability to affect overall spending "to steer the economy between recession and inflation."
  
  — [Krugman 3e slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)

#### Worked example 1: true cost of one year of college (my numbers; method from NY Fed)
- Data:
  - Tuition, fees, and books: $6,000.
  - Room and board: $5,000, which you would spend anyway on rent and food.
  - Best alternative: a full-time job paying $14,000 after tax.
- **Opportunity cost = $6,000 + $14,000 = $20,000.** Room and board is excluded because it is not caused by the choice.
- If the dorm costs $7,000 while living elsewhere would cost $5,000, add only the **$2,000 extra**, giving $22,000.
- Scale check against the NY Fed: forgone wages are $150k of $180k, or about 83% of the true cost of a degree. The biggest cost of college is usually the earnings you give up, not the tuition.
- Exam twist: a person with a very high-paying alternative (a star athlete, or someone with a big job offer) has a much higher opportunity cost of college. Leaving college can then be rational under Principle 2.

#### Worked example 2: marginal analysis (table version and calculus version)
- **Table.** Each extra study hour adds 10, 7, 4, then 2 exam points. You value a point at $3, and each hour costs $15 of forgone wages.
  - MB per hour is $30, $21, $12, $6. MC is $15.
  - **Study 2 hours.** The 3rd hour's MB ($12) is less than its MC ($15).
- **Calculus.** TB(Q) = 100Q − 2Q² and TC(Q) = 10Q + Q².
  - MB = TB′(Q) = 100 − 4Q. MC = TC′(Q) = 10 + 2Q.
  - Setting MB = MC gives **Q\* = 15**, where MB = MC = 40.
  - Net benefit NB(15) = 1,050 − 375 = **675**. NB(14) = NB(16) = 672, so 15 is the maximum.
  - Second-order condition: NB″ = −4 − 2 = −6 < 0, which confirms a maximum.
- Lesson: you maximize **net** benefit (TB − TC), not total benefit. At the optimum the **last** unit has MB = MC, but TB still far exceeds TC.

#### Worked example 3: "hire one more worker?" (the VMPL rule)
- **Discrete (OpenStax numbers).** With P = $4 and MPL = 4, 3, 2, 1, VMPL = $16, $12, $8, $4. At w = $8, hire 3 workers. The 3rd worker's VMPL equals the wage, so by convention you hire; the 4th worker's VMPL ($4) is below $8.
- **Calculus.** Let Q = f(L) = 20√L, with P = $5 and w = $25.
  - Profit is π(L) = P·f(L) − wL − FC.
  - The condition dπ/dL = P·f′(L) − w = 0 gives **w = P × MPL = VMPL**.
  - Here f′(L) = 10/√L, so VMPL = 50/√L. Setting 50/√L = 25 gives √L = 2, so **L\* = 4**.
  - Discrete check: the 4th worker adds 5.36 units × $5 = $26.79 > $25, so hire. The 5th adds 4.72 × $5 = $23.61 < $25, so stop.
- Rule in words: **hire one more worker if wage < price × marginal product of labor**. Because MPL diminishes, VMPL falls as L rises, and the stopping point exists.

#### Exam traps (principles)
1. Opportunity cost is the value of the **one** next-best alternative. Do not add up every alternative you gave up.
2. College: do not put room and board (unavoidable) into the opportunity cost, and do not forget forgone wages.
3. **Sunk costs** (already spent and unrecoverable) never enter a forward-looking marginal decision. This is a standard trap; it is not on the Ch.1 slides fetched here.
4. "Marginal" means the extra unit, not the average.
5. MB = MC at the optimum does **not** mean total benefit = total cost.
6. Efficiency ≠ equity. An efficient outcome can be unequal, and an equal one can be inefficient.
7. Equilibrium ≠ a "good" outcome. An equilibrium can be inefficient (for example, traffic congestion or pollution).
8. "Market failure" means **inefficiency** (externalities, market power, goods unsuited to markets). It does not mean "an outcome I dislike". Inequality is an equity issue.
9. Principles 10–12 are macro: recession ↔ spending < productive capacity; inflation ↔ spending > productive capacity.

#### Practice questions (answers follow each)
1. You skip a $40 shift to attend a free lecture, and the shift was your next-best option. What is the opportunity cost of the lecture? **Answer: $40.** A free ticket does not mean zero cost.
2. Which principle does each story illustrate?

   a. Hybrid-car sales rose when gasoline prices rose. **Answer: P4 (incentives).**
   
   b. Supermarket checkout lines end up about equally long. **Answer: P6 (equilibrium).**
   
   c. Disabled-parking spots are sometimes empty. **Answer: P7 (equity vs efficiency).**
   
   d. A factory ignores the smoke it imposes on neighbors. **Answer: P9 (an externality, so market failure).**
   
   e. Tourists spend less, hotel staff earn less, and the staff then spend less. **Answer: P10 (spending = income).**
   
   f. A government cuts taxes during a recession. **Answer: P12 (policy changes spending).**
3. P = $10. Successive workers have MPL of 8, 6, 5, 3, 2, and w = $40. How many workers should the firm hire?

   **Answer:** VMPL = 80, 60, 50, 30, 20. **Hire 3 workers**, because 50 > 40 but 30 < 40.
4. TB = 60Q − Q² and TC = 20Q. Find the optimal Q.

   **Answer:** MB = 60 − 2Q and MC = 20, so **Q = 20**. Then TB = 800, TC = 400, and NB = 400.
5. True or false: "A market in equilibrium is always efficient."

   **Answer: False.** Under market failure (for example, pollution), the equilibrium is inefficient (P8 and P9).
6. True or false: "Room and board is always part of the opportunity cost of college."

   **Answer: False.** Only the cost above what you would pay anyway counts.

## 2. The production possibility frontier (PPF): shape, points, efficiency, shifts, and computing opportunity cost

### Takeaway
- **What it is.** The PPF shows the maximum output combinations of two goods given resources and technology.
- **Points.** On the curve = efficient in production. Inside = inefficient (idle or misallocated resources). Outside = unattainable for production now.
- **Slope.** The slope (in absolute value) is the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis, also called the marginal rate of transformation: MRT = −dy/dx.
- **Shape.** A straight line means constant opportunity cost. Bowed out (concave) means increasing opportunity cost, because resources are specialized. Bowed in means decreasing opportunity cost, as with increasing returns or learning by doing.
- **Shifts.** More resources or better technology shift the frontier outward (economic growth). Technical progress in only one good pivots it outward along that good's axis.

### Cited Findings
- Krugman/Wells chapter-2 slides describe the PPF as illustrating "the trade-offs facing an economy that produces only two goods". It shows "the maximum quantity of one good that can be produced for any given production of the other." — [Krugman/Wells Ch.2 "Economic Models: Trade-offs and Trade" slides (SlideServe upload)](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)
- "Society can choose any combination of the two goods on or inside the PPF. However, it does not have enough resources to produce outside the PPF." — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- A budget constraint is a straight line whose slope comes from relative prices. The PPF "has a curved shape because of the law of the diminishing returns". — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- "The law of increasing opportunity cost ... holds that as production of a good or service increases, the marginal opportunity cost of producing it increases as well."
  - Example: shifting resources from health care into education. The first resources moved produce big education gains at little health cost. Near all-education, more education requires giving up doctors, nurses, and emergency rooms.
  
  — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- **Productive efficiency:** "Given the available inputs and technology, it is impossible to produce more of one good without decreasing the quantity that is produced of another good." All points on the PPF satisfy it; an interior point R is inefficient. — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- **Allocative efficiency:** "The particular combination of goods and services on the production possibility curve that a society produces represents the combination that society most desires." Only one productively efficient point is allocatively efficient. — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- On growth, OpenStax names three routes: using resources less wastefully; resource growth ("As resources grow over a period of years (e.g., more labor and more capital), the economy grows"); and better technology ("Improvements in technology can increase the level of production with given resources, and hence push out the PPF"). — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
- The MRT is "the slope of the production–possibility frontier (PPF) at any given point". It measures "how much of good Y is given up for one more unit of good X" (the opportunity cost). — [Wikipedia: Production–possibility frontier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- **Why the PPF is concave.** As an economy specializes more in one product, "the opportunity cost of producing that product increases, because we are using more and more resources that are less efficient in producing it". In the guns-and-butter example, the "least qualified (or most general)" gun workers move to butter first. — [Wikipedia: PPF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- **When it is straight.** "If opportunity costs are constant, a straight-line (linear) PPF is produced". This happens when "resources are not specialised and can be substituted for each other with no added cost." — [Wikipedia: PPF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- **Outward and inward shifts.** Outward shifts come from "growth of the availability of inputs, such as physical capital or labour, or from technological progress". Inward shifts happen if "the labour force shrinks, the supply of raw materials is depleted, or a natural disaster decreases the stock of physical capital". — [Wikipedia: PPF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- **Biased technical change.** It "shifts the PPF outwards more along the favored good's axis". — [Wikipedia: PPF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- **Inside and outside points.** Inside points are inefficient: "the economy can produce more of at least one good without sacrificing the production of any other good". Outside points "cannot be produced using currently available resources". Not every frontier point is Pareto efficient; that also requires MRT to equal consumers' marginal rate of substitution. — [Wikipedia: PPF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)
- A bowed-out (concave) curve represents increasing opportunity cost, a bowed-in (convex) curve decreasing opportunity cost, and a straight line constant opportunity cost (snippet). — [Khan Academy: PPCs for increasing, decreasing and constant opportunity cost](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/class-12-microeconomics/x3ff2523b5b42c590:introduction/x3ff2523b5b42c590:production-possibilities-frontier/v/ppcs-for-increasing-decreasing-and-constant-opportunity-cost)
- "The slope of the production possibility frontier illustrates the opportunity cost of producing oil in terms of corn." — [OpenStax 3e §33.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/33-1-absolute-and-comparative-advantage)
- **Bowed-in PPF.** With increasing returns, "Opportunity cost one more fish falls as fish production increases (One reason: learning by doing)". Trade can then rest on increasing returns as well as comparative advantage. The lecture links this to Paul Krugman's work. — [Univ. of Minnesota Econ 1101 lecture 7(iii), T. Holmes](http://users.econ.umn.edu/~holmes/class/2010f1101/prelim_lec_7_iii_2page.pdf)

### Inferences

#### How to read any PPF question (procedure)
1. Identify which good is on the x-axis. The slope's absolute value is the opportunity cost of the **x-good**, measured in units of the y-good.
2. The opportunity cost of the y-good is the reciprocal: |Δx/Δy|.
3. Classify each point:
   - **On** the frontier: efficient in production.
   - **Inside**: inefficient, with unemployment or misallocation.
   - **Outside**: unattainable now. It becomes reachable through growth, and it can be *consumed* (not produced) through trade.
4. Decide whether a change is a **movement** (choosing a different mix, or reducing unemployment) or a **shift** (resources or technology change).

#### Worked example 1: linear PPF in intercept form, x/20 + y/5 = 1 (my derivation)
- **Rearrange:** y = 5 − x/4. The x-intercept is 20 (all resources in x) and the y-intercept is 5.
- **Slope:** dy/dx = −1/4.
  - Opportunity cost of 1 x = **1/4 y**.
  - Opportunity cost of 1 y = **4 x** (the reciprocal).
- **General rule:** for x/a + y/b = 1, OC(x) = b/a units of y and OC(y) = a/b units of x. In words: the opportunity cost of a good is (the other good's intercept) ÷ (its own intercept).
- **Point tests** (plug the point into the left side):

  | Point | Calculation | Value | Result |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | (8, 3) | 0.4 + 0.6 | 1 | **On** the frontier (efficient) |
  | (8, 2) | 0.4 + 0.4 | 0.8 < 1 | **Inside** (inefficient) |
  | (12, 3) | 0.6 + 0.6 | 1.2 > 1 | **Outside** (unattainable) |

- **Check:** moving from (8, 3) to (12, 2) gains 4 x and loses 1 y, so 1/4 y per x. ✓
- **Technology improves only in x**, doubling the x-intercept to 40. The frontier becomes x/40 + y/5 = 1.
  - OC(x) falls to **1/8 y**.
  - OC(y) rises to **8 x**.
  - **Trap:** a technical improvement in x raises the opportunity cost of y measured in x, because each unit of y now gives up more x.

#### Worked example 2: a bowed-out PPF table with increasing opportunity cost (my numbers)

| Point | Wheat (x) | Cloth (y) | Cloth given up for these 10 wheat | OC per 1 wheat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 | — | — |
| B | 10 | 95 | 5 | 0.5 cloth |
| C | 20 | 85 | 10 | 1.0 |
| D | 30 | 70 | 15 | 1.5 |
| E | 40 | 45 | 25 | 2.5 |
| F | 50 | 0 | 45 | 4.5 |

- The opportunity cost of wheat rises with each step, so the frontier is bowed out.
- Reason: the resources moved last (for example, land best suited to cloth) are the worst at producing wheat.
- An exam may ask for the opportunity cost of *cloth* between D and E. Answer: 10 wheat / 25 cloth = 0.4 wheat per cloth.

#### Worked example 3: the calculus view of PPF curvature (my derivation)
- **Circle frontier.** Take x² + y² = 100.
  - Implicit differentiation: 2x + 2y·(dy/dx) = 0, so dy/dx = −x/y and **MRT = x/y**.
  - At (6, 8), MRT = 0.75 y per x. At (8, 6), MRT = 1.33. The opportunity cost of x rises as x rises, so the curve is bowed out.
  - Second derivative: d²y/dx² = −(x² + y²)/y³ = −100/y³ < 0, so the curve is concave.
- **Parabola frontier.** Take y = 100 − x².
  - MRT = −dy/dx = 2x. At x = 3 the opportunity cost is 6 y per x; at x = 5 it is 10.
  - Discrete check from x = 3 to x = 4: y falls from 91 to 84, a loss of 7. That is the average of the point values 6 and 8. ✓
- **Linear frontier.** Here dy/dx is constant, so opportunity cost is constant.
- **Rule:** concave (y″ < 0) means increasing opportunity cost; convex (y″ > 0) means decreasing opportunity cost (increasing returns); linear means constant opportunity cost.

#### Shift or no shift? (synthesis of the cited sources)

| Event | Effect on the PPF |
|---|---|
| More workers, more capital, newly discovered resources | Outward shift (economic growth) |
| Better technology in **both** goods | Outward shift |
| Better technology in **one** good | Pivots outward along that good's axis only |
| Earthquake or war destroys capital; labor force shrinks | Inward shift |
| Unemployment falls (idle workers hired) | **No shift.** The point moves from inside toward the frontier |
| Consumers' tastes change | **No shift.** Society picks a different point (allocative choice) |
| Opening to trade | **No shift in production possibilities.** Consumption can go beyond the PPF |

#### Exam traps (PPF)
1. Reporting the slope as the opportunity cost of the **y-good**. The slope is the opportunity cost of the x-good; the y-good's opportunity cost is the reciprocal.
2. Treating "producing inside the PPF" as impossible. It is possible, just inefficient.
3. Calling a move from inside to the frontier "economic growth (a shift)". It is a gain in efficiency, not a shift.
4. Assuming every point on the PPF is equally "best". All of them are productively efficient, but only one is allocatively efficient (the mix society wants).
5. Forgetting that bowed out means **increasing** opportunity cost, and mixing up "bowed out" with "bowed in".
6. Measuring opportunity cost in money. On a PPF it is measured in units of the other good.
7. Believing points outside the PPF can never be reached. Growth makes them producible later, and trade makes them consumable now (see Tom & Hank in §3).

#### Practice questions (answers follow)
1. Country Z's PPF is 3x + 2y = 60.

   a. What is the maximum x? **Answer: 20.**
   
   b. What is the maximum y? **Answer: 30.**
   
   c. What is the opportunity cost of x? **Answer:** y = 30 − 1.5x, so 1.5 y per x.
   
   d. What is the opportunity cost of y? **Answer: 2/3 x per y.**
   
   e. Is (10, 10) efficient? **Answer: No.** 3·10 + 2·10 = 50 < 60, so it lies inside the frontier.
2. Which event pivots Z's PPF outward along the y-axis only?

   **Answer:** a technology improvement in the y industry only.
3. On the PPF y = 64 − x², find the opportunity cost of x at x = 2 and at x = 6. What shape is the PPF?

   **Answer:** MRT = 2x, so 4 at x = 2 and 12 at x = 6. Opportunity cost increases, so the PPF is bowed out.
4. True or false: "Lower unemployment shifts the PPF outward."

   **Answer: False.** The economy moves toward the frontier; the frontier itself does not move.

## 3. Comparative vs absolute advantage: method, worked problems (output and input tables), terms of trade, gains, and traps

### Takeaway
- **Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost**, not higher productivity. Absolute advantage = producing more per unit of input, or needing fewer inputs per unit.
- With two producers and two goods, each producer has a comparative advantage in one good unless their opportunity costs are equal. This holds even when one producer has an absolute advantage in both goods.
- Specialize according to comparative advantage.
- A mutually beneficial price for good X (in units of Y) lies **strictly between** the two producers' opportunity costs of X.
- The gains come from the extra total output that specialization makes possible. The terms of trade decide how those gains are split.

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions.**
  - "When a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country, we say that this country has a comparative advantage in that good." — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
  - "Comparative advantage is not the same as absolute advantage, which is when a country can produce more of a good." — [OpenStax 3e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)
  - Krugman/Wells chapter-2 slides: an individual has a comparative advantage when "the opportunity cost of producing the good is lower for that individual than for other people". An absolute advantage means the individual "can do it better than other people". Mutual gains come from specialization based on comparative, not absolute, advantage. — [Krugman/Wells Ch.2 slides (SlideServe)](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)

### Inferences

#### The 5-step method (works for every table problem)
1. **Identify the table type.**
   - **Output table:** units produced per period. Bigger numbers mean more productive.
   - **Input table:** hours or workers per unit. *Smaller* numbers mean more productive.
2. **Absolute advantage.**
   - Output table: whoever has the larger number for that good.
   - Input table: whoever has the smaller number.
3. **Opportunity-cost table.** Compute it for every producer and both goods.
   - **Output (Other Over):** OC of X = (output of Y) ÷ (output of X).
   - **Input (Other Under):** OC of X = (input per X) ÷ (input per Y). The *other* good's input goes underneath.
   - Check: each producer's two opportunity costs must be reciprocals of each other.
4. **Comparative advantage and specialization.** The lower opportunity cost of a good gives the comparative advantage in it. Each producer specializes in, and exports, its comparative-advantage good.
5. **Terms of trade and gains.**
   - Mutually beneficial price of X (in Y per X): **OC_low(X) < price < OC_high(X)**.
   - Gains: compare total output before and after specialization, or each person's consumption before and after trade.
   - The closer the price is to the **partner's** opportunity cost, the bigger **your** share of the gains.

#### Worked problem 1: input table (OpenStax 33.1, completed with terms of trade)

| Hours per unit | Oil (barrel) | Corn (bushel) |
|---|---|---|
| Saudi Arabia (SA) | 1 | 4 |
| United States (US) | 2 | 1 |

- **Opportunity cost of oil (input: other under).** SA = 1/4 = **¼ corn**. US = 2/1 = **2 corn**.
- **Opportunity cost of corn.** SA = 4 oil. US = ½ oil.
- **Absolute advantage:** SA in oil (1 hr < 2 hr), US in corn (1 hr < 4 hr).
- **Comparative advantage:** SA in oil (¼ < 2), US in corn (½ < 4). Here absolute and comparative advantage coincide.
- **Terms of trade for 1 barrel:** between ¼ and 2 bushels of corn. For example, 1 barrel for 1 bushel benefits both:
  - SA receives 1 corn per barrel, worth more than the ¼ corn it costs SA to make a barrel.
  - The US pays 1 corn per barrel, less than the 2 corn it would cost the US to make one.

#### Worked problem 2: output table (Krugman's Tom & Hank; numbers as reported, arithmetic verified)

| Max per week | Fish | Coconuts | OC of 1 fish | OC of 1 coconut |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tom | 40 | 30 | 30/40 = **¾ coconut** | 40/30 = 4/3 fish |
| Hank | 10 | 20 | 20/10 = **2 coconuts** | 10/20 = **½ fish** |

- **Absolute advantage:** Tom in both goods.
- **Comparative advantage:** Tom in fish (¾ < 2); Hank in coconuts (½ < 4/3).
- **Autarky (both points lie on their PPFs).**
  - Tom's PPF is C = 30 − ¾F; at F = 28, C = 9. ✓
  - Hank's PPF is C = 20 − 2F; at F = 6, C = 8. ✓
  - Totals: 34 fish and 17 coconuts.
- **After specialization:** Tom catches 40 fish and Hank gathers 20 coconuts. Totals: 40 fish and 20 coconuts, which is **+6 fish and +3 coconuts**.
- **The trade:** 10 fish for 10 coconuts, a price of 1 coconut per fish, inside the range (¾, 2).
  - Tom ends with 30 fish and 10 coconuts, a gain of +2 fish and +1 coconut.
  - Hank ends with 10 fish and 10 coconuts, a gain of +4 fish and +2 coconuts.
- **Both now consume outside their own PPFs.**
  - Tom: 30/40 + 10/30 = 1.083 > 1.
  - Hank: 10/10 + 10/20 = 1.5 > 1.
  - This is how trade lets people consume beyond their production possibilities.
- **Price tests for 1 fish:**
  - 1.5 coconuts: yes, since it lies between ¾ and 2.
  - 2.5 coconuts: no. Hank would rather catch his own fish at 2.
  - 0.5 coconut: no. Tom would rather keep his fish, since giving one up costs him only ¾ coconut of production.

#### Worked problem 3: absolute advantage in both goods, input-style data (OpenStax 33.2 verified)
- **Opportunity cost of a shoe:** US = 40,000/10,000 = **4 refrigerators**; Mexico = 10,000/8,000 = **1.25 refrigerators**.
- **Opportunity cost of a refrigerator:** US = **0.25 shoes**; Mexico = 0.8 shoes.
- **Comparative advantage:** Mexico in shoes, the US in refrigerators, even though the US is more productive at both.
- **The OpenStax trade:** 4,000 refrigerators for 1,800 shoes, a price of about 2.22 refrigerators per shoe. This lies inside the range (1.25, 4).
  - US consumption: 5,300 shoes and 22,000 refrigerators, up from 5,000 and 20,000.
  - Mexico's consumption: 4,200 shoes and 6,500 refrigerators, up from 4,000 and 5,000.
- **Two kinds of bounds** (do not mix them up):
  - The OpenStax quantity bounds ("export fewer than 6,000 refrigerators") depend on the chosen production point.
  - The **price** bounds (between 1.25 and 4 refrigerators per shoe) depend only on opportunity costs.

#### Worked problem 4: my output table, absolute advantage in both goods

| Max per year (all resources in one good) | Cotton (bales) | Apricots (crates) |
|---|---|---|
| Uzbekistan (UZ) | 60 | 30 |
| Kyrgyzstan (KG) | 20 | 25 |

- **Absolute advantage:** UZ in both goods (60 > 20 and 30 > 25).
- **Opportunity cost of cotton:** UZ = 30/60 = **0.5 apricots**; KG = 25/20 = **1.25 apricots**.
- **Opportunity cost of apricots:** UZ = 2 cotton; KG = **0.8 cotton**.
- **Comparative advantage:** UZ in cotton, KG in apricots.
- **Autarky (on their PPFs).** UZ produces 40 cotton and 10 apricots (40/60 + 10/30 = 1). KG produces 12 cotton and 10 apricots (12/20 + 10/25 = 1). Totals: 52 cotton and 20 apricots.
- **After specialization:** UZ 60 cotton, KG 25 apricots. Totals: 60 cotton and 25 apricots, which is **+8 cotton and +5 apricots**.
- **The trade:** UZ exports 16 cotton for 14 apricots, a price of 0.875 apricots per cotton, inside the range (0.5, 1.25).
  - UZ consumes 44 cotton and 14 apricots, up from 40 and 10.
  - KG consumes 16 cotton and 11 apricots, up from 12 and 10.
  - Both are better off in both goods.

#### Worked problem 5: my input (hours) table, with the inversion trap

| Hours to produce one | Laptop repair | Website |
|---|---|---|
| Dilnoza | 2 | 6 |
| Timur | 4 | 8 |

- **Absolute advantage:** Dilnoza in both, since she needs fewer hours for each.
- **Opportunity cost of a repair (input: other under).** Dilnoza = 2/6 = **⅓ website**. Timur = 4/8 = **½ website**. So Dilnoza has the comparative advantage in repairs.
- **Opportunity cost of a website.** Dilnoza = 6/2 = 3 repairs. Timur = 8/4 = **2 repairs**. So Timur has the comparative advantage in websites.
- **The trap:** using the output formula on this input table gives repair OCs of 6/2 = 3 for Dilnoza and 8/4 = 2 for Timur. A student would then wrongly pick Timur for repairs. Always check whether the numbers are hours (input) or units (output).
- **As output data:** with 48 hours each, Dilnoza can make 24 repairs or 8 websites, and Timur 12 repairs or 6 websites. The output formula then gives the same answers (8/24 = ⅓ and 6/12 = ½).
- **Gains.**
  - Autarky with 24 hours on each task: Dilnoza makes 12 repairs and 4 websites; Timur 6 repairs and 3 websites. Totals: 18 repairs and 7 websites.
  - Specialization: Timur makes 6 websites. Dilnoza makes 1 website (6 hours) and 21 repairs (42 hours). Totals: **21 repairs and 7 websites**, which is **+3 repairs**.
- **The trade:** Timur sells 3 websites for 7.5 repairs, a price of 2.5 repairs per website, inside the range (2, 3).
  - Timur consumes 7.5 repairs and 3 websites, a gain of +1.5 repairs.
  - Dilnoza consumes 13.5 repairs and 4 websites, a gain of +1.5 repairs.

#### Worked problem 6: two intercept-form PPFs (my derivation)
- Country 1 has x/30 + y/10 = 1, so OC(x) = 10/30 = **⅓ y**.
- Country 2 has x/12 + y/12 = 1, so OC(x) = 12/12 = **1 y**.
- Country 1 has the comparative advantage in x. Country 2 has it in y: its OC(y) = 1 x, versus 3 x for Country 1.
- Mutually beneficial price of x: between ⅓ and 1 unit of y.

#### Calculus view (light)
- With linear PPFs, the opportunity cost is the constant −dy/dx = b/a, so complete specialization is optimal.
- With bowed-out PPFs, the opportunity cost (MRT) depends on the point.
  - A country facing a trade price p (units of y per x) produces where **MRT = p**, the point where the PPF is tangent to the price line.
  - Specialization is then usually **incomplete**. Comparative advantage is judged by comparing the MRTs at the autarky points.

#### Exam traps (comparative advantage)
1. **Confusing absolute with comparative advantage.** "Tom is better at both, so Tom should do both" is wrong. Specialization follows opportunity cost.
2. **Inverting opportunity costs.** Use Other Over for output tables and Other Under for input tables. Sanity check: the two opportunity costs for one person must be reciprocals.
3. **Mixing units in the terms of trade.** Compare "coconuts per fish" with "coconuts per fish", never with "fish per coconut".
4. **Terms of trade on the boundary.** A price equal to one side's opportunity cost gives that side zero gain. "Mutually beneficial" requires the price to be *strictly* between.
5. **One producer with comparative advantage in both goods** is impossible with two goods and two producers. Equal opportunity costs mean no gains from trade.
6. **Thinking a lower-productivity country cannot gain.** Mexico, Hank, Kyrgyzstan, and Timur all gain.
7. **Claiming trade shifts the PPF.** It expands *consumption* possibilities, not production possibilities.
8. **Reading input tables backwards.** Fewer hours per unit = more productive = absolute advantage.

1. Ana can make 12 shirts or 6 hats per day. Ben can make 8 shirts or 8 hats.

   a. Who has the comparative advantage in hats? **Answer: Ben.** Ana's opportunity cost of a hat is 2 shirts; Ben's is 1 shirt.
   
   b. Who has the absolute advantage in hats? **Answer: Ben** (8 > 6).
   
   c. What range of terms of trade benefits both, per hat? **Answer: 1 to 2 shirts.**
   
   d. Would Ana pay 2.5 shirts for a hat? **Answer: No.** She can make one for 2 shirts.
2. Hours per unit: X needs 2 for wheat and 4 for cloth; Y needs 6 for wheat and 3 for cloth.

   a. Who has the comparative advantage in wheat? **Answer: X.** The opportunity cost of wheat is 2/4 = 0.5 cloth for X and 6/3 = 2 cloth for Y.
   
   b. What is the range for 1 wheat? **Answer: 0.5 to 2 cloth.**
3. A can produce 100 cars or 50 trucks; B can produce 60 cars or 40 trucks. Is 1 truck for 1.8 cars mutually beneficial?

   **Answer: Yes.** The opportunity cost of a truck is 2 cars for A and 1.5 cars for B, and 1.8 lies between them.
4. C can produce 10 X or 20 Y; D can produce 5 X or 10 Y. Are there gains from trade?

   **Answer: No.** Both have an opportunity cost of X equal to 2 Y, even though C has the absolute advantage in both goods.

## 4. The circular-flow diagram: households, firms, two markets, factors of production, and money vs real flows

### Takeaway
The simple circular-flow model has two actors (households and firms) and two markets:
- **Goods and services market:** firms sell output to households, and households pay firms.
- **Factor markets:** households sell factors of production to firms, and firms pay households.

Real flows (goods, services, and factors) run one way around the loop; money flows (spending and factor income) run the opposite way. The simple model leaves out government, financial markets, the rest of the world, and transactions between firms.

### Cited Findings
- Krugman/Wells chapter 2: households and firms interact through "markets for goods and services" and "factor markets" where production resources are bought and sold. — [Krugman/Wells Ch.2 slides (SlideServe)](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)
- OpenStax's version has four flows:
  - Firms produce and sell goods and services to households (Arrow A).
  - Households pay firms, which becomes firm revenue (Arrow B).
  - Households provide labor and resources to firms (Arrow C).
  - Firms pay households wages, salaries, and benefits (Arrow D).
  
  The model "is stripped down to the essentials" and omits "financial markets, governments, and interactions with the rest of the globe (imports and exports)". — [OpenStax 3e §1.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/1-3-how-economists-use-theories-and-models-to-understand-economic-issues)
- OpenStax defines a theory as "a simplified representation of how two or more variables interact with each other". Models are the more applied tools used to test theories. — [OpenStax 3e §1.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/1-3-how-economists-use-theories-and-models-to-understand-economic-issues)
- Factor markets reverse the roles: households sell and firms buy. Households earn wages for labor, rent for land, interest for capital, and profit for entrepreneurship. Note that these generic sources list "entrepreneurship" as a factor (snippet). — [EconLearn: Circular Flow Model Explained](https://www.econlearn.org/blog/circular-flow-model-explained); [ReviewEcon: Circular Flow Model](https://www.reviewecon.com/circular-flow-model1)

### Inferences

#### The four flows in one table

| Flow | From → To | Market | Real or money? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods and services | Firms → Households | Goods & services | Real |
| Spending (firm revenue) | Households → Firms | Goods & services | Money |
| Factors of production | Households → Firms | Factor markets | Real |
| Factor income (wages, rent, interest, profit) | Firms → Households | Factor markets | Money |

#### Classify the transaction (exam-style; my examples)
- A family buys bread from a bakery: goods and services market. Bread flows firm → household; money flows household → firm.
- Toyota pays wages to a worker: factor market (labor). Labor flows household → firm; money flows firm → household.
- A household rents its land to a farm business: factor market (land).
- A student pays tuition to a private university: goods and services market, since the university acts as a firm.
- A shareholder receives dividends: factor income (a return to capital or ownership), so factor markets.
- A firm buys a machine from another firm: **not shown** in the simple model, which omits transactions between firms.
- A government pays a pension: **not in the simple model**. It belongs to Krugman's expanded circular flow, used later for GDP.

#### Exam traps (circular flow)
1. In factor markets, households are the **sellers** and firms the **buyers**. This reverses the roles in the goods market.
2. The money and real flows run in **opposite** directions. Do not draw them in the same direction.
3. Factor income is called wages, rent, interest, and profit, not "revenue". Revenue is what firms receive in the goods market.
4. The simple model omits government, banks and financial markets, imports and exports, and firm-to-firm sales.
5. **Krugman vs other texts:** Krugman's list of factors (see Gaps) uses *physical capital* and *human capital* and does not list "entrepreneurship". Many other texts list land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. On the midterm, use Krugman's list.

## 5. Positive vs normative economics: classifying tricky statements, forecasts, and why economists disagree

### Takeaway
- **Positive** statements describe how the economy *is* or *would* behave. They can be tested in principle, even when they are false. Conditional "if X, then Y" predictions and forecasts are positive.
- **Normative** statements say what *should* be. They contain value judgments ("should", "ought", "better", "fair", "too high", "good for").
- Economists disagree for two reasons: different values (normative) and different models or evidence (positive).

### Cited Findings
- Positive statements "describe the world as it is" and "are factual. They may be true or false, but we can test them, at least in principle." Normative statements "describe how the world should be" and "are subjective questions of opinion. We cannot test them since we cannot prove opinions to be true or false." — [OpenStax 3e §2.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-3-confronting-objections-to-the-economic-approach)
- OpenStax examples:
  - Positive analysis: a cost-benefit evaluation of a proposed subway system.
  - Normative analysis: arguing for extended unemployment compensation during COVID-19 "because a rich country like the United States should take care of its less fortunate citizens".
  - Disagreement can stem from values embedded in normative analysis.
  
  — [OpenStax 3e §2.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-3-confronting-objections-to-the-economic-approach)
- Krugman/Wells chapter 2: positive economics "describes the way the economy actually works", while normative economics "makes prescriptions about the way the economy should work". The deck notes that only positive questions have determinate answers (per page summary). — [Krugman/Wells Ch.2 slides (SlideServe)](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)
- MIT 14.01 (Fall 2023) Problem Set 1 answer key:

  | Statement | Answer | MIT's reasoning |
  |---|---|---|
  | "The unemployment rate in the US is currently 4 percent." | Positive | It "describes the state of the US economy" |
  | "The government should increase the minimum wage." | Normative | It is a "value judgement" |
  | "Inflation is always and everywhere caused by an increase in government spending." | **Positive** | It "establishes a causal relationship" |
  | "Tax cuts for businesses stimulate growth." | Positive | A causal claim "which can be true or false" |
  | "Tax cuts for businesses are good for the people of the US." | Normative | A prescription |

  — [MIT OCW 14.01 PS1 Solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-01-principles-of-microeconomics-fall-2023/mit14_01_f23_pset1sol.pdf)

### Inferences

#### Classification rules of thumb
1. **Value words** ("should, ought, must, better, worse, fair, unfair, too much, too little, good/bad for, deserve") make a statement normative.
2. **Testable claims** make a statement positive. This covers facts, causal claims, conditional "if… then" predictions, and forecasts, **even if they are wrong or unproven**. The MIT inflation example shows this: positive does not mean "true".
3. **Mixed statements.** If a sentence recommends an action, the overall statement is normative, even if it contains a positive "because" clause.
4. **Statements about beliefs** ("Most economists believe X") are positive. You can check them with a survey.
5. **Forecasts** ("GDP will grow 5% next year") are positive. They can be checked later.

#### Tricky statements, with answers
1. "If the minimum wage rises to $15, teen unemployment will rise." **Positive.** It is a testable conditional.
2. "The unemployment rate is too high." **Normative.** "Too high" is a judgment.
3. "Unemployment rose from 4.0% to 4.3% last quarter." **Positive.**
4. "Most economists believe rent control reduces the housing supply." **Positive.** It is a claim about beliefs.
5. "Rent control is unfair to landlords." **Normative.**
6. "Uzbekistan's GDP will grow 6% next year." **Positive.** It is a forecast.
7. "The government should tax sugary drinks because the tax would reduce obesity." **Normative overall.** Its "because" clause is positive.
8. "Free trade is good for Uzbekistan." **Normative.**
9. "Lower tariffs will reduce the price of imported cars." **Positive.**
10. "It is unfair that CEOs earn 300 times what workers earn." **Normative.** By contrast, "CEOs earn 300 times what workers earn" is **positive**, even if false.
11. "Policymakers should prioritize efficiency over equity." **Normative.**
12. "Raising the gas tax by 10% will reduce gasoline use by 3%." **Positive**, even though the number may be wrong.

#### Why economists disagree
- **Values.** They weigh efficiency, equity, and freedom differently. This is a normative disagreement (OpenStax).
- **Models and evidence.** They make different simplifying assumptions or read uncertain data differently. This is a positive disagreement, and Krugman's text emphasizes it (see Gaps).
- **Exam angle.** Positive disagreements can in principle be settled with evidence. Normative ones cannot.

## 6. Graphing appendix: relationships, slope (linear and nonlinear), intercepts, graph types, and pitfalls (truncated axes, omitted variables, reverse causality)

### Takeaway
- **Lines.** For a linear relationship y = b + mx, b is the vertical intercept and m the slope (rise/run).
- **Direction.** A positive slope means a positive (direct) relationship; a negative slope means a negative (inverse) one.
- **Special slopes.** A horizontal line has slope 0; a vertical line's slope is infinite or undefined.
- **Nonlinear curves.** Measure the slope with the **arc method** (straight line between two points) or the **point method** (tangent at one point, which is the derivative).
- **Maximum and minimum.** These are where the slope changes sign.
- **Graph types.** Time-series graphs, scatter diagrams, pie charts, and bar graphs.
- **Pitfalls.** Misleading scales or truncated axes, omitted variables, reverse causality, and in general correlation ≠ causation.

### Cited Findings
- **Slope and intercept.**
  - A linear equation has the form **y = b + mx**: b is the y-intercept and m the slope. "The slope of a line from one point to another is the change in the vertical axis divided by the change in the horizontal axis."
  - Example: for y = 9 + 3x, a 1-unit rise in x raises y by 3, so the slope is 3.
  - A positive slope means "when x increases, so does y". A negative slope means "when x increases, y decreases".
  
  — [OpenStax 3e Appendix A](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/a-the-use-of-mathematics-in-principles-of-economics)
- **Graph types and misleading presentation.**
  - OpenStax covers line graphs, pie charts (how a total divides into parts), and bar graphs (comparing quantities by bar height).
  - It shows several ways graphs mislead: stretching axes; changing the vertical scale (0–30% vs 3–10%) to exaggerate or minimize swings; choosing start and end points; and averaging data to smooth volatility.
  
  — [OpenStax 3e Appendix A](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/a-the-use-of-mathematics-in-principles-of-economics)
- **Relationships, slope, and intercepts.**
  - A positive relationship exists "when both variables move in the same direction". A negative relationship exists "when the variables move in opposite directions".
  - Slope = vertical change ÷ horizontal change. In the ski-club example, the slope is $10 per passenger, which is the ticket price.
  - An intercept is "the point at which a curve intersects an axis".
  
  — [Saylor *Principles of Economics* v2.0, Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)
- **Nonlinear slope.** The slope of a nonlinear curve at a point is found with a tangent line, "a straight line that touches, but does not intersect, a nonlinear curve at only one point." — [Saylor Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)
- **Movement vs shift.**
  - A movement along a curve happens "when the dependent variable changes in response to a change in the independent variable".
  - A shift "implies new values of one variable at each value of the other variable". Example: an increase in student-government funding shifts the entire revenue curve up by $200.
  
  — [Saylor Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)
- **Time-series graphs and scatter diagrams.**
  - "A time-series graph shows how the value of a particular variable or variables has changed over some period of time." The same tax-revenue data look trivial on a 0–100% scale and dramatic on a 16–21% scale.
  - A scatter diagram is "a graph that shows individual points relating values of the variable on one axis to values of the variable on the other." Pie and bar charts show values at one point in time.
  
  — [Saylor Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)
- **False cause.** Stock prices and San Juan temperatures moved together in 1987. "Attributing the stock crash in 1987 to the weather in San Juan would be an example of the fallacy of false cause." — [Saylor Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)
- **Krugman's appendix (snippet).** Krugman's "Graphs in Economics" appendix teaches the **arc method** (a straight line connecting two points on a curve) and the **point method** (the slope of the tangent at a point). On a linear curve, slope = rise/run between two points. — search results pointing to Macmillan's publisher-hosted [Krugman's Economics for AP, Section 1 Appendix: Graphs in Economics](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanapecon2e/krugmanapecon2e_ch1a_5.html) (host no longer resolves; content seen only as a search snippet)
- **Omitted variables and reverse causality (Mankiw-based course notes, not Krugman) (snippet).**
  - With an omitted variable, we "might decide that one variable on our graph is causing changes in the other variable when actually those changes are caused by a third omitted variable not pictured on the graph."
  - With reverse causality, "we might decide that A causes B when in fact B causes A."
  
  — [FIU ECO 2013, Ch.2 appendix notes](https://faculty.fiu.edu/~bidarkot/eco2013/ch02alec.doc)

### Inferences

#### Worked example 1: a linear curve
- Take P = 50 − 2Q.
  - Vertical intercept: P = 50 (when Q = 0).
  - Horizontal intercept: Q = 25 (when P = 0).
  - Slope: −2, a negative (inverse) relationship.
- A horizontal line (for example, P = 20) has slope 0. A vertical line (Q = 15) has an undefined (infinite) slope.

#### Worked example 2: arc vs point slope, maximum and minimum (calculus view)
- **Arc vs point.** Take y = 10x − x².
  - **Arc method** from x = 1 to x = 3: (21 − 9)/(3 − 1) = **6**.
  - **Point method** at x = 2: dy/dx = 10 − 2x = **6**. For a quadratic, the arc slope equals the point slope at the midpoint.
  - **Maximum:** dy/dx = 0 at x = 5, where y = 25. Since d²y/dx² = −2 < 0, it is a maximum. The slope is positive before x = 5 and negative after.
- **Minimum.** Take a U-shaped average-cost curve, AC = Q² − 8Q + 20.
  - dAC/dQ = 2Q − 8 = 0 at Q = 4, where AC = 4.
  - The second derivative is +2 > 0, so it is a **minimum**. The slope is negative before Q = 4 and positive after.

#### Worked example 3: area below or above a curve (used later for consumer and producer surplus)
- Demand is P = 50 − 2Q, and the price is $20, so Q = 15.
- The triangle above the price line and below the demand curve has area **½ × 15 × (50 − 20) = 225**.
- Calculus check: ∫₀¹⁵ (50 − 2Q − 20) dQ = [30Q − Q²]₀¹⁵ = 450 − 225 = **225**. ✓

#### Pick the right graph type (my examples)
- Uzbekistan's GDP growth each year from 2000 to 2025: **time-series graph**.
- Each country's income per person plotted against its life expectancy: **scatter diagram**.
- The share of a household budget spent on food, rent, transport, and other items: **pie chart**.
- Unemployment rates of five countries in 2025: **bar graph**.

#### Pitfalls, with classic examples (my examples; standard illustrations)
- **Truncated or rescaled axis.** A vertical axis starting at 16% instead of 0% (Saylor) makes small changes look dramatic. Always read where the axis starts and look for a break symbol (//).
- **Omitted variable.** Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because hot weather drives both. Snow-shovel and rock-salt sales rise together because snowfall drives both.
- **Reverse causality.** Cities with more police have more crime. Crime may cause more police, not the other way round.
- **Correlation ≠ causation.** In general, two series moving together (Saylor's San Juan example) prove nothing about cause.

#### Exam traps (graphs)
1. Slope ≠ steepness on the page; it depends on the axis units.
2. Slope ≠ elasticity. Elasticity comes later; slope is in units, elasticity in percentages.
3. A change in a variable **not on the axes** shifts the curve. A change in a variable **on the axes** is a movement along it.
4. The slope of a nonlinear curve is different at every point. Say which method you use (arc or point).
5. At a maximum or minimum, the slope is **zero**, not undefined.

#### Practice questions (answers follow)
1. Find the slope and the intercepts of y = 12 − 3x.

   **Answer:** the slope is −3, the y-intercept is 12, and the x-intercept is 4.
2. On y = x², find the arc slope from x = 2 to x = 4 and the point slope at x = 3.

   **Answer:** the arc slope is (16 − 4)/2 = 6, and the point slope is 2·3 = 6.
3. A graph of "number of firefighters at a fire" against "fire damage" slopes upward. Should cities send fewer firefighters?

   **Answer: No.** Fire size is an **omitted variable** that raises both firefighters and damage.
4. A chart of a firm's profit uses a vertical axis running from $9.8m to $10.2m. What is the risk?

   **Answer:** the axis is **truncated**, which exaggerates tiny changes.
