# Master the Margin, Master the Midterm

The first half of NTS3040 applies one idea in ten settings: compare the extra benefit of one more unit with its extra cost, then measure what is gained or lost as areas between curves. A student who can run five calculations without notes has covered most of what Krugman Ch. 1–8 can ask. The five are opportunity cost from a table or production possibility frontier; a linear supply-and-demand equilibrium with its surplus triangles; the wedge and deadweight-loss triangle created by a ceiling, floor, quota or tax; a cost table read through the rule that marginal cost crosses average cost at its minimum; and profit maximization at MR = MC with price read off the demand curve. The professor's light calculus is the same material written as derivatives. "Marginal" means first derivative, an optimum needs f′ = 0 with f″ deciding maximum or minimum, and three one-line results — dR/dP = Q(1 − |e|), MR = P(1 − 1/|E|) and d(ATC)/dQ = (MC − ATC)/Q — give nearly every proof worth memorizing. Most marks are lost to a short, predictable list of errors: "demand" written for "quantity demanded", the wrong triangle height, slope treated as elasticity, a monopolist set at P = MC, and a firm shut down because it makes a loss rather than because price has fallen below average variable cost. So the best use of the five to eleven days before the 24–30 October exam window is closed-book drilling against free answer keys, followed by a trap check, not rereading slides. Chapters 9–10 and Wheelan's six readings reward precise vocabulary and a few well-chosen stories (the Aral Sea, the black rhino, Wheelan's SUV gas tax) more than algebra. One caveat applies throughout: no legitimate open copy of the Krugman, Wells & Graddy text exists online. Krugman's exact wording and his own numerical examples are therefore reconstructed from publisher slides and secondary excerpts, and they must be checked against the class slides.

### The professor's calculus fits on one index card

Every "marginal" quantity in the course is the first derivative of a total — MC = dTC/dQ, MR = dTR/dQ, MPL = dQ/dL, MB = dTB/dQ — and the power rule (d/dx xⁿ = n·xⁿ⁻¹) with the sum and constant-multiple rules handles almost every function the course uses ([Paul's Online Notes](https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu/Classes/CalcI/DiffFormulas.aspx)). An interior optimum requires the first-order condition f′ = 0; the second-order condition then separates a maximum (f″ < 0) from a minimum (f″ > 0), and f″ = 0 is inconclusive ([Paul's Online Notes](https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu/Classes/CalcI/ShapeofGraphPtII.aspx)). Profit π = R − C gives π′ = R′ − C′ = 0, which is MR = MC, a maximum when R″ < C″. Paul's notes add two warnings that carry straight into exam answers: C′(n) approximates the cost of the **(n+1)th** unit, not the nth, and a critical point must be compared with the endpoints before it is declared the optimum ([Paul's Online Notes](https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu/Classes/CalcI/BusinessApps.aspx)). A compact example: with inverse demand P = 100 − Q and TC = 200 + 20Q + Q², profit is π = −2Q² + 80Q − 200, so **Q = 20, P = 80 and profit = 600**, and at that point MR = 100 − 2Q and MC = 20 + 2Q both equal 60.

Three short derivations recur so often that they are worth memorizing as proofs. The first links revenue to elasticity: with R = P·Q(P), the product rule gives **dR/dP = Q + P·(dQ/dP) = Q(1 + e) = Q(1 − |e|)**, so a price rise raises revenue exactly when demand is inelastic and revenue peaks at unit elasticity ([MATH 104 notes](https://math.ucr.edu/~joselg/teachingS13/elasticity.pdf)). Written per unit of output the same result is **MR = P(1 − 1/|E|)**, and for linear inverse demand P = A − BQ it becomes MR = A − 2BQ — the same intercept with twice the slope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand)). The second is the marginal–average rule, **d(ATC)/dQ = (Q·MC − TC)/Q² = (MC − ATC)/Q**: average cost falls while marginal cost is below it, rises while marginal cost is above it, and is flat — at its minimum — exactly where MC = ATC ([Paul's Online Notes](https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu/Classes/CalcI/BusinessApps.aspx)). Harvard's Oliver Knill calls the general version, g′ = (f − g)/x for any total F with marginal f and average g, the "strawberry theorem" ([Knill](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~knill/teaching/math1a2021/handouts/lecture31.pdf)), and the same algebra proves that MC crosses AVC at its minimum and that marginal product crosses average product at its maximum. The intuition is a class average: twenty students averaging 70 total 1,400, so a 21st student scoring 50 drags the average to 69.05 while one scoring 91 lifts it to 71. The third is diminishing returns, which hold when **f′(L) > 0 and f″(L) < 0**. Because MC = w/MPL ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_product_of_labor)), differentiating again gives dMC/dQ = −w·f″/(f′)³ > 0, which is exactly why diminishing returns make short-run marginal cost slope upward.

## Opportunity cost sets every trade-off in chapters 1 and 2

### Twelve principles: learn the wording, then attach one story to each

Krugman organizes Chapter 1 as four principles of individual choice, five of interaction and three economy-wide principles. Worth's third-edition lecture slides give the wording students should reproduce: "Resources are scarce", "The real cost of something is what you must give up to get it", "'How much?' is a decision at the margin" and "People usually take advantage of opportunities to make themselves better off"; then "There are gains from trade", "Markets move toward equilibrium", "Resources should be used as efficiently as possible to achieve society's goals", "Markets usually lead to efficiency" and "When markets don't achieve efficiency, government intervention can improve society's welfare"; and finally "One person's spending is another person's income", "Overall spending sometimes gets out of line with the economy's productive capacity" and "Government policies can change spending" ([Krugman & Wells 3e Ch. 1 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)). Exams test the principles three ways: naming the principle a story illustrates, computing an opportunity cost correctly, and making a marginal decision. The story column below comes from the same slide deck, so these are the examples the textbook itself uses.

The opportunity-cost calculation is where marks are won or lost. The New York Fed's 2024 estimate of the true cost of a four-year degree shows the logic. Direct costs are tuition, fees, books and supplies, about **$30,000** net; they exclude room and board "as those costs must be incurred whether one attends college or not". The opportunity cost of forgone wages is the median high-school graduate's earnings over four years, **$150,000** in 2024. The total rose from about $140,000 in the late 1990s to **$180,000**, and the estimated return to college is 12.5% ([NY Fed Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2025/04/is-college-still-worth-it/)). So more than four-fifths of the true cost of college is the earnings you give up, not the tuition. In exam form: tuition of $6,000, room and board of $5,000 that you would spend anyway, and a forgone after-tax job of $14,000 give an opportunity cost of **$20,000**. If the dorm costs $7,000 against $5,000 elsewhere, add only the $2,000 difference. Only the single next-best alternative counts, and sunk costs never enter a forward-looking decision: "typically you should ignore sunk costs" ([OpenStax 7.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/7-3-costs-in-the-short-run)).

Marginal analysis is the second calculation. You keep doing more while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost. If successive study hours add exam points worth $30, $21, $12 and $6, and each hour costs $15 of forgone wages, you study **2 hours**, because the third hour's $12 benefit is below its $15 cost. The calculus version uses TB = 100Q − 2Q² and TC = 10Q + Q². Then MB = 100 − 4Q and MC = 10 + 2Q, which meet at **Q = 15**, where net benefit is 1,050 − 375 = **675**; it falls to 672 at both Q = 14 and Q = 16, and NB″ = −6 confirms a maximum. The lesson is that you maximize *net* benefit; at the optimum, total benefit still far exceeds total cost.

The hiring rule is the same idea applied to labor. A firm hires up to the point where the wage equals the value of the marginal product, VMPL = P × MPL. In OpenStax's example, a price of $4 and marginal products of 4, 3, 2 and 1 give VMPLs of $16, $12, $8 and $4, so at a wage of $8 the firm hires three workers ([OpenStax 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/14-1-the-theory-of-labor-markets)). With calculus, maximizing π(L) = P·f(L) − wL gives P·f′(L) = w directly.

### The PPF's slope is the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis

Krugman's frontier shows "the maximum quantity of one good that can be produced for any given production of the other" ([Krugman/Wells Ch. 2 slides](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)). A point on the frontier is productively efficient, a point inside is feasible but wasteful because resources are idle or misallocated, and a point outside is unattainable with current resources and technology; only one frontier point is allocatively efficient, the mix society most desires ([OpenStax 2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)). The curve is bowed out because of the law of increasing opportunity cost: as production of one good expands, the resources moved into it are less and less suited to it, so each extra unit costs more of the other good, whereas resources equally good at both goods would give a straight line with constant opportunity cost ([OpenStax 2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)). More resources or better technology shift the frontier out, and technical progress in only one good pivots it outward along that good's axis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production%E2%80%93possibility_frontier)).

Take the frontier 3x + 2y = 60, which rearranges to y = 30 − 1.5x. Its intercepts give maximum outputs of **20 units of x and 30 of y**; one x costs **1.5 units of y** and one y costs the reciprocal, **⅔ of a unit of x**; and the point (10, 10) gives 3(10) + 2(10) = 50 < 60, so it lies inside the frontier and is inefficient. The general rule for x/a + y/b = 1 is that the opportunity cost of x equals b/a, the other good's intercept divided by the good's own. A useful trap follows: if technology doubles the x-intercept, the opportunity cost of x falls but the opportunity cost of y *rises*, because each unit of y now gives up more x. In calculus terms the marginal rate of transformation is MRT = −dy/dx. On y = 100 − x², MRT = 2x, so x costs 6 units of y at x = 3 and 10 at x = 5, and the discrete step from x = 3 to x = 4 costs 7, the average of the point values 6 and 8. A concave frontier (y″ < 0) means increasing opportunity cost, a convex one decreasing cost, and a straight line constant cost.

| Event | Effect on the PPF |
|---|---|
| More workers or capital; new resources discovered | Shifts outward (economic growth) |
| Better technology in only one good | Pivots outward along that good's axis |
| War or disaster destroys capital | Shifts inward |
| Unemployed workers are hired | No shift: the economy moves from inside toward the frontier |
| Tastes change | No shift: society picks a different point |
| The country opens to trade | No shift in production; consumption can go beyond the frontier |

### Comparative advantage: compute both opportunity costs, then price the trade between them

Comparative advantage is the lower opportunity cost; absolute advantage is the ability to "do it better", and mutual gains come from specializing by comparative, not absolute, advantage ([Krugman/Wells Ch. 2 slides](https://www.slideserve.com/emmettd/chapter-2-economic-models-trade-offs-and-trade-powerpoint-ppt-presentation)). OpenStax puts it bluntly: "Comparative advantage is not the same as absolute advantage" ([OpenStax 2.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices)). Every table problem yields to the same sequence. First decide whether the table gives **outputs** (units per period, where bigger means more productive) or **inputs** (hours per unit, where smaller means more productive), and read off absolute advantage. Then compute both producers' opportunity costs for both goods — study guides compress the formula into "Output, Other, Over" and "Input, Other goes Under" ([Fiveable](https://fiveable.me/ap-micro/unit-1/comparative-advantage-trade/study-guide/2dUIuaCgK10h5D4FTrJt)) — and check that each producer's two costs are reciprocals. Finally assign each good to the producer with the lower opportunity cost and price the trade **strictly between** the two opportunity costs, because at a price equal to one side's opportunity cost that side gains nothing ([Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/macroeconomics/macro-basic-economics-concepts/macro-comparative-advantage-and-the-gains-from-trade/a/lesson-summary-comparative-advantage-and-gains-from-trade)). Krugman's castaways show the method with an output table; these are the numbers secondary sites report, and they are internally consistent ([GonzoEcon](https://gonzoecon.com/2018/10/understanding-comparative-advantage-opportunity-cost-ppf/)).

| Weekly maximum | Fish | Coconuts | Opportunity cost of 1 fish | Opportunity cost of 1 coconut |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tom | 40 | 30 | 30/40 = ¾ coconut | 4/3 fish |
| Hank | 10 | 20 | 20/10 = 2 coconuts | ½ fish |

Tom has the absolute advantage in both goods but the comparative advantage only in fish (¾ < 2), while Hank has it in coconuts (½ < 4/3). Without trade Tom consumes 28 fish and 9 coconuts and Hank 6 and 8, both points on their frontiers, for a total of 34 fish and 17 coconuts. Specialization yields 40 fish and 20 coconuts, a gain of **6 fish and 3 coconuts**. If Tom then swaps 10 fish for 10 coconuts — a price of 1 coconut per fish, inside the (¾, 2) range — he ends with 30 fish and 10 coconuts and Hank with 10 and 10, and both consume *outside* their own frontiers (30/40 + 10/30 = 1.08 > 1 for Tom, 1.5 > 1 for Hank). That is how trade expands consumption without shifting production possibilities. A price of 1.5 coconuts per fish also works; 2.5 fails, because Hank can make his own fish for 2.

The classic trap is an input table read with the output formula. If Dilnoza needs 2 hours per laptop repair and 6 per website while Timur needs 4 and 8, "other under" says a repair costs Dilnoza 2/6 = ⅓ website and Timur 4/8 = ½, so she has the comparative advantage in repairs; a website costs her 3 repairs and him 2, so he has it in websites, and a mutually beneficial price lies between 2 and 3 repairs per website. Dividing the hours the "output" way inverts every answer. Being more productive at both goods does not cancel the gains either: in OpenStax's case the United States can make 10,000 shoes or 40,000 refrigerators and Mexico 8,000 or 10,000, so a shoe costs the US 4 refrigerators but Mexico only 1.25, and Mexico should make shoes ([OpenStax 33.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/33-2-what-happens-when-a-country-has-an-absolute-advantage-in-all-goods)).

### Positive statements can be false; normative statements carry a value word

A positive statement describes how the economy works and is testable in principle, even if it is wrong. MIT's 14.01 answer key classifies "Inflation is always and everywhere caused by an increase in government spending" as **positive**, because it "establishes a causal relationship". "The government should increase the minimum wage" is normative, a "value judgement" ([MIT OCW 14.01 PS1 solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-01-principles-of-microeconomics-fall-2023/mit14_01_f23_pset1sol.pdf)). Value words ("should", "fair", "too high", "good for") make a statement normative; forecasts and conditional predictions such as "If the minimum wage rises to $15, teen unemployment will rise" are positive; a sentence that recommends an action is normative overall even when it contains a positive "because" clause; and claims about beliefs ("most economists believe…") are positive, because a survey can check them. Economists disagree for two separable reasons, different values (a normative disagreement) and different models or readings of the evidence (a positive one); Krugman's own section on this could not be verified from an open source.

The circular-flow model has two actors and two markets. Firms sell goods and services to households, and households sell labor and other resources to firms in factor markets, where the usual roles reverse and households are the sellers; money and real flows run in opposite directions, and the simple model omits government, financial markets, foreign trade and firm-to-firm sales ([OpenStax 1.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/1-3-how-economists-use-theories-and-models-to-understand-economic-issues)). Krugman's list of factors (land, labor, physical capital and human capital, without "entrepreneurship") comes from memory of the textbook and is unverified here, so check it against the slides. In the graphing appendix, slope is rise over run; on a nonlinear curve it is measured between two points (the arc method) or by the tangent at one point (the point method, which is the derivative), and it is zero at a maximum or minimum. The pitfalls examiners like are truncated axes (the same tax-revenue data look trivial on a 0–100% scale and dramatic on a 16–21% scale), omitted variables that drive both plotted series, reverse causality, and attributing the 1987 stock crash to San Juan's weather, which Saylor's text calls "the fallacy of false cause" ([Saylor Appendix A](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_principles-of-economics-v2.0/s38-appendix-a-graphs-in-economics.html)).

## One wedge and one triangle explain every market intervention

### Name the curve before you move it

Demand is the whole price–quantity relationship; quantity demanded is a single point on it. OpenStax compresses the distinction into one line: "demand refers to the curve and quantity demanded refers to a (specific) point on the curve" ([OpenStax 3.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-1-demand-supply-and-equilibrium-in-markets-for-goods-and-services)). Only the good's own price moves you along its curve; anything else shifts the whole curve. Krugman's five demand shifters are the prices of related goods (a dearer substitute raises demand, a dearer complement lowers it), income (higher income raises demand for a normal good and lowers it for an inferior one, where "inferior" describes the income response, not quality), tastes, expectations and the number of consumers. His five supply shifters are input prices; the prices of related goods in production, where a dearer substitute in production cuts supply (if wheat pays more, cotton supply falls on shared land) and a dearer joint product raises it (if beef pays more, leather supply rises); technology; expectations, where sellers who expect higher prices hold stock back so supply falls today, the opposite direction from buyers' expectations, which raise demand today; and the number of producers. OpenStax adds weather and government policy, both acting through costs ([OpenStax 3.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-2-shifts-in-demand-and-supply-for-goods-and-services)). The sentence that prevents the commonest error is this one: "A shift in one curve never causes a shift in the other curve. Rather, a shift in one curve causes a movement along the second curve" ([OpenStax 3.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-3-changes-in-equilibrium-price-and-quantity-the-four-step-process)). So "demand rose, so the price rose, so supply rose" loses marks, and the correct chain ends "so **quantity supplied** rose". Market demand is the **horizontal** sum of individual demands — add the quantities at each price, with a kink wherever a buyer drops out — while adding prices (vertical summation) is reserved for public goods in Chapter 9.

When two curves shift at once, one outcome is certain and the other depends on the relative sizes of the shifts: same-direction shifts fix quantity, and opposite-direction shifts fix price.

| Change | Price | Quantity | Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | Demand ↑ and supply ↑ | ? | ↑ |
| Demand ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | Demand ↓ and supply ↓ | ? | ↓ |
| Supply ↑ | ↓ | ↑ | Demand ↑ and supply ↓ | ↑ | ? |
| Supply ↓ | ↑ | ↓ | Demand ↓ and supply ↑ | ↓ | ? |

For linear curves the ambiguity resolves neatly: equal **horizontal** rightward shifts of both curves leave the price unchanged, and equal **vertical** upward shifts (demand up, supply up, which means supply moves left) leave the quantity unchanged. The exam-safe wording is "Quantity definitely rises; price rises if the demand shift is larger, falls if the supply shift is larger, and is unchanged if they are equal." Real markets behave this way. When BTS played Busan in June 2026 the number of hotel rooms was fixed, so the demand surge showed up almost entirely as price: a Korea Consumer Agency survey found lodging rates up to **7.5 times** those of the surrounding weeks ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/entertainment/k-pop/20260527/bts-criticizes-alleged-busan-hotel-price-gouging-ahead-of-concerts)), and one listing jumped from 68,000 to 769,000 won ([Seoul Economic Daily](https://en.sedaily.com/society/2026/01/20/busan-hotel-prices-surge-10-fold-ahead-of-bts-concert)). Brazil's drought pushed arabica coffee up more than 40% in 2025, and the World Bank then expected a 14% fall in 2026 as supply recovered and U.S. tariffs on Brazilian coffee were removed ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/beverage-prices-retreat-as-supplies-recover)).

The best local case is Uzbekistan's exchange rate before September 2017. Treat the dollar as a good priced in so'm. The official price of about 4,210 so'm was far below a black-market rate near 7,700. On 5 September 2017 the central bank set 8,100 and abolished restrictions on buying currency ([RFE/RL](https://www.rferl.org/a/uzbekistan-devalues-currency/28718291.html); [Eurasianet](https://eurasianet.org/uzbekistan-returns-to-currency-convertibility-delivers-blow-to-black-market)). The IMF records a 50% depreciation of the official rate and a pre-reform black-market premium above 100% ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2018/03/14/ms031418-uzbekistan-staff-concluding-statement-of-the-2018-article-iv-mission)). In Krugman's language, that was a **binding price ceiling**, with the predicted shortage, rationing and black market, and removing it closed the gap.

### Solve in Q-form, draw in P-form, measure surplus as triangles

Take the constructed market Qd = 120 − 4P and Qs = −30 + 6P. Setting the two equal gives **P\* = 15 and Q\* = 60**; plug the price back into both equations as a check. For drawing, invert them: demand becomes P = 30 − 0.25Q, so the choke price is $30 and the slope you draw is −1/4, not −4, while supply becomes P = 5 + Q/6, so nothing is supplied below $5. Consumer surplus is ½ × 60 × (30 − 15) = **450**, producer surplus ½ × 60 × (15 − 5) = **300**, and total surplus **750**. A $10 ceiling creates Qd = 80 against Qs = 30, a **shortage of 50**, and only 30 units trade; the 30th buyer values the good at $22.50 while sellers need only $10, so the deadweight loss is ½ × 30 × 12.50 = **187.5**. If demand rises to Qd = 150 − 4P, the new equilibrium is P\* = 18 and Q\* = 78, matching the shortcut ΔP\* = Δa/(b + d) = 30/10.

Consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus price paid, and producer surplus is price received minus the lowest price the seller would accept ([OpenStax 3.5](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/3-5-demand-supply-and-efficiency)). On a graph the consumer-surplus triangle's height is **choke price minus market price**, not the market price, and its base is the quantity actually traded; in calculus form CS = ∫₀^Q (D(x) − P) dx and PS = ∫₀^Q (P − S(x)) dx ([UNL Applied Calculus](https://mathbooks.unl.edu/AppliedCalculus/sec-4-5-consumer-producer-surplus.html)). The efficiency of competitive markets also has a one-line proof. Define total surplus TS(Q) = ∫₀^Q [Pd(q) − Ps(q)] dq; then TS′(Q) = Pd(Q) − Ps(Q), which is zero exactly where the curves cross, and TS″ < 0, so equilibrium maximizes total surplus — it gives the good to the buyers who value it most, has it produced by the lowest-cost sellers and leaves no mutually beneficial trade undone. Efficiency is not fairness. When the price changes on fixed curves, the change in surplus splits into a rectangle for existing buyers and a triangle for new ones: on P = 60 − Q, a price cut from 40 to 30 raises consumer surplus from 200 to 450, a 200 rectangle (20 existing buyers each save 10) plus a 50 triangle (10 new buyers).

### Ceilings, floors, quotas and taxes create the same triangle and hand the rectangle to different people

The single most useful synthesis for Chapters 4–5 is to run all four interventions on one market. Demand is P = 120 − Q and supply is P = Q, so without intervention P\* = 60, Q\* = 60, and consumer and producer surplus are 1,800 each. Each intervention below cuts trade to 40 units and opens a wedge between the **demand price** ($80, the most the 40th buyer will pay) and the **supply price** ($40, the least the 40th seller will accept).

| Intervention | Buyers pay | Sellers get | Consumer surplus | Producer surplus | Who captures the 40 × $40 = 1,600 wedge rectangle | Deadweight loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling at $40 | $40 | $40 | 2,400 | 800 | Consumers | 400 |
| Floor at $80 | $80 | $80 | 800 | 2,400 | Producers | 400 |
| Quota of 40 units | $80 | $40 | 800 | 800 | License holders (quota rent) | 400 |
| Excise tax of $40 | $80 | $40 | 800 | 800 | Government (tax revenue) | 400 |

The triangle is identical in every row; only the owner of the rectangle changes. The deadweight loss is always **½ × (Q\* − quantity traded) × (demand price − supply price at that quantity)** = ½ × 20 × 40 = 400. Students who use P\* − ceiling = 20 as the height get 200, because they have counted only one of the two lost triangles. The ceiling and floor rows assume the scarce units go to the highest-value buyers or lowest-cost sellers. A ceiling binds only below equilibrium and a floor only above it. The quantity traded is always the short side of the market, and the shortage or surplus persists because the price is not allowed to adjust.

Real ceilings cost more than the triangle, which is why Krugman lists four extra problems — **inefficient allocation to consumers, wasted resources, inefficiently low quality and black markets** — and the evidence matches each. In 1979, gasoline capped at $1.00 against a free-market $1.20 cost a driver $1.40 a gallon once 30 minutes of queuing at $8 an hour was added, and under wartime controls "fat was added to hamburger, candy bars were made smaller", and landlords cut maintenance ([Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)). Glaeser and Luttmer find the misallocation costs of price controls "first order" while the classic undersupply losses are "second order", with a best estimate for New York rent control of about **$200 per apartment per year** ([NBER w6220](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6220)). The example shows why misallocation matters: hand the 40 units out at random among the 80 people willing to pay $40 and consumers' total value falls by 800, so consumer surplus drops to 1,600 — *below* the 1,800 consumers had without the ceiling — and the total loss triples to 1,200. A black market could clear at the $80 demand price, above the $60 free-market price, exactly as Rockoff warns ([Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/PriceControls.html)). San Francisco's rent-control expansion shows both sides at once: covered tenants were nearly **20% more likely to stay** at their addresses, but landlords cut rental supply by **15%**, which raised rents citywide by **5.1%** ([NBER w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)).

Floors produce the mirror image: **inefficient allocation of sales among sellers, wasted resources, inefficiently high quality and illegal activity**. The EU introduced milk quotas in 1984 to end the oversupply behind its "milk lakes" and "butter mountains" and kept them until March 2015 ([European Commission via PubAffairs](https://www.pubaffairsbruxelles.eu/eu-institution-news/milk-quotas-come-to-an-end-after-31-years-in-place/)), and by 1981 U.S. dairy supports had left the government holding **560 million pounds of cheese** in a program costing about $2 billion a year ([UPI](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1981/12/22/Reagan-frees-cheese-for-needy/5256377845200/)). In the example market, if the government buys the 40-unit surplus at $80 and destroys it, the loss rises from 400 to **2,800**. The minimum wage is the most examined floor, and the honest answer is that the evidence is debated. In the competitive model, labor demand Ld = 1,000 − 50W and supply Ls = 50W − 200 clear at W = $12 and 400 jobs, and a $14 minimum cuts employment to 300 while 500 people want work. Yet Card and Krueger found New Jersey fast-food employment rose **13%** relative to Pennsylvania after New Jersey's increase ([NBER w4509](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4509)); Cengiz and coauthors found low-wage job counts "essentially unchanged" over five years after 138 state increases ([NBER w25434](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434)); Neumark and Shirley report "a clear preponderance of negative estimates", especially for teens and the less-educated ([NBER w28388](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)); and the CBO's central estimate for a $15 federal minimum was **1.4 million fewer jobs** but **900,000 fewer people in poverty** ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/02/08/965483266/-15-minimum-wage-would-reduce-poverty-but-cost-jobs-cbo-says)). "All economists agree minimum wages destroy many jobs" is therefore false.

Quotas turn the wedge into an asset. The quota rent per unit equals demand price minus supply price at the quota, and a license sells for roughly the present value of those rents. New York caps taxi medallions at 13,587. A medallion sold for over **$1 million in 2014** and about **$160,000 by 2016–18**: quarterly Uber rides rose from 2 million to 8 million while yellow-cab rides fell from 42 to 38 million, shrinking the demand price at the quota and with it the rent ([Columbia HRLR](https://hrlr.law.columbia.edu/hrlr-online/distressed-drivers-solving-the-new-york-city-taxi-medallion-debt-crisis/)).

Taxes follow the same geometry plus one rule about who pays: "If demand is more inelastic than supply, consumers bear most of the tax burden, and if supply is more inelastic than demand, sellers bear most of the tax burden" ([OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)), and it makes no difference whether the law taxes buyers or sellers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_incidence)). In a constructed market where demand P = 180 − 3Q and supply P = 20 + Q both pass through (40, 60), demand elasticity there is 0.5 and supply elasticity 1.5. A $20 tax cuts quantity to 35, with buyers paying $75 and sellers keeping $55, so **buyers bear 75%**, exactly Es/(Es + Ed) = 1.5/2.0; revenue is $700 (the tax times the *new* quantity) and the deadweight loss only $50. With equal slopes instead (demand P = 100 − Q, supply P = 20 + Q), taxes of $10, $20 and $40 raise revenue of 350, 600 and 800 but deadweight losses of **25, 100 and 400** — deadweight loss grows with the square of the tax. Field evidence follows the rule. A 1¢ cigarette tax raises prices by **0.85¢** on average but only **0.51¢** right at a lower-tax border, where smokers can cross over and local demand is more elastic ([Harding, Leibtag & Lovenheim](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.4.4.169)); workers bear most of the payroll tax "because the employer passes it on in the form of lower wages" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_incidence)); and Philadelphia's soda tax raised prices 34% and cut sales in the city 46%, though cross-border shopping reduced the net fall to 22% ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3302335)). The same tools explain illegal markets. Enforcement against sellers raises their costs, so price rises and quantity falls, and total spending can *rise* when demand is inelastic. Enforcement against buyers lowers both price and quantity. The National Research Council warns against "confusing the indicator—the market price—with the policy objective (reducing drug use)" ([National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12976/chapter/4)).

### Elasticity measures responsiveness in percentages, never by slope

The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. It is negative, but by convention it is reported as a positive number ([OpenStax 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-1-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-price-elasticity-of-supply)).

Simple percentages give different answers depending on the direction of the move, which is why the course uses the **midpoint method**: divide each change by the average of the two values. Suppose price rises from $0.90 to $1.10 and quantity falls from 1,100 to 900. Simple percentages give an elasticity of 0.82 going up and 1.22 going down. The midpoint method gives 20% ÷ 20% = **1.00** either way. Total revenue is $990 at both points, which confirms unit elasticity, and only the midpoint answer agrees with that. The same trap appears in a University of Wisconsin handout, where one price change yields 1/3 by the simple method and 0.6 by the midpoint method ([UW–Madison handout](https://home.uchicago.edu/~kanit/kanitk/Teaching_%28UW-Madison%29/Entries/2013/1/21_ECON_101__Principles_of_Microeconomics%28Fall_2012%29_files/handout7_with_solutions.pdf)).

Krugman's summary table, titled "An Elasticity Menagerie" in the fourth edition, classifies the cases. It was seen only through a search index of the now-offline publisher page ([Macmillan asset page](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanwellsessentials4e/asset/ch5/tables/table_5_3.html)).

| Elasticity of demand | Name | Curve |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | |
| Exactly 1 | Unit-elastic | |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | |
| ∞ | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal |

The same table says cross-price elasticity is negative for complements and positive for substitutes, and income elasticity is negative for inferior goods.

Slope is not elasticity. OpenStax's points ($60, 3,000), ($70, 2,800), ($120, 1,800) and ($130, 1,600) all lie on one straight line, Q = 4,200 − 20P, yet the arc elasticity is 0.45 in the low-price stretch and 1.47 in the high-price one ([OpenStax 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-1-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-price-elasticity-of-supply)). The point formula explains why: along any line Qd = a − bP, |e| = bP/(a − bP), which is the distance from the price down to zero divided by the distance from the price up to the choke price. Demand is therefore elastic on the upper half of the line, unit-elastic at the midpoint (here $105, where revenue peaks at $105 × 2,100 = $220,500) and inelastic below it, and "flatter means more elastic" holds only when comparing two curves at the same point. The **total-revenue test** follows from the identity dR/dP = Q(1 − |e|): with inelastic demand a price rise raises revenue, with elastic demand it lowers revenue, and at unit elasticity revenue does not change. Geometrically, a price rise adds a **price-effect** rectangle (the higher price on the units still sold) and removes a **quantity-effect** rectangle (the units no longer sold); the table shows the race between them when the price rises from $0.90 to $1.10.

| Demand at $0.90 | Quantity change | Gain on units still sold | Loss on units no longer sold | Net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit-elastic | 1,100 → 900 | +$180 | −$180 | Nothing |
| Inelastic | 1,050 → 950 | +$190 | −$90 | +$100 |
| Elastic | 1,200 → 800 | +$160 | −$360 | −$200 |

Netflix supplies a real case. A 60% price rise in 2011 lost only 810,000 of 24.6 million subscribers, an elasticity of about **0.05** ([OpenStax 5.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-4-elasticity-in-areas-other-than-price)), so revenue rose by roughly half. Firms also never price on the inelastic stretch if they have market power: MR = P(1 − 1/|E|) is negative there.

Four determinants make demand more elastic, and the evidence shows each one. Close substitutes and a narrowly defined market matter most: a single brand such as Coca-Cola has an elasticity near **3.8** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand)) against **0.79** for soft drinks as a category ([Andreyeva, Long & Brownell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2804646/)), and one airline route has an elasticity of −1.4 against −0.8 at the national level ([IATA](https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/publications/economic-reports/estimating-air-travel-demand-elasticities---by-intervistas/)). Necessities are less elastic than luxuries: a meta-analysis of 160 studies puts eggs at **0.27** and food away from home at **0.81** ([Andreyeva et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2804646/)), while OpenStax's table puts housing at 0.12 and restaurant meals at 2.27 ([OpenStax 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing)) — estimates differ by study, so learn the ranking and the reason, not the decimals. A large share of the budget raises elasticity, and so does time: after correcting for publication bias, gasoline demand is **0.09 in the short run and 0.31 in the long run** ([Havranek, Irsova & Janda](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v34y2012i1p201-207.html)).

Cross-price and income elasticities keep their sign, because the sign carries the meaning. A cross-price elasticity of doughnuts with respect to the price of coffee of −1.5 means a 10% rise in coffee prices cuts doughnut sales by 15%, so they are complements ([UW–Madison handout](https://home.uchicago.edu/~kanit/kanitk/Teaching_%28UW-Madison%29/Entries/2013/1/21_ECON_101__Principles_of_Microeconomics%28Fall_2012%29_files/handout7_with_solutions.pdf)). Income elasticity below 0 marks an inferior good, between 0 and 1 a normal but income-inelastic good, and above 1 an income-elastic one; an income-inelastic good is still normal, not inferior. Food's income elasticity averages **0.78** in low-income countries and **0.50** in high-income ones, the pattern behind Engel's law ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2011/september/low-income-countries/)), while air travel's runs from 1.3 to 2.0 ([IATA briefing](https://www.travelready.org/PDF%20Files/Travel%20-%20IATA%20-%20Air%20Travel%20Demand.pdf)).

Supply elasticity depends on how easily inputs can be obtained and on time. It ranges from a vertical curve (a fixed resource) to a horizontal one (inputs freely available at constant cost). Saiz's housing estimates show the input determinant at work: supply elasticity is **0.66 in San Francisco and 0.76 in New York, against 2.55 in Atlanta and 2.30 in Houston**, because terrain and zoning limit buildable land in coastal cities ([Saiz 2010](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2010.125.3.1253)).

The Laffer curve is the total-revenue test applied to tax rates, and it suits the professor's calculus even though it is unconfirmed whether the course examines it; Wheelan's Chapter 4 raises it. Revenue is R = t·Y(t). With a base Y = Y₀(1 − t), R′ = Y₀(1 − 2t), so revenue peaks at **t\* = 50%** and rates of 30% and 70% raise identical revenue, while a constant-elasticity base gives **t\* = 1/(1 + e)**. Diamond and Saez apply the top-bracket version, 1/(1 + a·e), and put the revenue-maximizing top rate near **73%** ([Diamond & Saez](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/diamond-saezJEP11opttax.pdf)); the best elasticity estimates run from 0.12 to 0.40, which leaves the U.S. top rate "far from the top of the Laffer curve" ([Saez, Slemrod & Giertz](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-slemrod-giertzJEL12.pdf)); and when the Chicago Booth panel was asked whether a U.S. income-tax cut would raise revenue within five years, **none of its economists agreed** ([Clark Center](https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/laffer-curve/)). The exam point is that cutting a tax rate raises revenue only from above t\*.

| "−1.5 < 1, so demand is inelastic" | Classify by absolute value: 1.5 > 1, so demand is elastic |
| Plugging dP/dQ into the point formula | e = (dQ/dP)(P/Q); from P = 30 − 0.25Q, dQ/dP = −4 |
| Dropping the sign of cross-price or income elasticity | The sign separates substitutes from complements, and normal goods from inferior ones |

## MR = MC answers every firm question; only the demand curve changes

### Diminishing returns come from the fixed input, not from worse workers

Krugman's production example is George and Martha's wheat farm, with land fixed at 10 acres and labor varying; the setting is confirmed through a search excerpt of a publisher module ([Macmillan module](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanwellsmodulesmicro3eupdate/krugmanwellsmodulesmicro3eupdate_ch19_7.html)). The output figures circulating with the course, 0, 19, 36, 51, 64, 75, 84, 91 and 96 bushels for 0–8 workers, fit **Q = 20L − L²** exactly.

| Workers L | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Output Q | 19 | 36 | 51 | 64 | 75 | 84 | 91 | 96 |
| Marginal product (MPL) | 19 | 17 | 15 | 13 | 11 | 9 | 7 | 5 |
| Average product (APL) | 19 | 18 | 17 | 16 | 15 | 14 | 13 | 12 |

Marginal product falls by 2 bushels with every worker, so diminishing returns set in from the second worker, yet total output keeps rising because marginal product stays positive, and average product falls because marginal product sits below it. The calculus test is f′(L) = 20 − 2L > 0 with f″(L) = −2 < 0. The decline comes from sharing a fixed input, not from later workers being worse, since the model's workers are identical; early workers can even raise marginal product, as OpenStax's lumberjacks show with marginal products of 4, 6, 2, 1 and 0, where specialization pays before diminishing returns take over ([OpenStax 7.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/7-2-production-in-the-short-run)). Diminishing returns is a short-run idea that holds other inputs fixed — Fred Brooks's rule that "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" is the real-world version ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooks%27s_law)) — whereas diseconomies of scale are a long-run idea.

### Cost tables yield to ten identities, and the minima arrive in a fixed order

Economic profit subtracts implicit costs as well as explicit ones, so it is never larger than accounting profit. In OpenStax's example, Eryn leaves a $125,000 law job to open a practice ([OpenStax 7.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/7-1-explicit-and-implicit-costs-and-accounting-and-economic-profit)):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $200,000 |
| Rent and a clerk (explicit costs) | −$85,000 |
| **Accounting profit** | **$115,000** |
| Forgone salary (implicit cost) | −$125,000 |
| **Economic profit** | **−$10,000** |

The firm's cost table rests on a handful of identities. Fixed cost equals TC at zero output and is the same in every row; VC = TC − FC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ = ΔVC/ΔQ, measured per unit of **output**, not per worker; AFC = FC/Q, AVC = VC/Q and ATC = AFC + AVC; and the labor link is MC = w/MPL and AVC = w/APL ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_curve)). ATC is U-shaped because two forces pull against each other, which Krugman calls (check the slides for his exact wording) the **spreading effect**, as fixed cost spread over more units pulls AFC down forever, and the **diminishing returns effect**, as each extra unit needs more variable input and pushes AVC up. Krugman's Selena's Gourmet Salsas has a fixed cost of **$108** and a **minimum-cost output of three cases**, where MC crosses ATC from below at its lowest point ([Macmillan AP module, search excerpt](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanapecon2e/krugmanapecon2e_ch55_3.html)). A reconstruction that reproduces those facts is TC = 108 + 12Q², giving **MC = 24Q** and **ATC = 108/Q + 12Q**; then d(ATC)/dQ = −108/Q² + 12 splits neatly into the spreading term (−108/Q²) and the diminishing-returns term (+12), which cancel at **Q = 3**, where ATC = MC = **$72**.

Real marginal cost curves are usually swoosh-shaped rather than straight. OpenStax's barber shop pays $160 in fixed cost and $80 per barber. Haircuts rise 16, 40, 60, 72, 80 and 84 with one to six barbers and fall to 82 with a seventh. Marginal cost per haircut runs **$5.00** for the first barber (computed directly as $80/16) and then **$3.33, $4.00, $6.67, $10.00 and $20.00**: it falls while specialization raises marginal product, then rises once diminishing returns take over. Average total cost bottoms out at **$6.67 between 60 and 72 haircuts, exactly where marginal cost is $6.67** ([OpenStax 7.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/7-3-costs-in-the-short-run)).

The minima always arrive in the order **MC, then AVC, then ATC**, as a calculus version shows. Take TC = 100 + 20Q − 6Q² + Q³. Then MC = 20 − 12Q + 3Q², AVC = 20 − 6Q + Q², and the minima fall at Q = 2, 3 and 5. MC equals AVC at the third and equals ATC at the fifth.

| Q | TC | MC (derivative) | AVC | ATC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 115 | 11 | 15 | 115.00 |
| 2 | 124 | **8 (minimum)** | 12 | 62.00 |
| 3 | 133 | 11 | **11 (minimum, = MC)** | 44.33 |
| 4 | 148 | 20 | 12 | 37.00 |
| 5 | 175 | 35 | 15 | **35.00 (minimum, = MC)** |
| 6 | 220 | 56 | 20 | 36.67 |

The row Q = 4 is the classic trap: marginal cost is rising, but at 20 it is still below ATC of 37, so ATC is still *falling*. Two shift rules complete the short run: a fixed-cost change (rent, a lump-sum fee) moves only AFC and ATC, leaving MC, AVC and the profit-maximizing output in place, while a wage change moves MC, AVC and ATC ([ReviewEcon](https://www.reviewecon.com/what-do-i-need-to-know-about-cost-curves)).

In the long run every input is variable. The **long-run ATC** curve is the lower envelope of the short-run curves, touching each at one point, and MIT's answer key marks "short-run average cost is always at least as large as long-run average cost" **true** ([MIT OCW 14.01 PS2 solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-01-principles-of-microeconomics-fall-2023/mit14_01_f23_pset2sol.pdf)). Where long-run ATC falls there are **economies of scale**, which come from specialization (Smith's ten pin-makers produced "upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day", where alone they could make "perhaps not one" ([Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Smith/smWN.html?chapter_num=4#book-reader))), from large set-up costs (in chemical plants, doubling the cost of pipe material lets a firm process four times the volume ([OpenStax 7.5](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/7-5-costs-in-the-long-run))) and from network externalities, which make a platform worth more to each user the more users it has ([HBS Online](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/economies-of-scale)). Where long-run ATC rises there are **diseconomies of scale**, caused by "communication breakdowns, coordination issues, or management complexity" ([HBS Online](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/economies-of-scale)). Rising short-run MC is diminishing returns, not diseconomies of scale.

### P = MC picks a price taker's output, and two cost minimums decide the rest

Krugman defines a price-taking producer as one "whose actions have no effect on the market price of the good it sells" and gives two necessary conditions for perfect competition, "many producers, none of whom have a large market share" and a standardized product, with free entry and exit a third condition that is "often satisfied as well" ([Krugman & Wells Ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)). Whether a product counts as standardized depends on buyers — "the consumer is always right" — so brands that consumers believe differ are not standardized even if they are physically identical.

The same slides give the decision rules. The **price-taking firm's optimal output rule** is to produce where marginal cost equals the market price, because MR = P for a price taker, and in a table to "produce the largest quantity for which marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost". The **break-even price** is minimum ATC and the **shut-down price** is minimum AVC, because "fixed cost is irrelevant to the firm's optimal short-run production decision", so the **short-run supply curve** is the MC curve above the shut-down price. Krugman's tomato farm has a break-even price of $14 and a shut-down price of $10, and at $18 it produces 5 bushels at an ATC of $14.40 for a profit of **$18**. A constructed table shows the full procedure for Dilnoza's dried-apricot farm, which has a fixed cost of $40 and sells at whatever the market price is.

| Boxes | VC | TC | MC | AVC | ATC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 60 | 20 | 20.00 | 60.00 |
| 2 | 30 | 70 | 10 | 15.00 | 35.00 |
| 3 | 42 | 82 | 12 | **14.00** | 27.33 |
| 4 | 58 | 98 | 16 | 14.50 | 24.50 |
| 5 | 80 | 120 | 22 | 16.00 | **24.00** |
| 6 | 110 | 150 | 30 | 18.33 | 25.00 |
| 7 | 150 | 190 | 40 | 21.43 | 27.14 |

The shut-down price is **$14** and the break-even price is **$24**.

| Market price | Output (largest Q with MC ≤ P) | Profit | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| $34 | 6 | 204 − 150 = **+$54** | Economic profit; entry follows |
| $24 | 5 | **$0** | Break even (normal profit) |
| $18 | 4 | 72 − 98 = **−$26** | Keep producing: a smaller loss than the −$40 from shutting down |
| $14 | 3 | **−$40** | Indifferent: revenue just covers variable cost |
| $11 | 0 | **−$40** | Shut down: the best positive output loses $48 |

At $34 she produces 6 boxes, not the 5 that minimize ATC, because she maximizes total profit, not profit per unit.

In the long run, profit attracts entry and losses cause exit, until price equals minimum ATC and economic profit is zero. Zero economic profit is a normal return, because the owner's opportunity cost is already covered ([OpenStax 8.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/8-3-entry-and-exit-decisions-in-the-long-run)). Krugman adds that the long-run industry supply curve is often horizontal and "always more elastic" than the short-run curve, and that when a drug's patent expires, the shift to competition "is accompanied by a sharp fall in market price" ([Krugman & Wells Ch. 12 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-12/@@download/file/KWe3-Micro-ch12.pdf)). The FDA's December 2019 analysis puts numbers on that fall: generic prices run **39% below** the pre-entry brand price with one generic producer and **more than 95% below** it with six or more ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/center-drug-evaluation-and-research-cder/generic-competition-and-drug-prices); [FDA report](https://www.fda.gov/media/133509/download)).

### A monopolist's marginal revenue sits below demand because every price cut applies to all units

Krugman lists five sources of monopoly power, each a barrier to entry: control of a scarce resource or input; **increasing returns to scale**, which create a natural monopoly; technological superiority; **network externalities**; and **government-created barriers** such as patents and copyrights. A natural monopoly arises "when economies of scale are large relative to the quantity demanded", as with water and electricity networks, and a U.S. patent lasts **20 years** while a copyright runs for the author's life plus 70 years ([OpenStax 9.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/9-1-how-monopolies-form-barriers-to-entry)). The court in *U.S. v. Microsoft* described the network version as Windows' "applications barrier to entry", a chicken-and-egg problem in which no rival operating system could attract developers without users ([DoJ findings of fact](https://www.justice.gov/atr/us-v-microsoft-courts-findings-fact)). Whether a firm is a monopoly depends on how the market is drawn: a U.S. court found that "Google is a monopolist" in search, with about 90% of U.S. queries ([DoJ](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-wins-significant-remedies-against-google)), but Meta was ruled no monopolist once TikTok and YouTube were counted as rivals ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/11/18/tech/meta-antitrust-suit-decision-not-monopoly)). Uzbekistan's 2023 competition law lowered the threshold for a dominant position to **more than 40%** market share ([GRATA International](https://gratanet.com/news/uzbekistan-adopts-new-competition-law)).

To sell one more unit, a single-price monopolist must cut the price on every unit, so marginal revenue has two parts: the **quantity effect** (the price of the extra unit) and the **price effect** (the price cut times all the units already being sold). In the De Beers numbers in Krugman's publisher module, cutting the price from $550 to $500 to sell a tenth diamond adds $500 but "loses 9 × $50 = $450", so marginal revenue is just **$50** ([Macmillan AP module, search excerpt](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanapecon2e/krugmanapecon2e_ch61_2.html)). In calculus, **MR = P + Q·P′(Q)**, where the first term is the quantity effect and the second, negative term the price effect; for linear demand P = A − BQ this gives MR = A − 2BQ. Rewritten as MR = P(1 − 1/|E|), it shows that a monopolist with positive marginal cost always operates where demand is **elastic**, with a markup that obeys the Lerner rule (P − MC)/P = 1/|E|.

The solution always takes two steps: set MR = MC to choose the quantity, then go straight up to the **demand curve** to read the price. Cornell's answer key marks a price wrong precisely because "that point doesn't lie on the demand curve" ([Cornell Econ 101 answer key](https://courses.cit.cornell.edu/econ101-dl/answer-key-practice-2.html)). Take demand P = 100 − 2Q with constant MC = ATC = $20 (a constructed example). Then MR = 100 − 4Q, so **Qm = 20**, **Pm = $60** and profit = (60 − 20) × 20 = **$800**; at that point demand elasticity is 1.5 and the Lerner index 2/3, exactly 1/1.5.

| | Perfect competition (P = MC) | Monopoly (MR = MC) |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity | 40 | 20 |
| Price | $20 | $60 |
| Consumer surplus | 1,600 | 400 |
| Profit | 0 | 800 (a transfer from consumers) |
| Deadweight loss | 0 | **400** (the triangle between demand and MC from 20 to 40) |

A fixed cost of 300 would cut profit to 500 but leave the quantity and price unchanged. A monopolist whose demand lies below ATC at every quantity makes a loss: market power does not guarantee profit.

A natural monopoly is cheapest with one firm, so policy regulates its price instead of breaking it up. OpenStax shows that marginal-cost pricing leaves the firm "suffering losses" that need an "ongoing public subsidy", while average-cost pricing lets it cover its costs ([OpenStax 11.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/11-3-regulating-natural-monopolies)). A constructed example makes the comparison concrete, with demand P = 100 − Q and TC = 700 + 20Q:

| Pricing rule | Quantity | Price | Profit | Deadweight loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unregulated | 40 | $60 | **$900** | 800 |
| Price ceiling at ATC | 70 | $30 | 0 | **50** |
| Price set at MC | 80 | $20 | Loss equal to the **$700** fixed cost | 0 |

Splitting the firm in two would raise each half's ATC to $40.

Price discrimination works only if three conditions hold: the firm has market power, it can sort buyers by willingness to pay, and it can stop resale; the less elastic group then pays more. Krugman's airline case contrasts business travelers willing to pay $550 with students willing to pay $150 ([Macmillan module, search excerpt](http://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/krugmanwellsmodulesmicro3eupdate/krugmanwellsmodulesmicro3eupdate_ch13_19.html)). In a constructed version, an airline has 100 business travelers at $500, 150 students at $150 and a marginal cost of $100: a single price of $500 earns $40,000 and leaves the students on the ground, while charging each group its own price earns **$47,500** and flies everyone. **Perfect price discrimination** removes the price effect, so MR = P, output rises to the competitive quantity and the deadweight loss vanishes, but consumer surplus falls to zero; on the monopoly example's demand, profit becomes the whole $1,600 triangle. The outcome is efficient but not equitable. Everyday versions are easy to find. Spotify charges U.S. students $6.99 a month against $12.99 for individuals ([Spotify](https://www.spotify.com/us/premium/)). U.S. airlines charge solo travelers more than pairs only on weekday returns, because solo weekday travelers are more likely to be less elastic business flyers ([Hubbard & O'Brien](https://hubbardobrieneconomics.com/2025/07/24/solved-problem-why-do-u-s-airlines-charge-solo-travelers-higher-ticket-prices/)). The FTC has documented personal data being used to "target individual consumers with different prices" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-indicates-wide-range-personal-data-used-set-individualized-consumer)).

### The prisoners' dilemma explains both collusion and its collapse

In an oligopoly each firm's profit depends on its rivals' choices, so the tool is the payoff matrix. Take a constructed duopoly with demand P = 130 − Q, two identical firms and MC = $10. Acting together as a monopoly, they would produce 60 units in total (30 each) at a price of $70 and earn $1,800 each. If one firm cheats by producing 40, it earns $2,000 and the complier $1,500. If both produce 40, the price falls to $50 and each earns $1,600.

| | **B produces 30 (comply)** | **B produces 40 (cheat)** |
|---|---|---|
| **A produces 30** | 1,800 ; 1,800 | 1,500 ; 2,000 |
| **A produces 40** | 2,000 ; 1,500 | **1,600 ; 1,600** |

Cheating pays more whatever the rival does (2,000 > 1,800 and 1,600 > 1,500), so it is a **dominant strategy**. The **Nash equilibrium** — an outcome where "neither player could improve its position by unilaterally changing its move" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/)) — is therefore (cheat, cheat). Both firms would prefer (comply, comply), but it is not an equilibrium. Consumers gain when the collusion fails: the price falls from $70 to $50, between the monopoly price and the competitive price of $10.

Repetition changes the arithmetic. Cheating gains $200 once, but against a tit-for-tat rival it costs $200 in every later period, which is why firms that expect to meet indefinitely can sustain **tacit collusion**, and why Tit for Tat won Axelrod's tournaments ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/)). Real cartels break down in exactly the ways the model predicts. Collusion is illegal: the lysine cartel doubled the price of a $600-million-a-year product before ADM pleaded guilty and paid a $100 million fine ([OpenStax 10.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/10-2-oligopoly)). Members with spare capacity defect: the UAE, with about 4.8 million barrels a day of capacity, left OPEC and OPEC+ effective 1 May 2026 after belonging since 1967 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/28/what-are-opec-and-opec-and-why-has-the-uae-quit)), and Kazakhstan exceeded its OPEC+ quota by about 332,000 barrels a day in March 2025 ([Caspian Post](https://caspianpost.com/kazakhstan/kazakhstan-exceeds-opec-quota-by-332-000-bpd-in-march-says-s-p)). Demand shocks trigger price wars: in March 2020 Russia refused a cut and Saudi Arabia answered by raising output from 9.7 to 12.3 million barrels a day, and by 20 April U.S. oil futures had briefly traded below zero ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8652835/)). And leniency programs reward confession: Virgin Atlantic escaped a fine by reporting its fuel-surcharge collusion with British Airways ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/british-airways-fined-546m-for-collusion/)), a prisoners' dilemma imposed by the state.

### Monopolistic competition earns zero profit without producing at minimum cost

Monopolistic competition combines many firms, differentiated products and free entry and exit in the long run. In the short run each firm behaves like a small monopolist; entry then pulls each firm's demand curve left until it just touches ATC. In a constructed example, a firm with TC = 100 + Q² has MC = 2Q and minimum ATC of $20 at Q = 10. Facing short-run demand P = 56 − 3Q, it produces 7 units at $35 and earns $96, which attracts entry. When demand falls to P = 40 − 3Q, MR = MC gives **Q = 5 and P = $25 = ATC**, so profit is zero, and the ATC slope there is −3, equal to the demand slope: a tangency. But price exceeds MC ($25 against $10), and output is half the cost-minimizing 10 units — the shortfall is **excess capacity**. OpenStax states that the long-run price lies "on the downward-sloping portion of the average cost curve, not at the very bottom", defends the outcome on grounds of variety, and records that competing advertising campaigns may "simply neutralise one another" ([OpenStax 10.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/10-1-monopolistic-competition)).

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Firms and product | Many, identical | Many, differentiated | Few, identical or differentiated | One, no close substitutes |
| Entry barriers | None | None in the long run | Significant | Very high |
| Output rule | P = MC | MR = MC | Depends on rivals (game theory) | MR = MC |
| Long-run profit | Zero | Zero (demand tangent to ATC) | Can persist | Can persist |
| P = MC? Minimum ATC? | Yes; yes | No; no (excess capacity) | Usually no; not necessarily | No; not necessarily |

### Traps and practice for chapters 6 to 8

| Trap | Correct move |
|---|---|
| Treating the MC of a worker ($200) as MC per unit | MC = wage ÷ MPL, per unit of output |
| "MC is rising, so ATC is rising" | ATC rises only when MC > ATC |
| Thinking a rent rise changes output | Fixed costs move only AFC and ATC |
| Shutting down because price is below ATC | Shut down only below minimum AVC |
| Mixing up diminishing returns and diseconomies of scale | The first is short run with one input varying; the second is long run with every input varying |
| Assuming a competitive firm produces at minimum ATC | Only in long-run equilibrium; otherwise P = MC |
| Setting P = MC for a monopolist, or reading price off MR | Use MR = MC for quantity, then read price off demand |
| Computing monopoly profit as (P − MC) × Q | Use (P − ATC) × Q |
| Treating the collusive cell as the Nash equilibrium | In a prisoners' dilemma the equilibrium is mutual defection |
| Drawing the long-run tangency at minimum ATC | The tangency lies left of the minimum, so there is excess capacity |
| "Perfect price discrimination is inefficient" | It is efficient (no DWL), but consumer surplus is zero |

| $25 | 4 | −$36 | Produce |
| $20 | 0 | −$50 | Shut down: every positive output loses more than $50 |

The falling MC over the first three units is the specialization swoosh.

## Market failure, macro vocabulary and Wheelan's stories earn the essay marks

### An externality makes the market produce the wrong quantity, and the fix makes decision-makers pay the full cost

An externality is "the effect of a market exchange on a third party who is outside or 'external' to the exchange". With a negative externality, "the market produces too much of the product". In OpenStax's refrigerator example, pollution costs of $100 per unit move the socially optimal outcome from $650 and 45,000 units to **$700 and 40,000 units** ([OpenStax 12.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-1-the-economics-of-pollution)). Positive externalities produce the opposite error. Inventors capture only "one-third to one-half of the total economic benefits from innovations", so markets under-invest in research ([OpenStax 13.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-1-investments-in-innovation)).

In a constructed negative-externality example, demand (which equals marginal social benefit) is P = 100 − Q, private supply (marginal private cost) is P = 20 + Q, and each unit does $20 of damage to others, so marginal social cost is P = 40 + Q. The market produces **40** units at $60, but the optimum is **30**, where demand meets marginal social cost; the deadweight loss is the triangle between marginal social cost and demand over the 10 excess units, ½ × 10 × (80 − 60) = **$100**, and a **Pigouvian tax** of $20 per unit, equal to the marginal external cost at the optimum, restores 30 units. The positive case mirrors it: a flu shot with private demand P = 80 − Q, a $20 external benefit and supply P = 20 + Q is under-produced at 30 shots instead of 40, and a $20 subsidy per shot fixes it. The efficient amount of pollution is not zero either: facing a charge of $1,000 per 10 pounds, OpenStax's firm cuts only the 30 pounds whose abatement costs less than the tax ([OpenStax 12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools)).

Market tools "can achieve any desired reduction in pollution at a lower cost to society" than command-and-control rules, because the cheapest abater does the work. In a constructed comparison, firms A and B each emit 60 tons, A can abate at $10 a ton and B at $40, and the target is a 60-ton cut.

| Policy | How the 60-ton cut happens | Abatement cost |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform standard | Each firm cuts 30 tons | **$1,500** |
| $25-per-ton tax | A cuts all 60 tons (cheaper than the tax); B pays the tax | **$600** |
| Tradable permits | A cuts all 60 tons and sells its permits to B | **$600** |

Permits suit "a few dozen or a few hundred parties", while pollution charges suit millions of small emitters such as car engines ([OpenStax 12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools)). The Coase theorem adds a private route: bargaining reaches the efficient outcome whatever the assignment of property rights, provided those rights are clear and transaction costs are low, so if a factory's waste does $500 of damage to one fish farm and a filter costs $300, the filter gets installed either way and the property right decides only **who pays** ([OpenStax 12.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/12-3-market-oriented-environmental-tools)). Near Cheshire, Ohio, American Electric Power struck such a deal, paying about **$20 million** to buy out a polluted village of 221 people ([Tampa Bay Times](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/2002/05/08/dangerous-electricity-pollution/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire,_Ohio)), a story Wheelan uses in Chapter 3; with thousands of victims, bargaining collapses and the case for a tax or permits returns.

The Aral Sea is the local case to master. In the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the Syr Darya and Amu Darya rivers to irrigate cotton and other crops. The sea, once the world's fourth-largest lake, shrank until "fisheries and the communities that depended on them collapsed", and the exposed lakebed blew dust "contaminated with agricultural chemicals" ([NASA Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea)). Peer-reviewed work attributes the drying "overwhelmingly" to irrigation expansion ([Micklin 2007](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.earth.35.031306.140120)). Fisheries at Aralsk processed **20,000 tons** of fish in the 1980s but only **400 tons** of salt-tolerant species later. Kazakhstan's World Bank-funded Kok-Aral dike, completed in 2005 ([NASA Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea)), raised the North Aral by about **four meters** and increased the 2006 catch **tenfold** ([World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/668681468050713490/text/9249602006Jun20a0Box0385367B0PUBLIC.txt)). A top answer frames the case through five concepts: a negative externality, since irrigators faced a private cost of water far below its social cost; a common resource, since river water was rival but effectively non-excludable; the failure of Coasean bargaining among millions of victims across several states; government failure, since the diversion was a state plan rather than a market outcome; and partial repair through public investment.

### Two yes-or-no questions classify every good

Public goods are "nonexcludable and non-rival": people cannot easily be stopped from benefiting, and one person's use does not reduce another's ([OpenStax 13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)). Each combination of answers has its own market problem.

| | **Rival** | **Non-rival** |
|---|---|---|
| **Excludable** | **Private good:** plov in a restaurant, a shirt. Markets work. | **Artificially scarce (club) good:** a streaming subscription. Pricing it limits use that costs almost nothing. |
| **Non-excludable** | **Common resource:** fish, irrigation water, a congested free road. **Over-used**: the tragedy of the commons. | **Public good:** defense, flood control, fireworks. **Under-provided**, because people free-ride. |

Free riding is the public-good problem — people "have an incentive to let others pay for the public good and then to 'free ride'" — so public goods are financed through taxes and collective decisions, while common resources need licences, harvest limits or catch shares. A positive externality does not make something a public good: education has "huge positive spillovers but can be provided by a private company" ([OpenStax 13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/13-3-public-goods)), so vaccines and schooling are private goods with positive externalities. Because everyone consumes the same unit of a public good, its efficient quantity comes from adding individual marginal benefits **vertically** and providing until that sum equals marginal cost; this is standard theory, but check Krugman's wording. If 200 households each value street lighting at $10 and it costs $1,500, it is worth providing ($2,000 exceeds $1,500), yet voluntary contributions will fall short.

### The macro chapter is five big ideas and a few exact definitions

Chapter 10 introduces macroeconomics as the study of the economy as a whole, in which "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts", with sections on the business cycle, long-run growth, inflation and deflation, and international imbalances ([Macmillan 4e TOC](https://prod-cat-files.macmillan.cloud/CMS/SampleChapters/NDP000DKG/1416_Krugman%20Essentials%20of%20Economics%204e%20-%20Preface%20and%20TOC.pdf)). The NBER defines a recession as "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months" and dates the latest U.S. peak to **February 2020** and the trough to **April 2020** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/research/business-cycle-dating)), so "two consecutive quarters of falling GDP" is not the official definition. The unemployed are "jobless, looking for a job, and available for work", with at least one active search in the past four weeks, and the unemployment rate is the unemployed as a share of the labor force ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cps_htgm.htm)), so someone who stopped searching six weeks ago is out of the labor force, not unemployed. Uzbekistan supplies the disinflation example: real GDP grew **7.7%** in 2025, inflation fell to **7.3%** against a 5% target, the policy rate has been 14% since March 2025, and unemployment fell to 4.8% ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/04/13/mcs041326-uzbekistan)); inflation falling toward 5% is disinflation, not deflation. The chapter is also where Principles 10–12 become policy: spending below capacity means recession, and the babysitting co-op cured its slump by issuing more tickets ([Krugman & Wells 3e Ch. 1 slides](https://www.uni-giessen.de/de/fbz/fb02/fb/professuren/vwl/goetz/veraltet/lehre/copy2_of_downloadsordner/mikrooekonomie-i/foliensaetze/chapter-1)).

### Wheelan's six readings make one argument: markets work, incentives rule, and government must set the rules without smothering them

*Naked Economics* (3rd edition, W. W. Norton, 2019) is, in one reviewer's words, "economics by anecdote", with no graphs, charts or equations ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Naked_Economics.html?id=sT1kDwAAQBAJ); [MSU review](https://mtprof.msun.edu/Spr2008/youngrev.html)). Its value on the exam is supplying vivid, citable illustrations for Krugman's mechanisms. The assigned chapter titles are confirmed by a library scan of the 2010 contents page ([ETH Zürich TOC](https://toc.library.ethz.ch/objects/pdf/z01_978-0-393-33764-8_01.pdf)). Most anecdote details below come from summaries and reading guides of earlier editions, so verify any number in your own copy before quoting it, and read the Introduction yourself: its 2019 anecdotes could not be checked from outside sources.

## Conclusion

The ten chapters repeat three templates. The marginal–average identity makes MC cross ATC at its minimum, MP cross AP at its maximum, and one quiz score move a class average the same way. The unit-elastic peak appears three times: revenue is maximized where |e| = 1, marginal revenue is zero at that point, and a tax rate maximizes revenue where the base's elasticity reaches one. And the wedge-and-triangle picture covers ceilings, floors, quotas, taxes, the monopoly markup and externalities, each of which opens a gap between what the marginal buyer values and what the marginal unit costs, with the deadweight-loss triangle sitting on the lost quantity. Studying by template rather than by chapter turns the syllabus into three pictures and a handful of identities, which is why an unfamiliar exam question is almost always a familiar one in new clothes, and why a student who can derive dR/dP = Q(1 − |e|) or d(ATC)/dQ = (MC − ATC)/Q from scratch rarely needs to memorize what follows from them.

The real-world cases show where the models are strong and where they stop. They predict *directions* reliably — rent control shrinks supply, medallion values collapse when untaxed substitutes arrive, cartel members cheat, and tax pass-through falls where buyers can escape across a border — but *magnitudes* are contested, as the minimum-wage literature shows. The answer that earns top marks therefore states the model's prediction, cites one piece of evidence and names the caveat. Wheelan supplies the "why" and Krugman the "how much", so pairing one story with one graph in every essay answer is the cheapest way to show both; and because language is marked as strictly as arithmetic, "quantity demanded" versus "demand", "economic" versus "accounting" profit, and "shut down" versus "exit" deserve the same care as the algebra.
