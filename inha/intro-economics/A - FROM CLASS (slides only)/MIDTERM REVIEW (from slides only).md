**The Twelve Economic Principles (slide wording)** [W1-S19, S33, S52]
- **I. Individual choice (4):** 1 Resources are scarce. 2 The real cost of something is what you must give up to get it. 3 "How much?" (or "How many") is a decision at the margin. 4 People usually exploit opportunities to make themselves better off.
- **II. Interaction (5):** 1 There are gains from trade. 2 Markets move toward equilibrium. 3 Resources should be used as efficiently as possible to achieve society's goals. 4 Markets usually lead to efficiency. 5 When markets don't achieve efficiency, government intervention can improve society's welfare (side effects; monopoly; goods like drugs [W1-S51]).
- **III. Economy-wide (3):** 1 One person's spending is another person's income. 2 Overall spending sometimes gets out of line with the economy's productive capacity. 3 Government policies can change overall spending (spending policy, tax policy, control of money).

| ⭐ | **`R = t * Y(t)`** (t = tax rate, Y(t) = national income, R = government revenue) | [W5-S24] |

**Table 5-3, price elasticity of demand classes** [W5-S19]: 0 = perfectly inelastic (vertical D; price has no effect on Qd) · between 0 and 1 = inelastic (a price rise increases TR) · exactly 1 = unit-elastic (TR unchanged) · > 1 = elastic (a price rise reduces TR) · ∞ = perfectly elastic (horizontal D).
**Class examples** [W5-S8]: eggs 0.1, beef 0.4, stationery 0.5, gasoline 0.5 (inelastic); housing 1.2, restaurant meals 2.3, airline travel 2.4, foreign travel 4.1 (elastic).

## 5. Methods: step-by-step recipes from the professor's worked examples

### 5.1 Comparative advantage: his 3 steps [W2-S13, S14, S24, S25; S9, S10]
0. Get each side's two maximums (the PPF intercepts). From x/a + y/b = 1, a = the most x and b = the most y.
1. **Step 1, opportunity-cost table.** OC of one unit = (max of the other good) ÷ (max of this good). Rows = goods, columns = people or countries; write the unit.
2. **Step 2, comparative advantage.** In each **row**, compare **across** the two sides; the lower OC has the comparative advantage. ⚠️ Don't compare one side's own two numbers: the S13 bracket does that, and it gives the wrong answer on S24. Absolute advantage (producing more) is irrelevant here.
3. **Step 3, is the deal possible and are both better off?**
   - (1) Each side's minimum = amount it gives × its own OC of one unit given. If it receives at least that much, it is "more than happy". Tom: 10 × 3/4 = 7.5 < 10 ✓. Hank: 10 × 1/2 = 5 < 10 ✓.
   - (2) After-trade bundle = production − amount given + amount received. It must lie **outside** the side's own PPF (point test > 1): "They could not reach this without the trade." Gains = with trade − without trade.
   - Quick range check: a workable price for one unit lies between the two OCs (1 fish : 3/4 ~ 2 coconuts; 1 part : 4 ~ 10 sugar, and the S24 deal is 8).

### 5.2 Opportunity-cost table and point test from PPF equations [W2-S25, S5]
- US x/20 + y/5 = 1 (x = sugar, y = parts): 1 sugar = 5/20 = 1/4 part; 1 part = 20/5 = 4 sugar. Brazil x/40 + y/4 = 1: 1 sugar = 1/10 part; 1 part = 10 sugar.
- To test a point, plug it in. US (16, 1): 16/20 + 1/5 = 1, so it is on the PPF. US (16, 3): 0.8 + 0.6 = 1.4 > 1, so it is outside (reachable only with trade).

### 5.3 The marginal decision: hire or not [W1-S32]
1. MC of one more worker = w. 2. MB = p × `MP_L`, with p given by the market. 3. If w < p × `MP_L`, hire. 4. Repeat worker by worker. Because `MP_L` falls with diminishing returns [W6-S5], stop at the first worker for whom the condition fails.

### 5.4 Equilibrium, shortage/surplus, and shifts [W3-S10, S18–S19, S29–S41]
1. Put Qd and Qs side by side at each price. Equilibrium is the price where Qd = Qs.
2. Above equilibrium: surplus = Qs − Qd, and the price falls. Below: shortage = Qd − Qs, and the price rises. If a policy keeps it in place: a kept surplus → buyers market; a kept shortage → sellers market [S35].
3. For an event: did the good's **own price** change? Then it is a movement along the curve. If anything else changed ("other things equal or not?"), the curve shifts. Then: which curve, which way, is there a shortage or surplus at the old price, and what are the new P and Q? The market moves **along the other curve** [S38, S39].

| D shifts **right** when… [W3-S18, S19] | S shifts **right** when… [W3-S28–S30] |
|---|---|
| a substitute's price rises (tea ↑ → coffee demand ↑) | an input's price falls |
| a complement's price falls | the price of a substitute in production falls |
| income rises (normal good) / income falls (inferior good) | the price of a complement in production rises |
| tastes change in favor (or weather, a doctor's new advice ⭐ S10) | technology improves |
| the price is expected to **rise** in the future | the price is expected to **fall** in the future |
| the number of consumers rises | the number of producers rises |

The opposite change shifts the curve left. ⚠️ Trap: an expected future price rise means demand ↑ today but supply ↓ today.

### 5.5 Welfare change under a ceiling, floor or quota [W4-S3, S13, S14, S20, S21, S28, S32]
1. With no intervention: CS = the triangle under D and above Pe; PS = the triangle above S and below Pe; welfare = CS + PS.
2. Find the quantity actually traded: with a ceiling it is Qs at Pc; with a floor it is Qd at Pf; with a quota it is the quota.
3. Read the price on the **other** curve at that quantity: the demand price (ceiling) or the supply price (floor). With a quota you need both.
4. The areas after the policy:
   - Ceiling: CS = under D and above Pc, from 0 to Qc. PS = the triangle above S and below Pc.
   - Floor: CS = the triangle above Pf. PS = above S and below Pf, from 0 to Qf.
   - Quota: CS sits above the demand price, PS below the supply price, and the green rent rectangle in between.
5. DWL = the triangle between D and S from the traded quantity to Qe = ½ × price gap × (Qe − traded Q).
6. Transfers: under a ceiling, the (Pe − Pc) × Qc rectangle moves from PS to CS. Under a floor, (Pf − Pe) × Qf moves from CS to PS.

| Price ceiling: inefficiencies [W4-S15–S17] | Price floor: inefficiencies [W4-S22–S24] |
|---|---|
| (1) Inefficient allocation to consumers | Inefficient allocation of sales among sellers |
| (2) Wasted resources ✏️ "people will spend much time and money to rent houses" | Wasted resources (government purchases of unwanted surpluses; surpluses destroyed; produce goes "out of condition") |
| (3) Inefficiently low quality | Inefficiently high quality ✏️ "It tends to be buyer's market" |
| (4) Black markets ✏️ landlords "ask extra money … illegally" | Illegal activity: "bribery and corruption" ✏️ paying government inspectors |
| Result: a **persistent shortage** [S18] | Result: a **persistent surplus** [S24] |

Quota side effects [W4-S31]: missed mutually beneficial transactions, and incentives for illegal activities.

### 5.6 Quota rent [W4-S27–S30]
1. The demand price of the quota quantity = the price at which Qd equals the quota (taxi: 8 million rides → $6.00).
2. The supply price = the price at which Qs equals the quota ($4.00).
3. Wedge = quota rent per unit = $2.00. Total rent = $2 × 8m = $16m; DWL = ½ × 2 × (10 − 8)m = $2m.
4. Story check [S30]: a renter keeps 6.00 − rent and needs at least 4.00. Drivers outbid each other ($1.00 → $1.50 → …) up to exactly 6.00 − 4.00 = $2.00. At $2.50 the renter would keep only $3.50.

### 5.7 Producer pays vs consumer pays [W4-S33, S34, S36; W5-S21, S22]
1. Find the quantity at which demand price − supply price = t (taxi: t = $2 → 8m rides, $6 and $4).
2. Tax on the producer: S shifts up by t. The consumer pays the producer $6.00, the producer hands $2.00 to the government, and the producer's "real earnings" are $4.00.
3. Tax on the consumer: D shifts down by t. The consumer pays $6.00 in total ($4.00 to the producer + $2.00 tax).
4. Pc, Pp, quantity, revenue (t × Q = $16m) and DWL are all the same → "The same!". In general, Pc = Pp + t.

### 5.8 Tax incidence and elasticity [W4-S34, S37; W5-S2, S23]
1. Compare with the old price: consumer burden = Pc − Pe; producer burden = Pe − Pp. In the taxi case it is $6 − $5 = $1 and $5 − $4 = $1 (arithmetic).
2. Which side bears more? Inelastic demand (buyers still buy as many) → buyers; elastic demand → producers [W4-S37]. Flat D + steep S → mainly the producer; steep D + flat S → mainly the consumer [W5-S23]. The steep (inelastic) side carries more.

### 5.9 Simple % vs midpoint elasticity [W5-S4–S6, S19]
1. Simple method: %ΔQ = ΔQ ÷ initial Q, %ΔP = ΔP ÷ initial P, e = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP (absolute value). The answer depends on direction: on S5, A→B gives 18.18 ÷ 22.22 = 0.82 and B→A gives 22.22 ÷ 18.18 = 1.22.
2. Midpoint method: divide each change by its average. S5: 200/1,000 = 20% and 0.20/1.00 = 20%, so e = 1.00 in both directions.
3. Classify the value: 0, between 0 and 1, 1, > 1, or ∞ (§3.5).
4. Check the class with total revenue (5.10). On S5/S10, TR is $990 at both points, so the demand is unit-elastic, which only the midpoint answer predicts.

### 5.10 The total-revenue test using dR/dP [W5-S10–S12]
1. Compute TR = P × Q before and after.
2. Split the change: price effect (+ΔP × new Q, area C) vs quantity effect (− old P × ΔQ, area A). On S10, A = C = $180.
3. Use dR/dP = Q(1 − e): if e < 1, it is positive → raise P to raise R. If e = 1, it is 0 → R is at its max or min. If e > 1, it is negative → cut P to raise R.

### 5.11 The cost table [W6-S12, S16, S20]
- Columns in order: Q (or L and Q) · FC · VC · TC = FC + VC · MC = ΔTC/ΔQ (written between rows; = ΔVC) · AFC = FC/Q · AVC = VC/Q · ATC = TC/Q. Check: AFC + AVC = ATC.
- Worker version [S12]: MPL = ΔQ/ΔL, VC = wage × L, MC per unit = wage ÷ MPL.
- Minimum-cost output = the lowest ATC. Check that MC < ATC just before it and MC > ATC just after it. Ben's Boots: 3 pairs, $72. Wheat farm: 75 bushels, $18.67 (arithmetic).

### 5.12 The MC = ATC proof [W6-S27, S28]
1. For a minimum, set dATC/dQ = 0, with ATC = TC(Q)/Q.
2. dATC/dQ = [dTC/dQ · Q − TC(Q)]/Q² = [MC·Q − TC]/Q² = (1/Q)(MC − TC/Q) = **(1/Q)(MC − ATC)**. 🔎 The fraction rule used in the first step.
3. Since 1/Q > 0: MC > ATC → ATC is rising; MC = ATC → minimum; MC < ATC → ATC is falling.
4. AVC works the same way with VC in place of TC, since dVC/dQ = MC because FC doesn't change.
5. His intuition: if the average is 175, a newcomer above 175 raises it and a newcomer below lowers it. MC is the newcomer and ATC is the average. Boots: the 3rd pair costs $60 < $78 → ATC falls to $72; the 4th costs $84 > $72 → ATC rises to $75.

### 5.13 Slope, increasing/decreasing, max/min [W2-S37–S41]
1. Find f′(x) by following the slide's pattern: 2x² − 8x + 9 → 4x − 8. 🔎 The general rule.
2. Slope at x₀ = f′(x₀). If it is positive, f is increasing; if negative, decreasing.
3. Solve f′(x) = 0. 4. Find f″ (the derivative of f′): if f″ < 0 it is a maximum; if f″ > 0 a minimum. 5. Substitute x back into f to get the value. Slide: minimum at x = 2, y = 1.
