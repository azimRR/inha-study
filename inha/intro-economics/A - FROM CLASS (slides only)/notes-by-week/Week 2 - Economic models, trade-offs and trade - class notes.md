## 1. Big picture

- Economists use **models**, "simplified representations of reality", together with the **other things equal (ceteris paribus)** assumption [S2]–[S3].
- The **production possibility frontier (PPF)** shows the maximum amount of one good for any given amount of the other, and it shows two things: (1) trade-offs and (2) opportunity cost. A point on the curve is feasible and efficient, a point inside is feasible but not efficient, and a point outside is not feasible. A bowed-out shape means increasing opportunity cost, and an outward shift means economic growth [S4]–[S7].
- **Comparative advantage** means a lower opportunity cost than other people. It is *not* the same thing as absolute advantage. When each side specializes in its comparative advantage and they trade, *both* consume more of both goods. This holds even for the side with an absolute disadvantage in both goods (Tom & Hank; U.S. & Brazil) [S8]–[S17].
- ⭐ **The professor's 3-step "Full Analysis":**
  - Step 1: build the opportunity-cost table.
  - Step 2: decide who has comparative advantage in what.
  - Step 3: check whether the deal is possible (each side must receive at least "amount given × its own opportunity cost") and whether both are better off (each reaches a point it could not reach without trade) [S13]–[S14], [S24]–[S25].
  - The trade ratios that work lie between the two opportunity costs, e.g. 1 fish : (3/4 ~ 2) coconuts [S10].
- The **circular-flow diagram**: households and firms meet in *markets for goods and services* and in *factor markets*. Money flows one way around the circle; goods and services and factors flow the other way [S18]–[S21].
- **Positive economics** describes how the economy actually works. **Normative economics** prescribes how it should work. A **forecast** is a simple prediction of the future [S22]–[S23].
- **Appendix (graphs)** covers:
  - variables, causal relationships, and dependent vs independent variables
  - slope = rise over run, and linear vs nonlinear curves
  - time-series graphs, scatter diagrams, pie charts and bar graphs
  - pitfalls: truncated axes and scale, omitted variables, reverse causality
  - Slides: [S27]–[S36], [S43]–[S51].
- ⭐ **Professor's math:**
  - f′(x) = the slope of the tangent line, and its sign tells you whether f is increasing or decreasing.
  - Maximum: f′(x) = 0 and f″(x) < 0. Minimum: f′(x) = 0 and f″(x) > 0.
  - A preview of elastic vs inelastic.

### [S12] Comparative advantage vs absolute advantage — definitions
- "An individual has a **comparative advantage** in producing a good or service if the opportunity cost of producing the good is lower for that individual than for other people."
- "An individual has an **absolute advantage** in an activity if he or she can do it better than other people. Having an absolute advantage is not the same thing as having a comparative advantage."
- **Class illustration [S11]:** Tom catches more fish (40 vs 10) *and* gathers more coconuts (30 vs 20), so he has an absolute advantage in both. Hank still has the comparative advantage in coconuts.

- **Four boxes:** **Households** (top), **Firms** (bottom), **Markets for goods and services** (left), **Factor markets** (right).

### [S29] How Graphs Work — Curves on a Graph (linear vs nonlinear)
- "A **curve** is a line on a graph that depicts a relationship between two variables. It may be either a straight line or a curved line. If the curve is a straight line, the variables have a **linear relationship**. If the curve is not a straight line, the variables have a **nonlinear relationship**."

### [S30] How Graphs Work — Curves on a Graph (positive/negative relationship, intercepts)
- "Two variables have a **positive relationship** when an increase in the value of one variable is associated with an increase in the value of the other variable. It is illustrated by a curve that slopes upward from left to right."
- "Two variables have a **negative relationship** when an increase in the value of one variable is associated with a decrease in the value of the other variable. It is illustrated by a curve that slopes downward from left to right."
- "The **horizontal intercept** of a curve is the point at which it hits the horizontal axis; it indicates the value of the x-variable when the value of the y-variable is zero."
- "The **vertical intercept** of a curve is the point at which it hits the vertical axis; it shows the value of the y-variable when the value of the x-variable is zero."
- **Link:** the PPF end points (e.g. 20 sugar and 5 parts on [S25]) are its horizontal and vertical intercepts. Every PPF in the deck slopes downward, which is a negative relationship.

### [S31] A Key Concept: The Slope of a Curve — definition and formula
- "The **slope** of a line or curve is a measure of how steep it is. The slope of a line is measured by "rise over run"—the change in the y-variable between two points on the line divided by the change in the x-variable between those same two points."
- **"The Slope of a Linear Curve":** (Change in y) / (Change in x) = Δy/Δx = Slope.
- ⚠️ **Display glitch:** on the slide the formula shows "Dy/Dx". It means **Δy/Δx** (the Greek Δ is rendered as "D"); [S32] writes it with Δ.

### [S33] Horizontal and Vertical Curves and Their Slopes
- "When a curve is horizontal, the value of y along that curve never changes—it is constant. The slope of a horizontal curve is always zero."
- "If a curve is vertical, the value of x along the curve never changes—it is constant. The slope of a vertical line is equal to infinity."
- "A vertical or a horizontal curve has a special implication: it means that the x-variable and the y-variable are unrelated."

- **Outside the range:** a price of 2.5 coconuts per fish would be refused by Hank, and 0.5 by Tom.

- **Step 3 (2) Are both better off?**
  - At 20 cotton, K's own PPF allows only 6 × (1 − 20/30) = **2 cars**. K has 4, so K could not reach (20, 4) before. Compared with (15, 3) it has +5 cotton and +1 car.
  - At 40 cotton, U's own PPF allows only 3 × (1 − 40/60) = **1 car**. U has 2, so U could not reach (40, 2) before; it gains +1 car.
  - Both are better off.
- **Combined line (S25 style):** it joins K's max cars (6) and U's max cotton (60): **x/60 + y/6 = 1**.
  - K (20, 4): 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 ✓. U (40, 2): 2/3 + 1/3 = 1 ✓.
  - This works because the deal ratio of 10 cotton per car equals 60 ÷ 6, just as 16 ÷ 2 = 8 = 40 ÷ 5 on [S24]. With a different ratio the bundles would not lie on this line.
- **Range:** 1 car : (5 ~ 20) cotton. The deal (10 cotton per car) is inside ✓.
  - "2 cars for 8 cotton": K needs at least 10, so **not possible**.
  - "2 cars for 44 cotton": U needs at least 44 × 1/20 = 2.2 cars for 44 cotton but would get only 2, so **not possible** (22 per car is above 20).

### 5.13 NEW PRACTICE — derivative, max/min (same type as [S37]–[S41]; invented numbers)
🔎 The rule for differentiating is a Track B item. Below, each derivative follows the same pattern as the slide example (2x² → 4x, −8x → −8, +9 → 0).

**M-1.** y = 3x² − 12x + 5.
- **Tasks:** (a) the slope of the tangent at x = 1 and at x = 4; (b) increasing or decreasing at each; (c) where f′(x) = 0, whether it is a max or min, and the value of y there.
- **Solution:**
  - The derivative is y′ = f′(x) = **6x − 12**.
  - (a) f′(1) = 6 − 12 = **−6**; f′(4) = 24 − 12 = **12**.
  - (b) At x = 1, f′ < 0, so **decreasing**. At x = 4, f′ > 0, so **increasing** [S37].
  - (c) 6x − 12 = 0 gives **x = 2**. f″(x) = **6 > 0**, so this is a **minimum** [S41]. y = 3·4 − 24 + 5 = **−7**, so the minimum is at (2, −7).
  - The sign of f′ goes − (x = 1) → 0 (x = 2) → + (x = 4), exactly the [S41] pattern.

**M-2.** y = −2x² + 16x + 10.
- **Tasks:** (a) f′(x); (b) the x where f′(x) = 0, and whether it is a max or min; (c) the extreme value; (d) the slope at x = 1 and at x = 6.
- **Solution:**
  - (a) f′(x) = **−4x + 16**.
  - (b) −4x + 16 = 0 gives **x = 4**. f″(x) = **−4 < 0**, so this is a **maximum** [S40].
  - (c) y(4) = −32 + 64 + 10 = **42**.
  - (d) f′(1) = **12** > 0 (increasing); f′(6) = −24 + 16 = **−8** < 0 (decreasing). That is "positive to zero to then negative" [S40].
