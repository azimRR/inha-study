## 1. Big picture

- **What the model is for:** "The supply and demand model is a model of how a competitive market works." A competitive market has "many buyers and sellers of the same good or service", so ⭐ "no one can influence on determining the market price" [S3].
- **The five key elements of the model** [S4]: (1) the demand curve, (2) the supply curve, (3) the factors that shift the demand curve and the factors that shift the supply curve, (4) the equilibrium price, (5) how the equilibrium price changes when the supply or demand curves shift.
- **Demand** [S5–S19]: law of demand: a higher price, *other things equal*, means a smaller quantity demanded, so the demand curve slopes downward [S6–S7]. Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands [S8]. Demand shifts with the prices of related goods (substitutes/complements), income (normal/inferior goods), tastes and expectations [S13], plus the number of consumers [S19].
- **Supply** [S20–S30]: the quantity supplied rises when the price rises, and supply curves are usually upward sloping [S21]. Market supply is the horizontal sum of individual supplies [S23]. Supply shifts with input prices, prices of related goods or services, technology, expectations and the number of producers [S27–S30].
- **Movement vs shift** [S10–S12, S24–S26]: a change in the good's **own price** moves you **along** the curve. A change in **anything else** **shifts** the whole curve (right = increase, left = decrease).
- **Equilibrium** [S31–S37]: Qd = Qs where the curves cross (ticket example: **$250, 8,000 tickets**) [S32]. If the price is above equilibrium there is a surplus and the price falls [S34]. If it is below, there is a shortage and the price rises [S36]. "The market price always moves toward the equilibrium price" [S37].
- **Shifts change the equilibrium** [S38–S41]: demand ↑ gives P↑ Q↑ [S38]. Supply ↑ gives P↓ Q↑ [S39]. Demand ↑ with supply ↓ at the same time gives P↑, and Q↑ or Q↓ depending on which shift is larger [S40]. Supply ↓ (war on drugs) gives P↑, with Q falling much less than the shift [S41].
- **⭐ The professor's own notes to know cold:**
  - Y axis = Price, X axis = Quantity [S6].
  - Shift vs movement: "Other things being equal or not?", with the examples weather, doctor's new advice and taste change [S10].
  - Substitutes/complements: the ∂Y/∂Px signs and the coffee examples [S14].
  - If the government keeps a surplus in place, the result is a "Buyers market". If it keeps a shortage in place, the result is a "Sellers market" [S35].
  - The perfectly competitive ↔ monopoly spectrum [S42].
  - The K-pop concert opener [S2].

### [S10] The Demand Curve — Shifts of the Demand Curve (shift vs movement)
- "A **shift of the demand curve** is a change in the quantity demanded at any given price, represented by the change of the original demand curve to a new position, denoted by a new demand curve."
- ⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN NOTE — high exam priority (added sentence): "It can happen because of other changes than change in its own price. For example, weather, doctor's new advice, taste change."
- "A **movement along the demand curve** is a change in the quantity demanded of a good that is the result of a change in its own price."
- ⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN NOTE — high exam priority (box on the right, with "equal or not?" in red): **"Other things being equal or not?"**
  - How to read it, using the logic of [S7] + [S10]:
    - If only the good's own price changes and other things stay **equal**, you get a **movement along** the curve (the law of demand).
    - If some **other** thing changes (other things **not** equal, e.g. weather, a doctor's advice, tastes), you get a **shift** of the curve.

### [S14] Understanding Shifts of the Demand Curve — Changes in the Prices of Related Goods
- ⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN NOTE — high exam priority: these parts are additions (red or equation-style text):
  - the reverse cases in red: "(increase)", "(more)", "(less)";
  - the ∂ formulas;
  - the coffee examples.
- (In the exported slide image both boxes appear **upside-down** (flipped vertically). I read the text by flipping the image. This text is also missing from the raw text extract.)
- **Top box — Substitutes:** "Two goods are **substitutes** if a fall (increase) in the price of one of the goods makes consumers less (more) willing to buy the other good: ∂Y/∂Px > 0"
  - "ex: Coffee and Tea"
- **Bottom box — Complements:** "Two goods are **complements** if a fall (increase) in the price of one good makes people more (less) willing to buy the other good: : ∂Y/∂Px < 0 :"
  - "ex: Coffee and Sugar"
- **How to read the slide's formula:** Px = the price of one good (x). Y = the other good (how much of it people are willing to buy).
  - **> 0** means both move the same way: Px ↑ → more Y bought. These are **substitutes**.
  - **< 0** means they move in opposite ways: Px ↑ → less Y bought. These are **complements**.
  - This matches the slide's red "(increase) … (more)/(less)" wording.
- ⚠️ Possible slide typo: there are stray colons around the complements formula ("good: : ∂Y/∂Px < 0 :"). The symbols Y and Px are not defined on the slide.

### [S35] Why Does the Market Price Fall If It Is Above the Equilibrium Price? — Excess Supply / Excess Demand
- **Excess Supply:** "There is a **surplus** of a good when the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded. Surpluses occur when the price is above its equilibrium level:"
  - ⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN NOTE — high exam priority: "Suppose government has a policy that makes this surplus stays. What happens? → **Buyers market**."
- **Excess Demand:** "There is a **shortage** of a good when the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied. Shortages occur when the price is below its equilibrium level."
  - ⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN NOTE — high exam priority: "Suppose government has a policy that makes this shortage stays. What happens? → **Sellers market**."
- **The logic, using the slide's own definitions:**
  - If a surplus is kept in place, sellers keep wanting to sell more than buyers want to buy (Qs > Qd). Buyers are the scarce side, so it is a **buyers market**.
  - If a shortage is kept in place, buyers keep wanting more than is offered (Qd > Qs). Sellers are the scarce side, so it is a **sellers market**.
  - Normally the price would move to equilibrium [S34, S36, S37]. Here the policy keeps the surplus or shortage from going away.
- 🔎 Look up in Track B: the standard meaning of "buyers' market" / "sellers' market".
- ⚠️ Minor grammar on the slide: "makes this surplus stays / shortage stays" (should be "stay").

### 5.3 The law of demand and the upward-sloping supply in the ticket schedules [S6, S21]
- As the price falls from $350 to $100, **quantity demanded rises** at every step:
  - 5,000 → 6,000 → 8,000 → 11,000 → 15,000 → 20,000.

- As the price falls from $350 to $100, **quantity supplied falls** at every step:
  - 8,800 → 8,500 → 8,000 → 7,000 → 5,000 → 2,000.
  - This is "the quantity supplied rises when the price rises", and it is why the curve is "upward sloping" [S21].

### 5.6 Movement vs shift with numbers [S11, S25]
- **Demand** [S11]:
  - A ($350, 2,500) → B ($215, 5,000): the price fell by $135 and Qd rose by 2,500. This is a **movement along D1**.
  - A ($350, 2,500) → C ($350, 5,000): the price is unchanged and Qd rose by 2,500. This is a **shift from D1 to D2**.
- **Supply** [S25]:
  - A ($250, 9,000) → B ($200, 8,000): the price fell by $50 and Qs fell by 1,000. This is a **movement along S1**.
  - A ($250, 9,000) → C ($250, 8,000): the price is unchanged and Qs fell by 1,000. This is a **shift from S1 to S2**.
- The test: **did the good's own price change?** If yes, it is a movement. If the quantity changed at the same price, it is a shift [S10, S24].

### 5.7 Equilibrium, surplus and shortage from the ticket schedules [S6 + S21 → S32, S34, S36]
Put the demand schedule [S6] and the supply schedule [S21] side by side:

| Price | Qd [S6] | Qs [S21] | Qs − Qd | Result | Price will… |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $350 | 5,000 | 8,800 | +3,800 | **Surplus 3,800** (✓ S34) | fall |
| $300 | 6,000 | 8,500 | +2,500 | Surplus 2,500 | fall |
| **$250** | **8,000** | **8,000** | **0** | **Equilibrium** (✓ S32) | stay |
| $200 | 11,000 | 7,000 | −4,000 | Shortage 4,000 | rise |
| $150 | 15,000 | 5,000 | −10,000 | **Shortage 10,000** (✓ S36) | rise |
| $100 | 20,000 | 2,000 | −18,000 | Shortage 18,000 | rise |

- **Step 1:** find the price where Qd = Qs. That is $250, with 8,000 tickets: the **equilibrium price** (market-clearing price) and **equilibrium quantity** [S31–S32].
- **Step 2:** above $250, Qs > Qd, so there is a **surplus**. "This surplus will push the price down until it reaches the equilibrium price of $250." [S34]
- **Step 3:** below $250, Qd > Qs, so there is a **shortage**. "This shortage will push the price up until it reaches the equilibrium price of $250." [S36]
- **Note:** the "after announcement" schedules on [S9] and [S22] are exactly these two schedules. So after the Gretzky announcement the ticket market's equilibrium is $250 and 8,000 tickets.

### Answers

**MC1 — B.** "Be careful! In economics, the Y axis is always for the Price, and X axis is the quantity." ⭐ [S6]

**MC2 — B.** "…many buyers and sellers of the same good or service. So no one can influence on determining the market price." (⭐ the red part) [S3]

**MC3 — C.** Technology shifts **supply** [S27–S28]. The four demand shifters are the prices of related goods, income, tastes and expectations [S13].

**MC4 — C.** A change in the good's own price is a movement along the demand curve [S10]. A lower price raises the quantity demanded [S6–S7]. This is A→B on [S11]. D is wrong because a lower price means a *smaller* quantity supplied [S21].

**MC5 — B.** "ex: Coffee and Sugar" are complements, with ∂Y/∂Px < 0 [S14].

**MC6 — B.** "When a rise in income decreases the demand for a good, it is an inferior good." [S15]

**MC7 — B.** At $350, Qs = 8,800 and Qd = 5,000, so the surplus is 8,800 − 5,000 = 3,800. The price will fall to $250 [S34].

**MC8 — C.** "When supply of a good increases, the equilibrium price of the good falls and the equilibrium quantity rises." [S39]

**MC9 — B.** "→ Buyers market." A kept shortage gives a "Sellers market". ⭐ [S35]

**MC10 — B.** "The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of the individual demand curves of all consumers." [S8]

**SA1.**
- The slide's wording: "The law of demand says that a higher price for a good, other things equal, leads people to demand a smaller quantity of the good." [S7]
- "Other things equal" (red on the slide) means only the good's own price is changing. That gives a **movement along** the demand curve.
- If other things change (e.g., weather, a doctor's new advice, tastes), the whole curve **shifts**. This is the professor's question "Other things being equal or not?" ⭐ [S10]

**SA2.**
- **Shift:** "a change in the quantity demanded at any given price, represented by the change of the original demand curve to a new position, denoted by a new demand curve". It is caused by changes other than the good's own price.
  - Professor's examples ⭐: "weather, doctor's new advice, taste change" [S10].
  - Main factors: related goods' prices, income, tastes, expectations [S13], and the number of consumers [S19].
- **Movement:** "a change in the quantity demanded of a good that is the result of a change in its own price" [S10].
- **On the graph** [S11]:
  - A→B is a movement along D1: the price fell from $350 to $215 and Qd rose from 2,500 to 5,000.
  - A→C is a shift from D1 to D2: at the same $350, Qd rose from 2,500 to 5,000.

**SA3.**
- The slide's wording: "A competitive market is in equilibrium when price has moved to a level at which the quantity demanded of a good equals the quantity supplied of that good. The price at which this takes place is the equilibrium price, also referred to as the market-clearing price. The quantity of the good bought and sold at that price is the equilibrium quantity." [S31]
- On a graph it is point **E**, where the supply and demand curves intersect. For tickets that is $250 and 8,000 [S32].

**SA4.**
- **Surplus** (excess supply): "the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded". It occurs when the price is above equilibrium. The surplus pushes the price **down** [S34, S35].
- **Shortage** (excess demand): "the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied". It occurs when the price is below equilibrium. The shortage pushes the price **up** [S35, S36].
- "The market price always moves toward the equilibrium price, the price at which there is neither surplus nor shortage." [S37]

**SA5.** ⭐
- A surplus kept in place by policy → "**Buyers market**". A shortage kept in place → "**Sellers market**" [S35].
- **Why:** a kept surplus means Qs > Qd keeps going: more is offered than buyers want, so buyers are the scarce side. A kept shortage means Qd > Qs keeps going: buyers want more than is offered, so sellers are the scarce side.
- Normally the price would move to equilibrium and remove the surplus/shortage [S34, S36, S37]. Here the policy stops that.
- (🔎 Track B for the formal meaning of these terms.)

**C1.**
- (a) 3 + 2 = **5 pairs** [S8].
- (b) At $1: 2 + 1 = **3** thousand lb. At $2: 3 + 2 = **5** thousand lb [S23]. Market quantity = horizontal sum.

**C2.** Qd = Qs only at **$250**, where 8,000 = 8,000. So the equilibrium price is **$250** and the equilibrium quantity is **8,000 tickets** [S6, S21, S32].

**C3.**
- $300: Qs 8,500 − Qd 6,000 = **surplus 2,500**, so the price falls.
- $200: Qd 11,000 − Qs 7,000 = **shortage 4,000**, so the price rises.
- $100: Qd 20,000 − Qs 2,000 = **shortage 18,000**, so the price rises.
- Same method as the slides' own cases: $350 gives a surplus of 3,800 [S34] and $150 gives a shortage of 10,000 [S36].

**C4.**
- (a) Qd rises from 5,500 to 11,000, i.e. by **+5,500** [S9].
- (b) Qs falls from 8,000 to 7,000, i.e. by **−1,000** [S22].
- (c) The "after" columns are the [S6] and [S21] schedules, so the equilibrium is **$250 and 8,000 tickets** [S32].

**G1.**
- Tea is a **substitute** for coffee [S14]. "When the price of a substitute rises . . . demand for the original good increases" [S18].
- So the **demand curve for coffee shifts right** (D1 → D2).
- At the old price P1 there is a shortage. The market moves along the supply curve to E2.
- Result: **P ↑ and Q ↑** [S38].

**G2.**
- A technology change increases supply [S28, S30], so the **supply curve shifts right** (S1 → S2).
- At P1 there is a surplus. The price falls, and the market moves along the demand curve to E2.
- Result: **P ↓ and Q ↑** [S39].

**G3.**
- The **supply curve shifts left** (S1 → S2).
- The equilibrium price rises from P1 to P2, which is a movement along the demand curve. So **P ↑ and Q ↓**.
- However, "the actual reduction in the quantity of drugs supplied is much smaller than the shift of the supply curve" [S41].

**G4.**
- Demand shifts **right** and supply shifts **left** at the same time. This is the [S40] case, and its axes are "Price of ticket"/"Quantity of tickets".
- **The price rises** for sure.
- **The quantity is uncertain:** it rises if the demand increase is relatively larger (panel a). It falls if the supply decrease is relatively larger (panel b) [S40].

**G5.**
- Demand: "When the price is expected to rise in the future . . . demand for the good increases today" [S19]. So **D shifts right**.
- Supply: "When the price is expected to rise in the future . . . supply of the good decreases today" [S30]. So **S shifts left**.
- This is a simultaneous D-right / S-left shift. **P rises.** **Q rises or falls** depending on which shift is relatively larger [S40].

**G6.**
- "When the price of an input falls . . . supply of the good increases" [S29]. So **S shifts right**.
- Result: **P ↓ and Q ↑** [S39 rule].
