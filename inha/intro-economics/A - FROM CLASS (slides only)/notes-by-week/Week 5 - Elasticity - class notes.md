## 1. Big picture

- **Elasticity** is "a measure of responsiveness to changes in prices or incomes" [S2]. The deck covers four kinds: price elasticity of demand, cross-price elasticity of demand, income elasticity of demand, and price elasticity of supply [S2, S4, S14–S16].
- **Price elasticity of demand** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price, measured "as we move along the demand curve", and taken as an absolute value [S4, S19]. If you compute % changes from the *initial* value, you get different answers depending on direction (0.82 one way, 1.22 the other way in the professor's example). The **midpoint method** divides by the *average* of the start and end values, so it gives one answer both ways [S5, S6].
- **Classes of demand:** 0 = perfectly inelastic (vertical curve); between 0 and 1 = inelastic; exactly 1 = unit-elastic; greater than 1 = elastic; ∞ = perfectly elastic (horizontal curve) [S7, S19]. Estimated values run from eggs (0.1) to foreign travel (4.1) [S8]. The four determinants are close substitutes, necessity vs luxury, time, and the share of the budget/income [S9, S13].
- **Elasticity decides what a price change does to total revenue** (R = P × Q). The professor's math gives **dR/dP = Q(1 − e)**. If demand is inelastic (e < 1), raising the price raises revenue. If e = 1, revenue does not change (it is at a maximum or minimum). If demand is elastic (e > 1), raising the price lowers revenue [S10–S12, S19].
- **Other elasticities [S14–S20]:**
  - Cross-price elasticity > 0 means substitutes; < 0 means complements.
  - Income elasticity < 0 means an inferior good; > 0 means a normal good (> 1 is income-elastic, between 0 and 1 is income-inelastic).
  - Price elasticity of supply runs from 0 (vertical supply curve) to ∞ (horizontal supply curve). It depends on the availability of inputs and on time.
- **Excise tax [S21, S22]:** the tax t can be levied on producers (supply shifts **up** by t) or on consumers (demand shifts **down** by t). Either way, consumers pay Pc and producers keep Pp, with Pc = Pp + t, and the quantity traded falls. The diagrams show consumer surplus, producer surplus, tax revenue and deadweight loss.
- **Tax incidence** is "the measure of who bears [a tax's] burden" [S2]. The burden goes mainly to the **producer** when demand is elastic and supply is inelastic, and mainly to the **consumer** when demand is inelastic and supply is elastic [S23].
- **Laffer curve:** R = t · Y(t). As the tax rate rises, government revenue first rises, reaches a peak, then falls. So a higher tax rate does not always raise tax revenue [S24].

### [S6] Using the Midpoint Method to Calculate Elasticities
- Definition (verbatim): "The **midpoint method** is a technique for calculating the percent change. In this approach, we calculate changes in a variable compared with the average, or midpoint, of the starting and final values."
- **(5-4)** % change in X = (Change in X ÷ Average value of X) × 100
- **(5-5)**
  ```
                                (Q2 - Q1) / [(Q1 + Q2)/2]
  Price elasticity of demand = ---------------------------
                                (P2 - P1) / [(P1 + P2)/2]
  ```
- **Why it fixes S5:** the averages (Q1 + Q2)/2 and (P1 + P2)/2 are the same whichever point you call "1" and which "2". So A → B and B → A give the same number: 1.00 for the S5 data (§6.1).

### [S11] Mathematics for Revenue and Elasticity
⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN SLIDE — high exam priority
- Text (verbatim): `Revenue = Price * Quantity : R = P * Q`. "So, R is a function of P, but Q is also a function of P. Because when P increases, Q (=demand) decreases." `R = P * Q(P),`
- **Derivation**, transcribed exactly from the slide image:
  ```
  R = P * Q(P)

  dR/dP = Q + (dQ/dP)·P ,   where dQ/dP < 0

  If we divide this equation by Q,

  (dR/dP)·(1/Q) = 1 + (dQ/dP)·(P/Q)     Since elasticity e = |(dQ/Q) / (dP/P)| = |(dQ/dP) / (P/Q)| ,   <- see ⚠️ below

  (dR/dP)·(1/Q) = 1 − e ,   (remember that dQ/dP < 0)

  dR/dP = Q (1 − e)
  ```
- **Step-by-step logic:**
  1. R = P · Q(P). Price affects revenue directly (P) and also through quantity (Q depends on P).
  2. Differentiating with respect to P gives dR/dP = Q + (dQ/dP)·P. The first term is the direct effect of price; the second is the effect through the change in Q. 🔎 Look up in Track B: the "product rule", if this calculus step is new to you.
  3. Dividing both sides by Q gives (dR/dP)(1/Q) = 1 + (dQ/dP)(P/Q).
  4. dQ/dP < 0, and P and Q are positive, so (dQ/dP)(P/Q) is negative. It equals **−e**, where e = |(dQ/dP)(P/Q)|.
  5. So (dR/dP)(1/Q) = 1 − e. Multiplying by Q gives **dR/dP = Q(1 − e)**.
  6. Q is positive, so **the sign of dR/dP is the sign of (1 − e)**. S12 interprets the three cases.
- ⚠️ Possible slide typo: "e = |dQ/Q / dP/P| = |dQ/dP / P/Q|". The last step should **multiply**, not divide: (dQ/Q) ÷ (dP/P) = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q). Correct: **e = |(dQ/Q)/(dP/P)| = |(dQ/dP)·(P/Q)|**. The neighbouring line on the slide already uses the product (dQ/dP)(P/Q), which is the correct form.

### [S12] The Interpretation of the dR/dP = Q(1 − e)
⭐ PROFESSOR'S OWN SLIDE — high exam priority
- Verbatim, with the sign of dR/dP in bold:
  - **(1) dR/dP > 0** "means that, as price goes up, the total revenue increases (even though the demand Q decreases). So, when the price elasticity of demand is inelastic ( e < 1), The seller's revenue will increase if he raises the price."
  - **(2) dR/dP = 0** "means that although the price changes, the total revenue does not change. This is when the elasticity is 1. Also when the elasticity is 1, the total revenue becomes maximum or minimum."
  - **(3) dR/dP < 0** "means that as price goes up, the total revenue decreases. So if the elasticity is elastic ( e > 1) , the seller's revenue will decrease as he increases price. (Even though the price increases, its response, i.e., demand decreases too much.)"
- Summary:

  | e | 1 − e | dR/dP = Q(1 − e) | Seller raises price → total revenue |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | e < 1 (inelastic) | > 0 | > 0 | increases |
  | e = 1 (unit-elastic) | = 0 | = 0 | does not change (revenue is at a maximum or minimum) |
  | e > 1 (elastic) | < 0 | < 0 | decreases |

⭐ PROFESSOR'S ADDITIONS ON THIS SLIDE (the red note and the e_yx lines) — high exam priority
- Definition (verbatim): "The **cross-price elasticity of demand** between two goods measures the effect of the change in one good's price on the quantity demanded of the other good. It is equal to the percent change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percent change in the other good's price."
- **(5-7)** Cross-price elasticity of demand between goods A and B = % change in quantity of A demanded ÷ % change in price of B
- Red text (verbatim): "It is important to remember that the Cross-price elasticity can be either positive or negative:"

### [S21] Excise Tax (1) Tax to Producer

### [S24] Laffer Curve

## 3. Formula sheet

### 3.1 Price elasticity of demand — simple method [S4]
- **(5-1)** % change in quantity demanded = (Change in quantity demanded ÷ Initial quantity demanded) × 100
- **(5-2)** % change in price = (Change in price ÷ Initial price) × 100
- **(5-3)** Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
- Sign rule: "(use absolute value)" [S19].

### 3.2 Midpoint method [S6]
- **(5-4)** % change in X = (Change in X ÷ Average value of X) × 100
- **(5-5)**
  ```
                                (Q2 - Q1) / [(Q1 + Q2)/2]
  Price elasticity of demand = ---------------------------
                                (P2 - P1) / [(P1 + P2)/2]
  ```
  Q1, P1 = starting quantity and price; Q2, P2 = final quantity and price. (5-5) gives a negative number for a demand curve; report its absolute value [S19].

### 3.3 Total revenue and elasticity [S10–S12]
- **Total Revenue (R) = Price × Quantity Sold (Q)** [S10]; the slide writes it as `R = P * Q` [S11].
- The professor's derivation [S11]:
  ```
  R = P * Q(P)                                  (Q depends on P: when P rises, Q falls)
  dR/dP = Q + (dQ/dP)·P,   where dQ/dP < 0
  divide by Q:   (dR/dP)·(1/Q) = 1 + (dQ/dP)·(P/Q)
  elasticity:    e = |(dQ/Q)/(dP/P)| = |(dQ/dP)·(P/Q)|    (slide prints "/ (P/Q)" here: typo)
  since dQ/dP < 0:   (dQ/dP)·(P/Q) = −e
  so:            (dR/dP)·(1/Q) = 1 − e
                 dR/dP = Q(1 − e)
  ```
- **Interpretation [S12]:** dR/dP > 0 when e < 1 (raise price → revenue up); dR/dP = 0 when e = 1 (revenue unchanged; revenue at its maximum or minimum); dR/dP < 0 when e > 1 (raise price → revenue down).

### 3.4 Other demand elasticities [S14, S15, S19, S20]
- **(5-7)** Cross-price elasticity of demand between goods A and B = % change in quantity of A demanded ÷ % change in price of B
  - S19 wording: % change in quantity of one good demanded ÷ % change in price of another good.
  - `e_yx` (X price elasticity on demand for Y): **> 0 → substitutes**, **< 0 → complements**. Keep the sign.
- **(5-8)** Income elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income
  - Negative → inferior good. Positive → normal good. Positive but less than 1 → income-inelastic. Greater than 1 → income-elastic.

### 3.5 Supply [S16, S20]
- **(5-9)** Price elasticity of supply = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
  - 0 → perfectly inelastic (vertical). Between 0 and ∞ → ordinary upward-sloping. ∞ → perfectly elastic (horizontal).

### 3.6 Excise tax [S21, S22]
- **Pc = Pp + t** ("Consumer pays Pc = Pp + t, Government gets t" [S22]). Equivalently, Pp = Pc − t ("Producer will get Pp after paying tax t" [S21]).
- Tax-revenue rectangle: height = Pc − Pp = t, width = the quantity after the tax.

### 3.7 Laffer curve [S24]
- **`R = t * Y(t)`**, where t = tax rate and Y(t) = National Income; R is government revenue.

### 3.8 Symbols and notes
- **e** = price elasticity of demand (absolute value). **R** = total revenue. **P** = price. **Q** = quantity sold/demanded. **Q(P)** = quantity demanded as a function of price.
- **dR/dP** = change in revenue for a change in price. **dQ/dP** = change in quantity demanded for a change in price (< 0).
- **Pc** = price the consumer pays. **Pp** = price the producer gets.
- The deck uses **R** for both total revenue (S10–S12) and government revenue (S24). It uses **t** for both the excise tax (S21–S23) and the tax rate (S24).
- The numbering jumps from (5-5) to (5-7): **there is no (5-6) on the slides.** 🔎 Look up in Track B: equation (5-6) in Krugman ch. 5, if you want it (not covered in class).

## 6. Worked examples from class

### 6.1 Slide 5 — simple % change in both directions, then midpoint [S5, S6] ⭐
Data: **A = ($0.90, 1,100)**, **B = ($1.10, 900)**.

**Simple method** ((5-1) to (5-3), dividing by the *initial* value; signs dropped as on the slide):

| Step | A → B (start at A) | B → A (start at B) |
|---|---|---|
| Change in price | 1.10 − 0.90 = 0.20 | 0.90 − 1.10 = −0.20 → 0.20 |
| Initial price (base) | 0.90 | 1.10 |
| % change in price | 0.20/0.90 × 100 = **22.22%** | 0.20/1.10 × 100 = **18.18%** |
| Change in quantity | 900 − 1,100 = −200 → 200 | 1,100 − 900 = 200 |
| Initial quantity (base) | 1,100 | 900 |
| % change in quantity | 200/1,100 × 100 = **18.18%** | 200/900 × 100 = **22.22%** |
| Elasticity | 18.18 ÷ 22.22 = **0.82** | 22.22 ÷ 18.18 = **1.22** |
| Class [S19] | inelastic (between 0 and 1) | elastic (greater than 1) |

- Why the answers differ: the bases change with the direction (0.90 vs 1.10, and 1,100 vs 900). The two answers are exactly 9/11 and 11/9.
- Slide typos: "(= 18.1 / 22.22)" should be 18.18/22.22, and "(= 22.2 / 1.2)" should be 22.22/18.18 (literally 22.2/1.2 = 18.5). Both final answers (0.82, 1.22) are correct.

**Midpoint method** ((5-4), (5-5)):
1. Average price = (0.90 + 1.10)/2 = **1.00**. Average quantity = (1,100 + 900)/2 = **1,000**.
2. % change in price = 0.20/1.00 × 100 = **20%**. % change in quantity = 200/1,000 × 100 = **20%**.
3. A → B: (−200/1,000) ÷ (+0.20/1.00) = −0.20 ÷ 0.20 = −1, so the absolute value is **1.00**.
4. B → A: (+200/1,000) ÷ (−0.20/1.00) = −1, so the absolute value is **1.00**. **Same answer both ways.**
5. Class: **unit-elastic** ("Exactly 1") [S19].

**Consistency check with S10 (same numbers):** total revenue is $0.90 × 1,100 = **$990** at A and $1.10 × 900 = **$990** at B, so it is unchanged. Unit-elastic means "Changes in price have no effect on total revenue" [S19], which matches the midpoint answer. The simple answers would predict something else:
- 0.82 ("inelastic") predicts that the price rise A → B raises revenue.
- 1.22 ("elastic") predicts that the price fall B → A raises revenue.

Neither happens; only the midpoint answer fits.

### 6.2 Oil example — Figure 5-1 numbers with the S4 and S6 formulas [S3, S4, S6] (my arithmetic)
Data: A = ($20, 10 million barrels/day), B = ($21, 9.9 million barrels/day).
- **Simple (from A):** % change in Q = (10 − 9.9)/10 × 100 = **1%**; % change in P = (21 − 20)/20 × 100 = **5%**; e = 1 ÷ 5 = **0.2**.
- **Simple (from B):** % change in Q = 0.1/9.9 × 100 = 1.01%; % change in P = 1/21 × 100 = 4.76%; e ≈ **0.21**. The direction problem again, though small here.
- **Midpoint:** average Q = 9.95, average P = 20.5. % change in Q = 0.1/9.95 × 100 = 1.005%; % change in P = 1/20.5 × 100 = 4.878%; e = 1.005 ÷ 4.878 ≈ **0.21**.
- **Class:** between 0 and 1, so **inelastic** [S19].
- **Revenue:**
  - R at A = $20 × 10 million = **$200 million/day**. R at B = $21 × 9.9 million = **$207.9 million/day**, so revenue **rises by $7.9 million**.
  - This is S12 case (1): inelastic demand, so raising the price raises revenue.

### 6.3 Total revenue test cases [S10, S12, S19]
- **Figure 5-4 (S10):**
  - Quantity effect: area A = $0.90 × (1,100 − 900) = **−$180**.
  - Price effect: area C = ($1.10 − $0.90) × 900 = **+$180**.
  - Unchanged part: area B = $0.90 × 900 = $810.
  - Net change = +180 − 180 = **0** (TR $990 → $990), which means unit-elastic.
- **The general rule** ("The overall effect can go either way, depending upon the price elasticity of demand" [S10]):
  - Inelastic (0 < e < 1): price ↑ → TR ↑; price ↓ → TR ↓ [S19, S12 (1)]
  - Unit-elastic (e = 1): price change → TR unchanged [S19, S12 (2)]
  - Elastic (e > 1): price ↑ → TR ↓; price ↓ → TR ↑ [S19, S12 (3)]
- **Sign test with dR/dP = Q(1 − e), using Table 5-1 values** (my arithmetic):
  - Eggs (0.1): Q(1 − 0.1) = **+0.9Q** > 0 → raising the price raises revenue.
  - Gasoline (0.5): Q(1 − 0.5) = **+0.5Q** > 0 → raising the price raises revenue.
  - Housing (1.2): Q(1 − 1.2) = **−0.2Q** < 0 → raising the price lowers revenue.
  - Foreign travel (4.1): Q(1 − 4.1) = **−3.1Q** < 0 → raising the price lowers revenue.

### 6.4 Reading the sign: cross-price and income elasticity [S14, S15, S19, S20]
Keep the sign for these two elasticities. The sign is the answer.
- **Cross-price** (`e_yx` = % change in quantity of Y demanded ÷ % change in price of X):
  - Price of X ↑ and quantity of Y ↑: (+) ÷ (+) = **positive** → **substitutes**. "Quantity demanded of one good rises when the price of another rises."
  - Price of X ↑ and quantity of Y ↓: (−) ÷ (+) = **negative** → **complements**. "Quantity demanded of one good falls when the price of another rises."
- **Income** (% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income):
  - Income ↑ and quantity ↓: **negative** → **inferior good**.
  - Income ↑ and quantity ↑, but by a *smaller* % than income: **between 0 and 1** → **normal good, income-inelastic** ("but not as rapidly as income").
  - Income ↑ and quantity ↑ by a *larger* % than income: **greater than 1** → **normal good, income-elastic** ("and more rapidly than income").

### 6.5 Supply — Figure 5-5 numbers [S17, S18]
- **(a) Cell phone frequencies:**
  - The price goes from $2,000 to $3,000: % change in price = 1,000/2,000 × 100 = **50%** (simple) or 1,000/2,500 × 100 = **40%** (midpoint).
  - The quantity stays at 100: % change in quantity = **0%**.
  - Price elasticity of supply = 0 ÷ 50% (or 0 ÷ 40%) = **0**, so supply is **perfectly inelastic** (vertical line).
- **(b) Pizza at $12:**
  - At exactly $12, producers supply any quantity. Below $12, they supply zero. Above $12, quantity supplied is "infinite" (the caption says "an extremely large quantity").
  - Even a tiny price change causes a huge change in quantity, so the elasticity is **infinite**: **perfectly elastic** supply (horizontal line). The $12 is the cost of producing a pizza (yellow box).

### 6.6 Reading the excise-tax diagrams [S21, S22] (no numbers on the slides)
- Tax on the producer: `S_t` = S shifted **up** by t. Tax on the consumer: `D_t` = D shifted **down** by t.
- Either way: consumer pays **Pc**, producer keeps **Pp**, and **Pc = Pp + t** (government gets t). The quantity falls (red arrow).
- Tax revenue = green rectangle = (Pc − Pp) × new quantity = t × new quantity. The yellow triangle (deadweight loss) lies between the new and the original quantity.

**Q10.**

**Q13.**

- **On the consumer (S22):** demand shifts **down** by t (D to `D_t`).

**Q17.**

**Q18.**

**Q19.** [S15, S20]

**Q20.**
- (a) The % change in Q is 0, so e = 0 ÷ 50% (simple) or 0 ÷ 40% (midpoint) = **0**: **perfectly inelastic supply** (vertical S1) [S17, S18].
- (b) % change in Q = 40/120 × 100 = 33.3% and % change in P = 2/5 × 100 = 40%, so e = 33.3 ÷ 40 ≈ **0.83**. Table 5-3 class: "Greater than 0, less than ∞ — Ordinary upward-sloping supply curve" [(5-4) on S6, S16, S20].

**Q21.**
- The steps, from [S11]:
  1. dR/dP = Q + (dQ/dP)·P.
  2. Divide by Q: (dR/dP)(1/Q) = 1 + (dQ/dP)(P/Q).
  3. Since dQ/dP < 0, (dQ/dP)(P/Q) = −e.
  4. So dR/dP = Q(1 − e).
- Q > 0, so dR/dP > 0 exactly when 1 − e > 0, i.e., **e < 1 (inelastic)**. Then raising the price raises revenue.
- For e = 1 there is no change; for e > 1 revenue falls [S12].

**Q22.** [S7, S19]
- ① Vertical: "No elastic (perfectly no elastic)", i.e., perfectly inelastic demand, value 0.
- ② Steep downward line: "Elastic".
- ③ Flatter downward line: "More elastic".
- ④ Horizontal: "Infinitely elastic (perfectly elastic)", i.e., perfectly elastic demand, value ∞.

**Q23.**
- **The diagram, as on [S21]:**
  - `S_t` lies above S by t. `S_t` ∩ D gives Pc and the new, smaller quantity; at that quantity, S gives Pp.
  - CS = the triangle above Pc under D. Tax revenue = the rectangle between Pp and Pc out to the new quantity. PS = the triangle below Pp above S. DWL = the triangle between the new and the old quantity.
- **The S23 left case** (flat D, steep S): the original price is near the top of the tax rectangle. Most of t comes out of the price producers get, so the tax burden goes **mainly to the producer** [S23].

**Q24.**
- **Not always.** The Laffer curve rises from zero, peaks, then falls back to zero as the tax rate increases. A higher tax rate raises government revenue only on the rising part, left of the peak. Beyond the peak it lowers revenue [S24].
- In `R = t * Y(t)`, national income Y depends on t. For R to fall as t rises, Y(t) must be falling as t rises [S24].
