## 1. Big picture

- **Welfare = consumer surplus + producer surplus** ("Welfare: The benefits that a society can get") [S3, S10]. Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − price paid (area below the demand curve, above the price); producer surplus = price received − cost (area above the supply curve, below the price) [S4–S9].
- Government intervenes in markets in two ways [S2, S11]: **price controls** — a **price ceiling** (maximum price, set *lower* than the market equilibrium price; e.g. rent control) or a **price floor** (minimum price, set *higher* than the market equilibrium price; e.g. minimum wage) — and **quantity controls** (e.g. taxi licensing).
- **Price ceiling** → persistent **shortage** (NYC apartments: ceiling $800 vs equilibrium $1,000 → shortage of 400,000) + 4 inefficiencies (inefficient allocation to consumers, wasted resources, inefficiently low quality, black markets) + deadweight loss [S13–S18].
- **Price floor** → persistent **surplus** (butter: floor $1.20 vs equilibrium $1.00; minimum wage) + 4 inefficiencies (inefficient allocation of sales among sellers, wasted resources, inefficiently high quality, illegal activity) + deadweight loss [S19–S24].
- **Quota / license** (taxi rides limited to 8 instead of 10 million) → a **wedge** between the **demand price** (\$6) and the **supply price** (\$4); the difference is the **quota rent** (\$2 per ride) earned by license holders; plus deadweight loss, missed mutually beneficial transactions and illegal activity [S25–S32].
- **Excise tax** = "A Surprise Parallel": a $2 tax per ride creates the same $6 / $4 wedge and the same 8 million rides as the quota; revenue = $2 × 8 million = $16 million; it makes no difference whether the producer or the consumer officially pays ("The same!") [S33–S36].
- **Tax incidence** = who really pays a tax; it is a question of **elasticity**: if buyers keep buying as much (inelastic demand) the burden goes to buyers, if they buy much less (elastic demand) producers bear it [S34, S37]. Because of deadweight loss, a tax's true cost is more than the tax revenue collected [S2].
- Why do these policies exist despite their problems? "There are people who get benefits from the price ceiling. Sometime, they are well organized and politically strong." [S18]

### [S15] Price Ceilings — why inefficient; (1) inefficient allocation to consumers
- Subtitle: "Why a Price Ceiling Causes Inefficiency".
- Boxed definition: "A market or an economy is **inefficient** if there are missed opportunities: some people could be made better off without making other people worse off by, for example, subletting."
- "**(1) Inefficient Allocation to Consumers**": "Price ceilings often lead to inefficiency in the form of **inefficient allocation to consumers**: people who want the good badly and are willing to pay a high price don't get it, and those who care relatively little about the good and are only willing to pay a low price do get it."

### [S16] Price Ceilings — (2) wasted resources, (3) inefficiently low quality
- "**(2) Wasted Resources**": "Price ceilings typically lead to inefficiency in the form of **wasted resources**: people spend money and expend effort in order to deal with the shortages caused by the price ceiling."
  - ✏️ Professor's line: "For the rent control, people will spend much time and money to rent houses."
- "**(3) Inefficiently Low Quality**": "Price ceilings often lead to inefficiency in that the goods being offered are of **inefficiently low quality**: sellers offer low-quality goods at a low price even though buyers would prefer a higher quality at a higher price."

### [S17] Price Ceilings — (4) black markets
- "**(4) Black Markets can be created**": "A **black market** is a market in which goods or services are bought and sold illegally—either because it is illegal to sell them at all or because the prices charged are legally prohibited by a price ceiling."
  - ✏️ Professor's line: "In the case of rent control, landlords can ask extra money to house seekers illegally in addition to the rent"

### [S29] Controlling Quantities — "Quota Rent"
- Subtitle: The Anatomy of Quantity Controls: "Quota Rent".
- Verbatim: "A quantity control, or quota, drives a **wedge** between the demand price and the supply price of a good; that is, the price paid by buyers ends up being higher than that received by sellers. The difference between the demand and supply price at the quota limit is **the quota rent**, the earnings that accrue to the license-holder from ownership of the right to sell the good. It is equal to the market price of the license when the licenses are traded."

### 5.6 The professor's taxi story — why the rent is exactly $2.00 [S30]
Setup (from the slide): every would-be driver ("I myself, Mr. A, Mr. B, Mr. C, …") is happy with $4.00 per ride and unhappy with less (= **supply price $4.00**). Taxis are scarce, so customers are willing to pay **\$6.00** (= **demand price**). "I" am the only person with the right to run a taxi (the license), cannot drive today, and want to rent the taxi out.

A renter collects $6.00 from the rider and pays the rent to the owner, so he keeps 6.00 − rent:

| Rent offered | Renter keeps | At least $4.00? | What the slide says |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1.00 (Mr. A) | $5.00 | yes | "possible, but Mr. B is going to give me $1.50" |
| $1.50 (Mr. B) | $4.50 | yes | still above $4.00, so another driver can still offer more (drivers outbid each other, as Mr. B outbid Mr. A) |
| **\$2.00** | **\$4.00** | **yes — exactly** | "The rent will be exactly $2.00. I, and the renter both will be happy." |
| $2.50 | $3.50 | **no** | "impossible, no one will borrow my taxi because rider pays only $6.00 … he can only earn $3.50 which is less than $4.00" |

Conclusion: rent = demand price − supply price = 6.00 − 4.00 = **\$2.00** = the **quota rent** [S29], the same $2 per ride as in Figure 4-14 [S28]. It is "the earnings that accrue to the license-holder from ownership of the right to sell the good" [S29] — the owner earns it without driving.

### 5.7 Excise tax — revenue, deadweight loss, true cost [S33–S35, S2]

2. A $2 tax per ride on drivers: S₁ shifts up by $2 to S₂ [S33]. New point A: riders pay **\$6.00**; drivers receive, net of tax, **\$4.00** (point B); rides = **8 million**.
3. Tax revenue (green rectangle [S35]) = $2 × 8 million = **\$16 million** ✓ (printed on [S35]).
4. Deadweight loss: the same triangle A–B–E as the quota [S28] = ½ × 2 × 2 million = **\$2 million** (my arithmetic).
5. *My arithmetic — "its true cost is more than the amount of tax revenue collected" [S2]:* riders lose (6 − 5) × 8 million + ½ × 1 × 2 million = $9 million of CS; drivers lose (5 − 4) × 8 million + ½ × 1 × 2 million = $9 million of PS. Together they lose **\$18 million**, but the government collects only **\$16 million**; the missing **\$2 million** is the deadweight loss.
6. The parallel with the quota: the same $16 million rectangle is quota rent under a quota ([S28]/[S32]) and tax revenue under a tax ([S35]).

### 5.8 Producer pays vs consumer pays — "The same!" [S36, S33–S34]
Starting point (slide): the producer needs $4.00 and the tax is $2.00 ("\$4.00 + $2.00 tax").

| | Producer pays the tax | Consumer pays the tax |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer hands over | $6.00 (all of it to the producer) | $6.00 in total: $4.00 to the producer + $2.00 to the government |
| Government collects | $2.00 (from the producer) | $2.00 (from the consumer) |
| Producer keeps | 6.00 − 2.00 = $4.00 ("real earnings") | $4.00 |
| Graph | supply shifts **up** by $2 (S₁ → S₂) [S33] | demand shifts **down** by $2 (D₁ → D₂) [S34] |
| Points | A = $6 paid by riders, B = $4 kept by drivers, 8 million rides | the same A, B and 8 million rides |

→ "The same!" — "who officially pays a tax is irrelevant when answering the question of who bears the burden of the tax" [S34].

### 5.9 Tax incidence and elasticity — bottled water [S37] (+ taxi numbers [S33]–[S34])
1. The question: who really bears a tax — consumers or producers? ("The incidence of a tax is a measure of who really pays it" [S34].)
2. Bottled-water tax, two official set-ups (slide):
   - (1) the producer pays → less profit → lays off employees to stay alive;
   - (2) the buyer pays → increased price → less demand → [so the producer sells less →] lays off employees to stay alive.
   So even when buyers officially pay, producers can be hurt through lower demand — consistent with "The same!" [S36].
3. "Question is on elasticity of demand and supply." Suppose buyers pay:
   - buyers still buy **as many bottles as before** → demand is **inelastic** → **the tax burden goes to buyers**;
   - buyers buy **much less** than before → demand is **elastic** → **producers bear the tax**.
4. *My arithmetic, taxi example:* before the tax the fare was $5; after the $2 tax riders pay $6, i.e. $1 more, and drivers keep $4, i.e. $1 less → here the $2 burden is split $1 / $1.
5. 🔎 Look up in Track B: how price elasticity of demand and supply is defined and measured, and how the supply side's elasticity changes the split (not covered on the slide).
