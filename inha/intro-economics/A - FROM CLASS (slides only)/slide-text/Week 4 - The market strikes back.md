Price Ceilings

Why a Price Ceiling Causes Inefficiency

A market or an economy is inefficient if there are missed opportunities: some people could be made better off without making other people worse off by, for example, subletting.

Price ceilings often lead to inefficiency in the form of inefficient allocation to consumers: people who want the good badly and are willing to pay a high price don’t get it, and those who care relatively little about the good and are only willing to pay a low price do get it.

(1) Inefficient Allocation to Consumers

Price Ceilings

So Why Are There Price Ceilings?

We have seen three common results of price ceilings:

■ A persistent shortage of the good
■ Inefficiency arising from this persistent shortage in the form of inefficient allocation of the good to consumers, resources wasted in searching for the good, and the inefficiently low quality of the good offered for sale
■ The emergence of illegal, black market activity

There are people who get benefits from the price ceiling. Sometime, they are well organized and politically strong.

## Slide 24: Slide 24

Price Floors

So Why Are There Price Floors?

To sum up, a price floor creates various negative side effects:

■ A persistent surplus of the good
■ Inefficiency arising from the persistent surplus (from low quantity demanded) in the form of inefficient allocation of sales among sellers, wasted resources, and an inefficiently high level of quality offered by suppliers
■ The temptation to engage in illegal activity, particularly bribery and corruption of government officials.
In the case of minimum wage, firms may hire at a wage lower than the minimum wage and give money to government inspectors.

## Slide 29: Slide 29

© 2007 Worth Publishers Essentials of Economics Krugman • Wells • Olney

Controlling Quantities

The Anatomy of Quantity Controls: “Quota Rent”

A quantity control, or quota, drives a wedge between the demand price and the supply price of a good; that is, the price paid by buyers ends up being higher than that received by sellers. The difference between the demand and supply price at the quota limit is the quota rent, the earnings that accrue to the license-holder from ownership of the right to sell the good. It is equal to the market price of the license when the licenses are traded.

## Slide 30: Quota Rent

chapter

© 2007 Worth Publishers Essentials of Economics Krugman • Wells • Olney

Quota Rent

(I myself, Mr. A, Mr. B, Mr. C, …………..) would like to earn money by driving a taxi.

We will be all happy if we can get $4.00 from customers for a ride (We will not be happy if we get less than $4.00 a ride). But customers would like to pay us $6.00 because there are not enough taxis.

Right now I am the only person having the right to run a taxi. But today I cannot drive it. So, I want to let other person drive it – I want to rent out to other driver. How much can I get for the rent?

$1.00 from Mr. A? – possible, but Mr. B is going to give me $1.50.
$2.50 – impossible, no one will borrow my taxi because rider pays only $6.00. If he pays me $2.50, he can only earn $3.50 which is less than $4.00.
The rent will be exactly $2.00. I, and the renter both will be happy.

Tax Incidence

What is tax incidence?
Who bears (consumers or producers) a tax?
Situation: Government made taxes on bottled water
(1)Producer pays for it : less profit, lays off employees to stay alive
(2)Buyer pays for it : increased price, less demand, lays off employees to stay alive
Question is on elasticity of demand and supply
Suppose buyers pays for it.
If buyers still buy as many bottles as before, then demand is
inelastic, tax burden goes to buyers.
If buyers buy much less than before, demand is elastic,
producers burden the tax.
